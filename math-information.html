Module 5 · Information theory

How surprising is a result, how far is a model from the truth, and how much does one variable tell you about another? Three measures answer these questions, and they sit under almost every classification loss, every language model and every representation-learning method.

5.1

Surprise & entropy

The questionA weather forecast says "rain tomorrow" with probability 0.9, and a second says "rain tomorrow" with probability 0.1. When rain comes, which forecast was more informative? And how many yes/no questions, on average, would you need to pin down tomorrow's weather?

Intuition: surprise is the cost of being wrong

A rare event is more surprising than a common one. If you were told "it did not rain", you would shrug; if you were told "it snowed in July here", you would sit up. We want a number that grows as the probability shrinks, and that adds up sensibly when independent events happen together.

The standard choice is the surprise (also called self-information) of an outcome with probability p:

\text{surprise}(p) = -\log_2 p \quad\text{bits}

The minus sign makes it positive, because \log_2 p is negative for any probability below one. The base-2 logarithm measures in bits: one bit is the surprise of a fair coin landing heads, and it is the answer to one yes/no question. (With natural logarithms the same quantity is measured in nats; the ideas do not change.)

Build it up

One outcome. The surprise of a certain event is -\log_2 1=0: nothing to learn. A coin landing heads (p = 0.5) gives -\log_2 0.5=1 bit. A rare outcome with p=1/1024 gives 10 bits, the same as learning the answer to ten yes/no questions. Doubling the rarity adds exactly one bit, which is why the logarithm is the right tool.

Worked example — two forecasts

The rain forecast at probability 0.9 has surprise -\log_2 0.9=0.1520 bits if it rains, and -\log_2 0.1=3.3219 bits if it stays dry. Rain was expected, so it tells you little; a dry day after a 0.9 forecast is a big surprise.

Surprise is a property of one outcome. To judge the whole forecast we need the average surprise, weighted by how often each outcome happens.

Entropy: average surprise. If outcome x has probability p(x), the expected surprise is the entropy:

H(p) = \sum_x p(x)\,\bigl(-\log_2 p(x)\bigr) = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x)
Symbols. p(x): probability of outcome x. \sum_x: sum over all outcomes. H(p): entropy in bits, the expected surprise. By convention 0\log 0=0, because an impossible outcome never occurs and adds no surprise.
Worked example — the loaded coin

A fair coin, p=(0.5,0.5): H=-(0.5\log_2 0.5+0.5\log_2 0.5)=1 bit. Every flip is as uncertain as possible for two outcomes.

A loaded coin, p=(0.9,0.1): H=-(0.9\cdot0.1520+0.1\cdot3.3219)=0.4690 bits. Most flips are heads, so the average surprise is far below one bit.

A fair six-sided die: H=\log_2 6=2.5850 bits. A distribution with four unequal outcomes, (\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18): H=1.75 bits, which equals the average number of yes/no questions an optimal guessing strategy needs. This is the bridge to compression.

What entropy is maximised by. Among all distributions over n outcomes, the uniform one has the largest entropy, \log_2 n. Any bias lowers it. So entropy measures how spread out, and so how uncertain, a distribution is.

Try this
  1. Set p to 0.5 and read H. Then move p towards 0 and 1. Where is the entropy largest, and why does it fall to zero at both ends?
  2. Find the p where the heads surprise and the tails surprise are equal. Is it the same p where entropy peaks? Explain why not.
  3. Press Play and watch H(p) trace its hump. What does the dashed line show at each moment?
Common traps
  • Bits versus nats. The same distribution gives a different number in nats (multiply bits by \ln 2\approx0.693). Always state which unit you use.
  • Entropy is not a property of one outcome. The surprise of the outcome you actually saw is a random variable; the entropy is the expectation over all outcomes.
  • High entropy is not "good" per se. A uniform guess is maximally uncertain, which is not the same as maximally accurate.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Compression and coding: entropy is the lower bound on the average number of bits needed to store outcomes drawn from a source (Shannon's source coding theorem).
  • Uncertainty estimates: a classifier that outputs a flat distribution over 1000 ImageNet classes has entropy near \log_2 1000\approx10 bits; a confident one has low entropy.
  • Decision-making: active learning and exploration pick the input whose predicted distribution has the highest entropy.

Check yourself

1. Compute the surprise, in bits, of an outcome with probability 1/8.
−log₂(1/8) = 3 bits. Three halvings, three yes/no questions.
2. What is the entropy of a distribution over 4 outcomes with probabilities (0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25)?
H = −4 · 0.25 · log₂ 0.25 = 2 bits, which is log₂ 4, the maximum for four outcomes.
3. Why is H of the loaded coin (0.9, 0.1) smaller than 1 bit?
Because the outcomes are unequally likely, so the average surprise is pulled down. The most probable outcome carries little surprise and the rare one is averaged in with a small weight: H = 0.4690 bits.

Takeaways

  • Surprise of an outcome: -\log_2 p bits; rarer means more surprising, and independent surprises add.
  • Entropy H(p) is the expected surprise; it peaks for the uniform distribution and is zero for a certain outcome.
  • Entropy is the minimum average number of bits needed to encode outcomes from the source.
5.2

Cross-entropy & KL divergence

The questionA classifier outputs a probability for each class, and the true label is a single class. How do we score the classifier? And when a model is trained by maximising likelihood, what exactly is it trying to make close to the data?

Intuition: the price of using the wrong code

Suppose you design a code that spends short codewords on outcomes you expect and long codewords on outcomes you do not. If the world actually behaves like p but you built the code for a belief q, every message costs more bits than it needed. The extra cost is the mismatch between the two.

Two quantities describe this. The cross-entropy is the total average cost of using your belief q when the truth is p. The KL divergence is just the extra cost over the unavoidable minimum H(p).

Build it up

Start with the cost of one outcome x. If you believe q(x) but it happens with truth p(x), your code gives it a length -\log_2 q(x) bits, and it occurs on average with weight p(x). Summing gives the cross-entropy:

H(p,q) = -\sum_x p(x)\,\log_2 q(x)

If q=p this is just the entropy H(p), the best possible. The extra cost is the Kullback–Leibler divergence:

\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) = \sum_x p(x)\,\log_2\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = H(p,q)-H(p)
Symbols. p: the true distribution. q: the model's distribution. H(p,q): cross-entropy. \mathrm{KL}(p\|q): KL divergence, read "the KL from p to q". Both are in bits.
Worked example — three classes

The truth is p=(0.7,\,0.2,\,0.1) over (cat, dog, bird). The model says q=(0.5,\,0.3,\,0.2).

Entropy of the truth: H(p)=-(0.7\log_2 0.7+0.2\log_2 0.2+0.1\log_2 0.1)=1.1568 bits.

Cross-entropy: H(p,q)=-(0.7\log_2 0.5+0.2\log_2 0.3+0.1\log_2 0.2)=1.2796 bits.

KL: \mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=1.2796-1.1568=0.1228 bits. The model wastes about an eighth of a bit per symbol.

Reverse KL: \mathrm{KL}(q\|p)=0.1328 bits. It is not the same. KL is asymmetric; the two directions penalise errors differently.

Why KL is never negative. Using Jensen's inequality on the concave logarithm one can show \mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\ge0, with equality exactly when q=p. Nothing can beat the true distribution's own code. This is the reason cross-entropy is minimised at q=p.

Cross-entropy is maximum likelihood. Train a classifier on N labelled examples. Its likelihood of the data is \prod_i q(y_i\mid x_i). Taking -\tfrac1N\log_2 of that gives the average cross-entropy between the empirical label distribution (a one-hot vector per example) and the model. So minimising the standard classification loss is exactly maximising likelihood.

Try this
  1. Set q equal to p (cat 0.7, dog 0.2). What is the KL? Why does the cross-entropy then equal H(p)?
  2. Make the model confident and wrong: q(cat) = 0.05. Watch the cross-entropy grow. Why does a near-zero probability on the true class hurt so much?
  3. Find a model where KL(p‖q) is small but KL(q‖p) is large, or the reverse. What does that say about which direction a model should be penalised in?
Common traps
  • KL is not a distance. It is not symmetric and does not satisfy the triangle inequality. Call it a divergence.
  • The order matters in the formula. Writing \mathrm{KL}(q\|p) when you meant \mathrm{KL}(p\|q) changes which errors are punished most.
  • Zero probability is fatal. If q(x)=0 where p(x)>0, the cross-entropy is infinite. Models use softmax to keep every probability above zero.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Classification loss: softmax plus cross-entropy is the default loss for every neural classifier, from image models to language models (next-token prediction).
  • Distillation: a small student model is trained to match a large teacher's output distribution with a KL term.
  • RLHF and fine-tuning: policy updates are often regularised with a KL penalty that keeps the new model close to a reference model.
  • Variational models: VAEs use KL to pull the learned latent distribution towards a simple prior.

Check yourself

1. A one-hot label says the true class is "dog", and the model gives q(dog) = 0.25. What is the cross-entropy of this example?
The truth is p = (0, 1, 0), so H(p,q) = −log₂ 0.25 = 2 bits. The loss for a single example is just the negative log of the probability given to the correct class.
2. Why is KL(p‖q) ≥ 0, and when is it exactly zero?
By Jensen's inequality on the concave log, KL ≥ 0 for any two distributions, and it is zero exactly when q = p on every outcome with p(x) > 0.
3. Why does minimising cross-entropy over a dataset equal maximising likelihood?
The log-likelihood of independent labels is a sum of log q(y_i | x_i). Each term equals minus the cross-entropy between a one-hot label and the model's distribution. Negating and averaging turns maximising the likelihood into minimising the cross-entropy loss.

Takeaways

  • Cross-entropy H(p,q)=-\sum p\log_2 q is the average cost of coding with the wrong belief q.
  • \mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=H(p,q)-H(p)\ge0: the wasted bits; asymmetric, zero only when q = p.
  • Minimising cross-entropy is maximum likelihood, which is why it is the standard loss for classifiers and language models.
5.3

Mutual information

The questionTwo variables can be unrelated yet have zero correlation, or be strongly related in a way correlation misses. How much does knowing a photo's label tell you about its pixels, or a word's context about the word itself? We need a measure of dependence that captures any relationship.

Intuition: how much uncertainty does one variable remove?

Let X be a coin you are about to flip and Y a friend's guess at the result. If the guess is always right, knowing Y removes all of X's uncertainty. If the guess is random, it removes none. Mutual information measures exactly this: the reduction in uncertainty about one variable from observing the other.

Build it up

Uncertainty about X is its entropy H(X). After seeing Y, the uncertainty that remains is the conditional entropy H(X\mid Y), the average entropy of X within each value of Y. The reduction is the mutual information:

I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X\mid Y) = H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)
Symbols. H(X,Y)=-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2 p(x,y): joint entropy. H(X\mid Y): uncertainty left in X once Y is known. I(X;Y)\ge0, and it is zero exactly when X and Y are independent.

The second form is the one to compute with: sum the three entropies from the joint table.

Worked example — a noisy copy

Let X be a fair bit, so H(X)=1 bit. Let Y copy X with 90% accuracy. The joint table is P(X,Y)=\begin{pmatrix}0.4&0.1\\0.1&0.4\end{pmatrix} (rows X = 0,1; columns Y = 0,1). Its marginals are fair, so H(Y)=1.

Joint entropy: H(X,Y)=-(2\cdot0.4\log_2 0.4+2\cdot0.1\log_2 0.1)=1.7219 bits.

Mutual information: I=1+1-1.7219=0.2781 bits. Knowing the noisy copy removes about a quarter of a bit of the one bit of uncertainty.

Now make the joint table independent: \begin{pmatrix}0.25&0.25\\0.25&0.25\end{pmatrix}. Then H(X,Y)=2 and I=1+1-2=0: no information, exactly as independence predicts.

A stronger copy, \begin{pmatrix}0.45&0.05\\0.05&0.45\end{pmatrix} (95% accuracy), gives I=0.5310 bits. Dependence increases monotonically as the copy gets more accurate.

Why not correlation? Correlation only sees linear relationships. Take X uniform on \{-1,0,1\} and Y=X^2. The correlation is zero, yet Y is determined by X, so I(X;Y)=H(Y)>0. Mutual information catches any dependence, linear or not.

Try this
  1. Set ε = 0: the variables are independent and I = 0. Increase ε slowly. At what point does I pass 0.1 bits?
  2. At the maximum ε = 0.25, Y is a perfect copy of X. What is I, and why does it equal H(X)?
  3. Press Play. Watch how the joint cells and the bars change together. Which bar grows as dependence grows, and which stays fixed?
Common traps
  • Zero correlation is not independence. The Y=X^2 example above is the standard counterexample.
  • MI is not a causal effect. It measures statistical dependence in either direction, with no notion of which one causes the other.
  • Estimating MI from data is hard. With few samples, plug-in estimates of entropy are biased. Continuous variables need density or neural estimators.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Contrastive learning: methods such as InfoNCE train representations so that matching pairs (two views of one image) have high mutual information and mismatched pairs low. InfoNCE gives a lower bound on I.
  • Information bottleneck: a good representation Z keeps I(Z;Y) high for the target Y while keeping I(Z;X) low, discarding nuisance detail.
  • Feature selection: ranking inputs by their MI with the label catches non-linear relationships that correlation misses.

Check yourself

1. Two binary variables have independent fair coins. What is I(X;Y)?
0 bits. For independent variables the joint entropy equals H(X) + H(Y), so I = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y) = 0.
2. If Y is a deterministic function of X, what is I(X;Y)?
I(X;Y) = H(Y), because H(X,Y) = H(X) when Y is determined by X, so I = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X) = H(Y). Knowing X removes all of Y's uncertainty.
3. Why can two variables have zero correlation and yet a positive mutual information?
Correlation measures only linear association. A non-linear dependence such as Y = X² can give zero covariance while Y is completely determined by X, so the mutual information is positive.

Takeaways

  • I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y) is the uncertainty about X removed by knowing Y, in bits.
  • It is zero exactly for independent variables and catches non-linear dependence that correlation misses.
  • Contrastive learning and the information bottleneck use mutual information as their training objective.
