Module 3 · Statistics for data science

You almost never see the whole population: a few hundred customers stand in for millions, one A/B test for every future visitor. Statistics is the discipline of saying how far a sample can be trusted. This module sits in the test and model stage of the data science loop (ask and collect, clean and prepare, explore, test and model, communicate, deploy and monitor), and it reaches back to the first stage, because a sample is only as good as the way it was collected.

3.1

Populations, samples & sampling bias

The questionA shop owner wants to know the average weekly spend per customer to plan stock. The shop's app can message customers and a survey there collects 4 answers within an hour. Cheap, quick, and the average is £73.25. Can she plan on that number? And if she waits a month and collects 4 000 app answers, does the number become trustworthy?

Intuition: taste the soup, but stir it first

To judge a pot of soup you taste one spoonful. That works only if the pot is well stirred. If all the salt sank to the bottom and you skim the top, a thousand spoonfuls from the top will all agree with each other and all be wrong. In data work the pot is the population: every customer, every transaction, every future user you care about. The spoonful is the sample. Stirring is random sampling: giving every member of the population a known, non-zero chance of being picked, by a mechanism that does not depend on what you are measuring.

Two things can go wrong with a sample. It can be noisy: a small random spoonful happens to be unrepresentative, and the next spoonful differs. That error shrinks as the sample grows, and the rest of this module measures it. Or it can be biased: the way you picked members tilts the answer in one direction every time. That error does not shrink as the sample grows. It is the more dangerous one, because a large biased sample looks very precise.

Build it up

Definitions first. The population is the whole group the question is about. The sampling frame is the actual list you can draw from (customers with the app installed, phone numbers that are listed, rows that were logged). The sample is the rows you really measured. Trouble starts when the frame differs from the population (coverage bias), when who ends up in the sample depends on the quantity you measure (selection bias, for example people with strong opinions answer surveys), or when people who were picked do not respond (non-response bias). A convenience sample takes whoever is easiest to reach.

Worked example: twelve customers

A tiny shop has exactly 12 customers, so we can know the truth. Weekly spend in £, and whether the customer uses the app:

customer123456789101112
spend (£)121518222530344155638095
app user?nonononononononoyesyesyesyes

The truth. The population mean is \mu=490/12=40.83 (the eight non-app customers total 197, the four app users total 293).

Convenience sample. The survey reaches only the four app users: \bar x=(55+63+80+95)/4=73.25. That is £32.42 too high. The app users are a different kind of customer, and they are the only ones the survey can see.

Random sample. Draw 4 customers by lot, say customers 2, 5, 8 and 11 (spends 15, 25, 41, 80): \bar x=161/4=40.25, only £0.58 below the truth. A different random draw would give a different number, but the draws scatter around 40.83, while the convenience sample is always 73.25.

A bigger convenience sample. Add as many app answers as you like: the average of the app users converges to 73.25, the average of their group. The error stays at £32.42, however large the sample.

The lab below does this at a larger scale: 2 000 customers, 20% of them app users, and two samples growing in parallel. The slider says how reachable the non-app customers are for the convenience survey. At 0% the survey only ever sees app users.

The formal picture

For a simple random sample of size n from a population of N units, every subset of size n is equally likely. Then the sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean. For a non-random sample the expected value of the sample mean is the mean of the group you could reach:

\mathbb{E}[\bar x_{\text{random}}] = \mu, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\bar x_{\text{convenience}}] = \mu_{\text{reachable}} \neq \mu \quad\text{(bias } = \mu_{\text{reachable}}-\mu)
Symbols. \bar x: the sample mean, the average of the n measured values. \mu: the population mean, the number you want. \mathbb{E}[\cdot]: the average over many repetitions of the sampling procedure. \mu_{\text{reachable}}: the mean of the part of the population the procedure can actually select. Bias is the gap between the two; it does not depend on n.

This is the sampling counterpart of what the mathematics module calls bias and variance: random sampling removes bias, and a larger n removes variance. Only a better design removes bias.

Try this
  1. Leave the reachability slider at 0% and press play. Watch the green (random) and red (convenience) running averages: both wiggle at first, then green settles onto the dashed true mean while red settles onto a higher value.
  2. Let it run past 500 customers. The red line is steadier than green at the end, yet farther from the truth. Precision is not accuracy.
  3. Drag reachability to 30%, then 100%. At 100% the "convenience" sample is just a random one, and the two lines agree.
Common traps
  • "More data fixes it." More rows from the same biased process give a smaller noise and the same bias. The convenience sample above is off by £32.42 at n = 4 and at n = 4 000.
  • Survivorship bias is selection bias in time: analysing only the customers who stayed, the loans that were repaid, or the models that were deployed. The ones that left are missing from the frame.
  • Self-selection. A review site shows the people angry or delighted enough to write. Their average says little about the average customer.
  • Random is not "whatever came first". The first 1 000 rows of a log are tied to one time of day or one day of week. Randomness is a procedure, such as a random number generator, not an impression.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Training-set bias. A model learns the population its training data represents. A face dataset collected from public web photos under-represents some groups, and the model's accuracy on them is lower no matter how many more web photos are added.
  • Survey and label design. Human ratings gathered from a convenient pool of annotators bake the pool's views into the labels. Reward models and preference data inherit this directly.
  • Train/serve skew. If the sampling frame for training (logged traffic from the old system) differs from the population the model will face, offline metrics are biased estimates of live performance.

Check yourself

1. In the twelve-customer table, a random sample happens to contain customers 9, 10, 11, 12. Does that mean random sampling was biased?
No. Random sampling is unbiased as a procedure: averaged over all possible samples the sample mean equals 40.83. That particular draw (probability 1 in 495 for 4 of 12) gives 73.25 by bad luck, which is noise, and it is rare. The convenience sample gives 73.25 every single time.
2. A hospital audits the quality of care by surveying patients who returned for a follow-up visit. Name the bias.
Selection (survivorship) bias in the sampling frame: patients who did badly, moved, or lost trust do not return, so they are absent. The returning patients are probably happier than the typical patient, so satisfaction is overestimated, and surveying more returning patients does not repair it.
3. With a random sample, why does the error shrink as n grows, but with a convenience sample it does not?
Random sampling error is noise around the right centre, μ, and averaging more values reduces noise. A convenience sample is centred on μreachable, the wrong centre; more values only pin down that wrong centre more tightly.

Takeaways

  • A sample estimates a population only if it was selected by a mechanism unrelated to what you measure.
  • Noise shrinks with n; bias does not. A large convenience sample is precisely wrong.
  • Always ask: who could not have ended up in this sample? Name the frame, and name who is missing.
3.2

The sampling distribution & the central limit theorem

The questionYou compute one average from one sample of 36 orders and get £22. If a colleague repeated the study with another 36 orders, they would get a different number. How different? By how much would the answer wobble, and what do you gain by collecting four times as many orders?

Intuition: the wobble of the average

An average computed from data is a random thing, because the data are. Imagine repeating the entire study again and again, each time with a fresh random sample of the same size, and writing down each average. Those averages form their own distribution, called the sampling distribution of the mean. Nobody ever sees it in real life (you get one sample), but it is exactly what tells you how much one average can be trusted. Its centre is the true mean. Its spread, the standard error, says how far a typical sample average lands from the truth.

Averaging smooths out extremes: one huge order in a sample of 36 moves the average by only a thirty-sixth of its size. So averages vary less than single values, and the more values you average, the less they vary.

Build it up

Worked example: every possible sample, counted

A tiny population of six orders (in £10 units): \{1,2,3,4,5,20\}. One order is large, so the population is skewed. Its mean is \mu=35/6=5.833 and its standard deviation (dividing by the population size 6) is \sigma=6.4657.

Take samples of n=2 with replacement. There are 36 equally likely ordered pairs. Their means, with how many pairs give each:

mean11.522.533.544.5510.51111.51212.520
pairs123454321222221

Add them up (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 36). The average of all 36 sample means is exactly 5.833, equal to \mu: the sample mean is unbiased. The standard deviation of the 36 sample means is 4.5720, and the formula \sigma/\sqrt{n}=6.4657/\sqrt{2}=4.5720 matches to all digits. With n=4 the formula gives 6.4657/2=3.2329: doubling the sample size from 2 to 4 shrank the standard error by a factor of \sqrt{2} (4.572 to 3.233).

The orders in the original table: 36 orders with \sigma=12 give \mathrm{SE}=12/\sqrt{36}=2; with 144 orders, 12/\sqrt{144}=1. Four times as much data halves the standard error. That is why halving the uncertainty is expensive.

The surprise is the shape. The population is wildly skewed, and the n = 2 sample means already have a cluster near 1 to 5 and a small group around 10 to 12. With larger n the shape smooths into a bell, whatever the population looks like.

The formal statement

\mathbb{E}[\bar X]=\mu,\qquad \mathrm{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},\qquad \frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\ \xrightarrow{\ n\to\infty\ }\ \mathcal{N}(0,1)
Symbols. \bar X: the sample mean, treated as a random quantity (capital letters are random, small letters are the numbers you observed). \mu,\sigma: the population mean and standard deviation. n: the sample size. \mathrm{SE}: the standard error, the standard deviation of \bar X across repeated samples. The arrow says that, for independent draws from a population with finite variance, the standardised mean approaches the standard normal distribution as n grows (the central limit theorem, proved in Module 3 of the mathematics topic).

How big must n be? The theorem is a limit, not a promise at any given size. For mildly skewed data, n\approx30 is often enough; for heavy skew (the lognormal choice in the lab) it can take hundreds. The standard error formula holds for every n; only the bell shape needs a large one. And \sigma is usually unknown, so in practice you estimate it with the sample standard deviation s.

Try this
  1. Keep "skewed (exponential)" and n = 1. The green histogram of means copies the population. Now set n = 5, then 30: the bars narrow around the dashed centre, and the purple normal curve fits them better.
  2. Compare the readout's "SD of the sample means" with σ/√n. Change n from 25 to 100 and see the standard deviation fall by about half.
  3. Switch to "very skewed (lognormal)" and n = 5. The means are still lopsided; raise n and watch how much larger n has to be before the bell appears. Then try "two humps" and "flat".
Common traps
  • Standard deviation versus standard error. σ describes how spread out single values are; SE describes how spread out averages are. Quoting the SD of the data as the uncertainty of the mean overstates it by \sqrt{n}.
  • "The CLT makes my data normal." It does not. The raw data keep their skewed shape; only the distribution of the average approaches a bell.
  • Dependent data. The formula assumes independent draws. Ten rows from the same user, or consecutive days of one time series, carry less information than ten independent rows, so the true SE is larger than σ/√n.
  • The formula is for the mean. Medians, ratios and model metrics have different standard errors (lesson 3.6 gets them by resampling).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Every confidence interval and test in the next lessons is built from this standard error.
  • Mini-batch gradients. A batch of size B averages B per-example gradients, so its noise falls like 1/\sqrt{B}: four times the batch halves the gradient noise, the same law.
  • Evaluation noise. An accuracy measured on 1 000 test items has a standard error of about \sqrt{p(1-p)/1000}, around 1.4 percentage points at 90% accuracy. Differences smaller than that are not evidence of a better model.

Check yourself

1. Order values have σ = 12. A study uses n = 9. What is the standard error, and what n makes it 1?
SE = 12/√9 = 4. For SE = 1 you need √n = 12, so n = 144: sixteen times as many orders as 9 to shrink the error four-fold.
2. In the six-order population, why is the average of the 36 sample means exactly 5.833, while any single sample mean can be as large as 20?
Unbiasedness is about the long-run average over all samples: each order appears equally often, so the mean of the sample means equals μ = 35/6 = 5.833. A single sample (two copies of the order of 20) can land far away, which is why SE = 4.572 is large for n = 2.
3. A colleague says "my data are skewed, so I cannot use the standard error formula." What is wrong?
σ/√n is the standard deviation of the sample mean for any population with finite variance and independent draws, normal or not. Skew only affects whether the sampling distribution is close to a bell at small n, which matters when converting SE into probabilities.

Takeaways

  • The sampling distribution is what you would see by repeating the whole study; its spread, \sigma/\sqrt{n}, is the standard error.
  • Four times the data halves the error, and the bell shape of the means arrives with larger n whatever the population looks like.
  • Independence is part of the assumption: correlated rows are worth fewer than their count.
3.3

Confidence intervals & what they mean

The questionA clinic promises patients a waiting time of "about 7 minutes". An analyst times 8 random patients and gets an average of 8.475 minutes. What range of true average waits is consistent with this sample, and what exactly can be claimed about that range?

Intuition: a net that is thrown, not a target that moves

The true average wait is one fixed number, even though we do not know it. We build a range around the sample average wide enough that, if we repeated the whole procedure many times, most of the ranges we built would contain the fixed number. Think of throwing a net at a fixed fish: you cannot say after one throw that this net has a 95% chance of containing the fish (it either does or does not), but you can say that your throwing technique catches the fish 95 throws in 100.

From lesson 3.2 we know the sample average lands within about two standard errors of the truth about 95% of the time. Flip that around: the truth lies within about two standard errors of the sample average about 95% of the time. That is the whole idea.

Build it up

Worked example: 8 waiting times (reused in 3.4 and 3.6)
patient12345678
wait (min)7.29.16.48.810.37.98.59.6

Step 1, the mean. The values sum to 67.8, so \bar x=67.8/8=8.475.

Step 2, the sample standard deviation. Squared deviations from 8.475: 1.6256, 0.3906, 4.3056, 0.1056, 3.3306, 0.3306, 0.0006, 1.2656, total 11.355. Divide by n-1=7: variance 1.6221, so s=1.2736.

Step 3, the standard error. \mathrm{SE}=s/\sqrt{8}=1.2736/2.8284=0.4503.

Step 4, the multiplier. We estimated σ by s from only 8 values, which adds extra uncertainty, so the multiplier comes from the t distribution with 7 degrees of freedom, not the normal: t^*_{0.975,7}=2.3646 (compare 1.96).

Step 5, the interval. Margin of error 2.3646\times0.4503=1.0648, so the 95% confidence interval is 8.475\pm1.065, that is [7.41, 9.54] minutes.

Using 1.96 by mistake would give [7.59, 9.36]: too narrow, and in the long run it would cover the truth less than 95% of the time. A 90% interval from the same data is [7.62, 9.33]; a 99% interval is [6.90, 10.05]. Higher confidence costs width. The promised 7 minutes is outside the 95% interval and inside the 99% one.

The formal definition

\bar x \;\pm\; t^{*}_{n-1}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \qquad\text{(or } \bar x \pm z^{*}\,\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\text{ when }\sigma\text{ is known)}
Symbols. \bar x: the sample mean. s: the sample standard deviation (divide by n-1). n: sample size. s/\sqrt{n}: the estimated standard error. t^*_{n-1}: the critical value of the t distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom that leaves (1-\text{level})/2 in each tail (2.3646 for 95% and n = 8). z^*: the same for the normal (1.960 for 95%). The whole term after ± is the margin of error.

What a 95% interval means. The procedure "collect a random sample, compute \bar x\pm t^*s/\sqrt n" produces an interval that contains the true mean in 95% of repetitions, assuming random sampling and a roughly bell-shaped sampling distribution. The 95% belongs to the procedure. The particular interval [7.41, 9.54] either contains the true mean or does not; there is no probability left to attach once the numbers are written down.

The common misreading. "There is a 95% probability that the true mean lies between 7.41 and 9.54" treats μ as random and the interval as fixed, the opposite of the frequentist set-up. The answer to the question people really want (the probability of a value given the data) needs a prior and is the Bayesian credible interval from MAP and Bayes. A defensible plain-language reading is: "values from 7.4 to 9.5 are the ones the data do not rule out at the 5% level."

The width is driven by three things, which the lab lets you move: bigger n makes it narrower (by 1/\sqrt{n}), higher confidence makes it wider, and noisier data (bigger s) make it wider.

Try this
  1. At n = 10 and 95%, let 100 or more studies arrive. Count the red rows (misses): about 5%. Watch the tally in the readout wander and then settle near 95%.
  2. Change the level to 80%, then 99%. Red rows become frequent, then almost vanish, and the bars get shorter, then longer.
  3. Set n = 3: the intervals are huge and sometimes fall off the axis (the t multiplier is 4.30), but the coverage is still about 95%. Then n = 100: narrow bars, same coverage.
Common traps
  • "95% of the data lie in the interval." No: the interval is for the mean. About 95% of individual patients would fall within roughly ±2s of the mean, a much wider range (here about 5.9 to 11.0).
  • Two intervals overlapping means no difference. Not exactly: the proper comparison uses the interval of the difference. Overlap is a rough check only.
  • Ignoring the assumptions. The interval says nothing about bias: a perfectly computed interval from a convenience sample is a precise statement about the wrong population (lesson 3.1).
  • Using 1.96 with tiny n. With n = 8 the right multiplier is 2.36; with n = 3 it is 4.30.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Reporting model metrics. "Accuracy 0.874" means little; "0.874, 95% CI [0.861, 0.887] on 3 000 test items" says how much to trust it. See ML evaluation.
  • Benchmark leaderboards. Two models separated by less than the interval widths are statistically tied. Many published gains are inside the noise.
  • A/B tests report a confidence interval for the lift, which is what drives a launch decision (Module 5).

Check yourself

1. Recompute the interval if the same mean and s came from n = 32 patients. (t* for 31 degrees of freedom is 2.0395.)
SE = 1.2736/√32 = 0.2251; margin = 2.0395 × 0.2251 = 0.459; interval 8.475 ± 0.459 = [8.02, 8.93]. Four times the patients gave about half the margin (1.065 down to 0.459, a little less than half, because t* also shrank).
2. True or false: "the true mean has a 95% chance of being in [7.41, 9.54]."
False in the frequentist reading. The true mean is a fixed number, so it is either in the interval or not. The 95% describes how often this method produces intervals that contain it. (If you want a probability statement about μ you need a Bayesian model with a prior.)
3. The clinic promised 7 minutes. Does the 95% interval support the promise?
7 lies outside [7.41, 9.54], so average waits of 7 minutes are not among the values the data are consistent with at the 5% level: the sample is evidence the promise is not met. With only 8 patients, the plausible range is still wide: 7.4 to 9.5.

Takeaways

  • Interval = estimate ± multiplier × standard error; for a mean from a small sample use t^* with n-1 degrees of freedom: here [7.41, 9.54].
  • "95%" describes the long-run behaviour of the procedure, not the chance that one interval holds the truth.
  • Width falls as 1/\sqrt{n}, rises with confidence and noise, and says nothing about bias.
3.4

Hypothesis tests: null, alternative, test statistic & p-value

The questionThe clinic claims its average wait is 7 minutes. Our 8 patients averaged 8.475. Is that just the normal wobble of a small sample, or is the claim hard to believe? We need a rule that turns "how far from the claim?" into a number that can be compared with a threshold chosen in advance.

Intuition: innocent until the data are surprising

A hypothesis test is a proof by contradiction made probabilistic. Assume the claim is true (the null hypothesis, H_0). Ask: if it were true, how surprising would data like ours be? If very surprising, doubt the claim. If unremarkable, you have not shown the claim is wrong. Like a court, the test never "proves innocence": failing to reject H_0 only says the evidence was not strong enough.

"Surprising" needs a scale. Here the scale is the standard error from 3.2: a sample average more than two or three standard errors from the claimed value is hard to blame on chance.

Build it up

Worked example: the one-sample t-test by hand

Same 8 waits as in 3.3: \bar x=8.475, s=1.2736, \mathrm{SE}=0.4503.

1. Hypotheses. H_0:\mu=7 (the claim). H_1:\mu\neq7 (two-sided: we care about waits that are shorter or longer than promised).

2. Significance level. Choose \alpha=0.05 before looking at the data.

3. Test statistic. How many standard errors is the sample mean from the claim? t=\dfrac{8.475-7}{0.4503}=3.2756, with n-1=7 degrees of freedom.

4. The p-value. If H_0 were true, the t statistic would follow the t distribution with 7 degrees of freedom. The chance of a statistic at least as far from 0 as 3.2756, in either direction, is p=2\,P(T_7\ge3.2756)=0.0136 (one-sided, 0.0068).

5. Decision. 0.0136 < 0.05, so reject H_0: the data are hard to reconcile with a true mean of 7. Equivalently the critical value is 2.3646 and 3.2756 exceeds it, which matches the 95% interval of 3.3, [7.41, 9.54], which excludes 7.

Contrast. Suppose the clinic had claimed 8 minutes. Then t=(8.475-8)/0.4503=1.0549, p=0.3265: not unusual if the true mean were 8, so we do not reject. That is not a demonstration that the mean is 8; 8 is simply inside the interval along with many other values.

The formal definition

t=\frac{\bar x-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}},\qquad p\text{-value}=P\bigl(|T_{n-1}|\ge |t_{\text{obs}}|\;\big|\;H_0\bigr),\qquad \text{reject }H_0\text{ if }p\le\alpha
Symbols. \mu_0: the value claimed by H_0 (7). t: the test statistic, the distance of \bar x from \mu_0 in units of standard error. T_{n-1}: a random variable that follows the t distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom, which is how t behaves if H_0 holds and the data are roughly normal or n is large. t_{\text{obs}}: the value computed from our data. \alpha: the significance level, the false-alarm rate you accept (lesson 3.5).

What the p-value is. The probability, computed assuming H_0 is true, of getting data at least as extreme as ours. It is not the probability that H_0 is true. For us, "if the true mean were 7, only 1.4% of samples of 8 would land this far from 7 or farther." The test statistic compares data against a model; it does not rank the model against alternatives.

The lab reveals the same 8 values one by one. With only 2 or 3 values the t distribution has heavy tails, so even a large gap gives a modest t; as values accumulate the curve tightens and the standard error shrinks. You can edit the list and the claimed value, or simulate 30 values with a chosen true mean.

Try this
  1. Press play with the default data and μ₀ = 7. Watch the t statistic and p-value move as each value arrives; the final values are t = 3.276 and p = 0.0136.
  2. Change μ₀ to 8: p becomes 0.3265, and the red tail area covers most of the curve. Then μ₀ = 8.5: t is almost 0 and p is near 1.
  3. Set the true mean slider to 7 and press Simulate several times (then play). With no real effect, p still sometimes dips below 0.05 on the way. Do you see why stopping at the first dip would be cheating?
Common traps
  • "p = 0.0136 means a 1.4% chance that H0 is true." No. It is P(data this extreme | H0), not P(H0 | data). Getting P(H0 | data) needs a prior (Bayes' rule).
  • "p > 0.05 means no effect." It means the data are not surprising under H0. With n = 8 even a real difference of 0.5 minutes may not show.
  • Statistical versus practical significance. With 100 000 patients a 0.02-minute difference gives a p-value far below 0.001. Report the size of the effect and its interval, not only p.
  • p-hacking and peeking. Trying many hypotheses, subgroups or stopping times until p < 0.05 turns a 5% false-alarm rate into a much larger one (Module 5).
  • Choosing sides after seeing the data. A one-sided p of 0.0068 is justified only if you committed to the direction beforehand.
Where it shows up in AI
  • A/B test analysis: does the new ranking model change click-through, or is the difference noise? The test statistic and p-value are the same recipe with a difference of two proportions.
  • Model comparison: paired t-tests or permutation tests on per-fold scores decide whether model B is really better than A.
  • Feature screening: t-tests and their relatives rank thousands of candidate features, which is where multiple-comparison corrections become essential.

Check yourself

1. A sample of n = 25 has x̄ = 52 and s = 10. Test H0: μ = 50. What are t and the decision at α = 0.05?
SE = 10/√25 = 2, so t = (52 − 50)/2 = 1.00 with 24 degrees of freedom. The two-sided p-value is 0.327, well above 0.05, so we do not reject H0: a 2-unit gap is within ordinary sampling wobble here.
2. Under H0 and α = 0.05, in how many of 1 000 independent studies of a true null will you wrongly reject?
About 50. By construction, when H0 is true the p-value is below 0.05 in about 5% of studies, which is exactly the false-alarm rate α.
3. Our p-value is 0.0136. Which is correct: (a) H0 has a 1.36% chance of being true; (b) if H0 were true, data this extreme would arise about 1.36% of the time?
(b). The p-value conditions on H0 being true. Statement (a) reverses the conditioning and is the classic misreading.

Takeaways

  • State H_0, H_1 and \alpha first; compute t=(\bar x-\mu_0)/\mathrm{SE}; read the p-value from the t distribution: here t = 3.2756, p = 0.0136.
  • The p-value is the probability of data this extreme or more if H0 is true; it is not the probability that H0 is true, and not an effect size.
  • Failing to reject is not confirming; report the effect and its interval alongside p.
3.5

Type I & II errors, power & sample size

The questionA team plans to test whether a redesigned checkout page raises average basket value by £5. Each basket varies with a standard deviation of £10. If they test 25 customers, how likely is the test to detect a real £5 gain? How many customers do they need to be reasonably sure, and how should they decide that before running anything?

Intuition: two ways to be wrong

A smoke alarm can fail in two ways. It can ring with no fire (a false alarm), or stay silent during a real fire (a miss). You can make it more sensitive and have more false alarms, or less sensitive and have more misses. Statistical tests are alarms for effects, with the same trade-off:

H0 is true (no effect)H1 is true (real effect)
reject H0type I error (false alarm), probability \alphacorrect: a detection, probability power 1-\beta
do not rejectcorrecttype II error (a miss), probability \beta

You choose \alpha directly (usually 0.05). \beta is not chosen; it is determined by the true effect, the noise, the sample size and \alpha. That is why planning matters: power is something you buy with sample size.

Build it up

Worked example: power of the checkout test

Suppose the true gain is \delta=5, one basket has \sigma=10 (assume it is known, so a z-test), and n=25, \alpha=0.05 two-sided.

The null world. The sample mean has SE = 10/\sqrt{25}=2. We reject when the sample mean is beyond 1.96\times2=3.92 from 0.

The real world. The sample mean is centred on 5 with the same SE of 2. The distance of its centre from the rejection line, in SE units, is 5/2=2.5. The chance of landing beyond 3.92: P(\bar X>3.92)=P(Z>(3.92-5)/2)=P(Z>-0.54)=0.7054 (the far tail below -3.92 adds almost nothing).

So power = 70.5% and the type II error rate is \beta=29.5\%: roughly 3 real £5 improvements out of 10 would be missed with 25 customers.

Choosing n. A common target is 80% power. Solving gives n=\bigl((1.96+0.8416)\cdot10/5\bigr)^2=31.4, so round up to 32 (check: 31 gives 79.5%, just short; 32 gives 80.7%).

A smaller effect. If the true gain is only £2, power at n = 25 is just 17.0%, and 80% power needs ((1.96+0.8416)\cdot10/2)^2=196.2, so 197 customers. Halving the effect needs four times the data.

The cost of many tests. With \alpha=0.05 and 20 tests of true nulls, the chance of at least one false alarm is 1-0.95^{20}=64.2\% (Module 5 handles this).

The formal definition

\text{power}=1-\beta=P\Bigl(\,|\bar X|>z_{1-\alpha/2}\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ \Big|\ \mu=\delta\Bigr)\approx\Phi\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\delta}{\sigma/\sqrt n}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\Bigr),\qquad n\approx\frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})^{2}\,\sigma^{2}}{\delta^{2}}
Symbols. \alpha: the type I error rate (the false-alarm probability when H0 is true). \beta: the type II error rate (missing a real effect); power is 1-\beta. \delta: the true effect size you want to be able to detect. \sigma: the standard deviation of one observation. z_{1-\alpha/2}: the normal cut-off for a two-sided test (1.960 for \alpha=0.05); z_{1-\beta}: the cut-off for the target power (0.8416 for 80%). \Phi: the standard normal cumulative distribution. The first expression is a close approximation that drops the tail in the wrong direction; the lab uses the exact two-tail expression.

Power rises with the effect size \delta, with the sample size n, and with \alpha (a more lenient test detects more, with more false alarms); it falls with the noise \sigma. The combination \delta/(\sigma/\sqrt n), the effect in SE units, is what matters. With a t-test the numbers are slightly lower because s must be estimated.

Try this
  1. With the defaults (δ = 5, σ = 10, n = 25, α = 0.05) read off the power, 70.5%, and let experiments accumulate. The share of green (detected) experiments settles near that number.
  2. Slide n up to 32: the two curves narrow, the blue curve leaves the rejection region, and power crosses the 80% line on the lower plot.
  3. Set δ = 0. The blue curve sits on the red one and the "power" equals α: every rejection is a false alarm. Then set α to 0.01 with δ = 5, n = 25: false alarms drop to 1% but power falls to 47.0%.
Common traps
  • Choosing n after seeing the data. Sample size is fixed in advance from the smallest effect worth detecting. Collecting until the result "looks significant" invalidates the error rates.
  • Underpowered tests. A study with 17% power finds real effects only one time in six; the effects it does find tend to be overestimates (the winner's curse).
  • "Not significant, so no effect." With low power a non-significant result is nearly uninformative. Report the interval.
  • Power of what effect? Power is computed for an assumed true effect. If you guess a large δ to get a small n, the study is likely underpowered for the realistic one.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Experiment planning: online-experiment platforms ask for the minimum detectable effect and return the number of users and days needed, exactly the formula above applied to proportions (Module 5).
  • Test-set size: to detect a 1-point accuracy gain between two models you need a test set of thousands of items; a few hundred cannot resolve it.
  • Safety and regression tests: a failure rate of 1 in 1 000 needs thousands of trials before the test has any power to see a doubling.

Check yourself

1. In the checkout test (δ = 5, σ = 10, n = 25), what are α, β and power?
α = 5% (chosen), power = 70.5%, so β = 29.5%. About 3 in 10 real £5 improvements would be missed.
2. You want 80% power to detect δ = 2.5 with σ = 10 and α = 0.05. What n?
n = ((1.96 + 0.8416) × 10 / 2.5)² = (11.206)² = 125.6, so 126. Compare 32 for δ = 5: halving the effect quadruples the sample.
3. Why can you not make both α and β as small as you like with a fixed n?
Moving the rejection line outward lowers α (fewer false alarms) but pushes more of the real-effect curve inside the "do not reject" region, raising β. Only increasing n (narrower curves) or reducing noise lowers both at once.

Takeaways

  • \alpha is the false-alarm rate you set; power 1-\beta is the detection rate you must plan for: here 70.5% at n = 25, 80.7% at n = 32.
  • Power grows with effect size and sample size and shrinks with noise; halving the effect needs four times the sample.
  • Choose n before the experiment from the smallest effect that matters, never after peeking at the results.
3.6

The bootstrap: uncertainty without formulas

The questionYou have 8 waiting times and want the uncertainty of their median, or of some ratio or model metric that has no neat standard-error formula. You cannot go back and collect thousands of new samples. Can the one sample you have be used to imitate repeating the study?

Intuition: treat the sample as a small stand-in population

The sampling distribution of 3.2 needs fresh samples from the population, which you do not have. The best available picture of the population is the sample itself. So pretend the 8 values are the population, and draw new samples of size 8 from it. Each new sample is made by picking a value, writing it down and putting it back, 8 times. This is sampling with replacement, so a value can appear twice or three times and another not at all. Compute your statistic on every resample, and the spread of those statistics stands in for the spread of the real sampling distribution. That is the bootstrap: pulling yourself up by your own data.

Build it up

Worked example: one resample, then ten thousand

Original data (positions 1 to 8): 7.2, 9.1, 6.4, 8.8, 10.3, 7.9, 8.5, 9.6; mean 8.475, median (average of the 4th and 5th sorted values 8.5 and 8.8) = 8.65.

One resample. Pick positions at random with replacement: 1, 1, 8, 6, 5, 4, 4, 2. The resample is 7.2, 7.2, 9.6, 7.9, 10.3, 8.8, 8.8, 9.1, with mean 68.9/8=8.6125. Position 1 appears twice, 4 appears twice, and 3 and 7 not at all. The chance a given value is missing from a resample of 8 is (7/8)^8=34.4\%, so about a third of the data are left out each time and about 65.6% of the distinct values (5.25 of 8) appear.

Many resamples. Repeat 10 000 times (seeded generator, seed 1) and keep each mean. The 10 000 means average 8.472, and their standard deviation, the bootstrap standard error, is 0.419, close to the formula's 0.450 (the bootstrap resamples from the sample itself, which uses the divide-by-n variance, and is slightly narrower). Sort the means; the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles are 7.64 and 9.27. That is the percentile bootstrap interval: [7.64, 9.27], against the formula's [7.41, 9.54] from 3.3. For a mean with tiny n the bootstrap interval is a little too narrow.

A statistic without a formula. The sample median is 8.65. Its bootstrap percentile interval is [7.20, 9.60], much wider and jumpier, because the median of 8 values can only take values such as 7.2 or 9.6 that are themselves data points: with this little data the bootstrap gives a crude answer. The same code, with a different statistic, works for medians, correlations, AUC, or a model's accuracy.

The formal definition

x^{*(b)}=\bigl(x_{J_1},\dots,x_{J_n}\bigr),\ J_i\sim\text{Uniform}\{1..n\};\quad \hat\theta^{*(b)}=T\bigl(x^{*(b)}\bigr),\ b=1..B;\qquad \text{CI}_{95}=\bigl[\,q_{0.025},\,q_{0.975}\,\bigr]\text{ of }\{\hat\theta^{*(b)}\}
Symbols. x_1..x_n: the original sample. J_i: a random position chosen uniformly from 1 to n, independently for each slot (with replacement). x^{*(b)}: the b-th resample, also of size n. T: the statistic of interest (mean, median, a model metric). \hat\theta^{*(b)}: its value on resample b. B: the number of resamples (1 000 to 10 000 is typical). q_{0.025},q_{0.975}: the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resampled statistics. \mathrm{SE}_{\text{boot}} is their standard deviation. Resampling is a computer simulation; with n=8 there are only \binom{15}{8}=6435 different resamples (with n=3 only 10).

When the bootstrap fails. It works by assuming the sample looks like the population, so it fails when the sample cannot show the feature that drives the statistic:

  • Extreme statistics such as the maximum: a resample can never exceed the largest value you have, and the observed maximum is picked in 65.6% of resamples (1-(7/8)^8), so the bootstrap puts the whole uncertainty below the observed maximum (here an interval of [9.1, 10.3] with 10.3 as its top end). The true maximum could be far larger.
  • Very small n (a handful of values): the resamples have too few distinct outcomes, and rare events in the population are likely not in the sample at all.
  • Heavy tails (infinite variance, e.g. Cauchy-like data): the mean does not settle as n grows, and the bootstrap distribution of the mean does not converge to the right shape.
  • Dependent data (time series, grouped rows): resampling single rows destroys the dependence; resample whole blocks or whole groups instead.
  • Biased samples: no resampling can repair a sample that missed part of the population (lesson 3.1).

The lab starts with the 8 waits. Resamples are drawn at a slow pace (one value at a time, so you can see circles stack on repeated values); speed up to build the histogram. At 10 000 resamples the readout matches the numbers above. Edit the list, or switch to the maximum to see it fail.

Try this
  1. Press play at the default speed. Watch one value at a time being drawn and its circle stack; after the eighth draw the resample's mean drops into the histogram. Notice values with two circles and values with none.
  2. Raise resamples per second to about 50 and let it pass 10 000. The 95% interval settles near [7.64, 9.27]; compare with the formula interval printed next to it.
  3. Switch the statistic to "maximum". The histogram becomes a spike on the observed maximum, 10.3 (about 66% of resamples hit it), and the interval is [9.1, 10.3]: it can never extend above the largest value you have, which is false precision. Now switch to "median" and see the lumpy shape. Finally replace the data with "1, 2, 100" and look at the mean: only 10 distinct resamples exist.
Common traps
  • Resampling without replacement just returns the original sample every time; the replacement creates the variation.
  • Resampling the wrong unit. If rows are grouped (several rows per customer), resample customers, not rows. Otherwise the interval is too narrow.
  • Treating it as magic. It cannot create information: with 8 values the bootstrap interval is still unreliable, and it cannot fix bias.
  • Too few resamples. A few dozen resamples give noisy percentiles; use at least 1 000 for an interval, more for tails.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Model uncertainty on small datasets: bootstrapping the test set gives an interval for accuracy, F1 or AUC without any formula; see resampling in the mathematics module and ML evaluation.
  • Bagging and random forests train each model on a bootstrap resample and average them: the same trick, used to reduce variance rather than measure it.
  • Comparing two models: resample the test items once, score both models on the same resample, and read the interval of the difference.

Check yourself

1. How many values of the original sample of 8 does an average resample leave out?
Each value is missed with probability (7/8)^8 = 0.3436, so on average 8 × 0.3436 ≈ 2.7 of the 8 values are left out of each resample (and about 5.3 distinct values appear).
2. Why can resampling without replacement not be used for the bootstrap?
Drawing all n values without replacement just reorders the original sample, so every resample has the same mean (8.475) and no spread. Replacement is what produces different resamples and therefore an estimate of variability.
3. A bootstrap interval for the maximum of a sample is squeezed against the observed maximum (here [9.1, 10.3], with 10.3 as its upper end). Why is that wrong?
A resample cannot contain anything larger than the observed maximum, so the bootstrap cannot see the possibility that the population has larger values. The true maximum is typically larger than the sample's, and the real uncertainty is much greater than the bootstrap suggests.

Takeaways

  • Resample the sample with replacement, recompute the statistic, and repeat: the spread of the results estimates its uncertainty (here [7.64, 9.27] for the mean, 10 000 resamples).
  • It works for any statistic, with no standard-error formula, but it assumes the sample looks like the population.
  • It fails for extremes such as the maximum, for tiny n, heavy tails, dependent rows and biased samples.
