8.1

Semi- & self-supervised learning

The questionYou have 1,000,000 photos but a doctor has labelled only 50 of them. Labelling costs money; photos are free. Is there any way the 999,950 unlabelled photos can still make the model better?

Intuition

Unlabelled data has no answers, but it still tells you the shape of the world: which points cluster together, which are near each other, which look like slightly altered copies of the same thing. Three ideas turn that shape into learning signal.

  • Label propagation (smoothness). If two points are close, they probably share a label. Put a few labels on a similarity graph and let them flow along the edges, like dye spreading through a network of pipes.
  • Pseudo-labelling (self-training). Train on the few labels, let the model label the rest, keep only the predictions it is very sure about, add them to the training set and repeat. The model teaches itself — which is dangerous if it is confidently wrong.
  • Self-supervised / contrastive learning. Invent the labels from the data itself: two augmented crops of the same photo should have similar embeddings, crops of different photos should not. No human label is needed, and the learned embedding is useful for later tasks. Language models do the same with "predict the next/masked word".

Build it up: three hand-worked examples

1. Label propagation. Five points on a chain: 1 — 2 — 3 — 4 — 5. Only point 1 is labelled +1 and point 5 is labelled -1. Rule: each unlabelled node takes the average of its neighbours; labelled nodes stay clamped. Start with the unlabelled ones at 0.

2+1(1+0)/2 = 0.5(0.5−0.5)/2 = 0(0−1)/2 = −0.5−1

It has already stopped changing: node 2 is half way to "+", node 4 half way to "−", and node 3 sits exactly on the fence (0). Predicted labels: 2 → +, 3 → undecided, 4 → −. In a longer chain the values would form a straight ramp; in a clustered graph the labels fill each cluster and drop sharply at the thin gap between clusters — that is the "cluster assumption".

Worked example · pseudo-label thresholds

A model trained on the labelled data outputs the probability of its top class for 6 unlabelled points: 0.97, 0.91, 0.84, 0.62, 0.55, 0.99. Suppose (we secretly know) predictions 3 and 4 are wrong, the other four right.

A high threshold gives few but clean new labels; a low one gives many labels but poisons training with wrong ones that the next round then reinforces (confirmation bias). Real systems often use a high τ (0.95) and raise coverage over rounds.

The similarity of two unit vectors is the cosine of their angle difference. Row i compares image i's view A with all three B views; the matching view i is the positive, the other two are negatives. Pipeline viewPreprocessing: build a similarity graph (k-NN, cost O(N²) naively) or define augmentations (crop, flip, colour jitter, mask). Stored: for label propagation, the graph and the score per node (it is transductive — no model for new points); for contrastive learning, an encoder whose embeddings you later reuse with a small classifier trained on the few labels. Contrastive training needs large batches (many negatives) and heavy compute; inference is one encoder pass. Prefer it when labels are scarce and unlabelled data is plentiful; avoid pseudo-labelling when the base model is poor (errors compound).

Common traps
  • Confirmation bias: pseudo-labelling a wrong prediction trains the model to repeat it. Keep τ high, and always evaluate on truly labelled held-out data.
  • A graph that bridges classes destroys label propagation; the quality of the similarity measure is everything.
  • Unlabelled data only helps if it comes from the same distribution as your target data.
  • Contrastive learning with too few negatives, or augmentations that are too weak, collapses to trivial solutions.
Where it shows up in AI
  • SimCLR / MoCo / CLIP use contrastive losses (CLIP pairs an image with its caption). BERT / GPT are self-supervised: the label is a masked or next word taken from the text itself.
  • FixMatch / Noisy Student are pseudo-labelling with a confidence threshold and strong augmentation. Label propagation is used for graph data (fraud rings, social networks).

Check yourself

1. In the chain example, what would f₃ be if point 5 were also +1?
All unlabelled nodes would converge to +1 (the ramp is flat), so f₃ = +1.
2. Why does a lower pseudo-label threshold increase coverage but risk lower accuracy?
Less-confident predictions are accepted; they are more often wrong (0.84 and 0.62 were wrong in our example), and wrong labels are then learned as truth.
3. Row 3 has loss 0.061 and row 2 has 0.375. What does that say about the embeddings?
In row 3 the positive (cos 0.94) is far ahead of both negatives (−1, −0.64), so the softmax is confident. In row 2 a negative (0.342) is closer to the anchor, so the positive gets only 69% of the probability.

Takeaways

  • Unlabelled data supplies structure: neighbourhood, confidence, or invented pretext tasks.
  • Propagation = neighbour averaging with labelled nodes clamped; pseudo-labels trade coverage against accuracy; contrastive loss = softmax over "which view is mine?".
  • Store the encoder (contrastive) or the graph (propagation); evaluate only on real labels.
8.2

Transfer learning & fine-tuning

The questionYou must recognise 10 kinds of crop disease from 500 leaf photos. Training a network from scratch needs millions of images. Yet someone already trained a network on 1.2 million everyday photos. Can you borrow what it already knows?

Intuition

A network learns in layers. The first layers learn generic things — edges, colours, textures — that every image task needs. Later layers learn task-specific things (a dog's snout). A person who can already draw does not relearn "what a line is" to learn to draw leaves. So we reuse the early layers of a pretrained model and only retrain the end. Two recipes:

  • Feature extraction: freeze the whole backbone; train only a new final layer (the "head") on top of its outputs. Cheap, safe with very little data.
  • Fine-tuning: also unfreeze some or all backbone layers and train them gently (small learning rate), so they bend toward the new domain. Better with more data, riskier (can forget — "catastrophic forgetting").

It works to the degree that the new domain resembles the old one. A model pretrained on photos transfers well to leaf photos, less to X-rays, and poorly to audio spectrograms. A mismatch between pretraining and target data distributions is called domain shift.

Build it up: counting what is trained

Take a small CNN pretrained on a big dataset. A convolution with cin input channels, cout filters of size 3×3 has c_{in}\,c_{out}\cdot 9+c_{out} parameters (weights plus biases). We replace the old 1000-class head by a new 10-class one: 128\cdot10+10=1290.

Two practical rules visible in the last column. The new head starts random so it needs a normal learning rate; the pretrained layers are already good, so they get smaller rates, smaller the earlier (more generic) they are — discriminative learning rates. Often you first train only the head for a few epochs (so random head gradients do not wreck the backbone), then unfreeze.

Worked example · data vs method (illustrative)

With 100 target images the head alone (1,290 numbers) can be fitted reliably, whereas 98,730 free numbers would overfit: the data has 100 labels, far fewer than parameters. With 100,000 images, fine-tuning everything can gain several points because the features adapt. Rule of thumb: little data + similar domain → feature extraction; more data or a different domain → fine-tune, with small learning rates. The lab below shows this trade-off with an illustrative curve.

Adapters and LoRA. Huge models (billions of parameters) cannot be fully fine-tuned by most people. Adapters insert tiny trainable modules between frozen layers; LoRA learns a low-rank update W+BA to a frozen weight matrix: for a 4096×4096 matrix (16.8 M numbers) a rank-8 update trains only 2\cdot4096\cdot8=65{,}536 numbers (0.39%).

Pipeline viewPreprocess exactly like the source model (same image size, mean/std normalisation, tokeniser) or its features mean nothing. Stored: one pretrained checkpoint (shared, versioned in the registry) plus your small delta — the head, or adapter/LoRA weights (kilobytes–megabytes). Training cost drops from days to minutes–hours; inference cost equals the backbone's. With frozen backbones you can even cache the backbone's features once and train the head on them in seconds.

Common traps
  • Wrong preprocessing (different normalisation) silently ruins the features.
  • Fine-tuning everything with a large learning rate on a tiny dataset erases the pretrained knowledge. Use small η, and unfreeze gradually.
  • Batch-norm statistics: frozen layers in "train mode" still update running means — freeze them in eval mode, or the "frozen" layer changes.
  • Leakage: the pretraining data may contain your test images. Check licences and overlap.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Almost every vision project starts from an ImageNet or CLIP backbone (CNN lessons); NLP starts from BERT/GPT-style checkpoints; speech from wav2vec.
  • LoRA/QLoRA adapt large language models on a single GPU. Domain adaptation for satellite or medical images is transfer with domain shift.

Check yourself

1. How many parameters train when only conv4 and the head are unfrozen? What share is that?
73,856 + 1,290 = 75,146 of 98,730, i.e. 76.1%. (Note that "just the last block" is still most of this tiny network, because the last block is the widest; in a real ResNet-50 the last stage is also a large share.)
2. Why does the head get a larger learning rate than conv1?
The head is random and must learn from scratch; conv1 already detects generic edges and needs only tiny adjustments, and larger steps would destroy it.
3. A rank-8 LoRA on a 1000×1000 matrix trains what fraction of its 1,000,000 numbers?
2·1000·8 = 16,000 → 1.6%.

Takeaways

  • Reuse generic early layers; train a new head (feature extraction) or also gently fine-tune the backbone.
  • Less data / similar domain → freeze more; more data / larger shift → unfreeze more with small, layer-wise learning rates.
  • You store a shared checkpoint plus a small task-specific delta; preprocessing must match the source model.
8.3

Recommender systems

The questionA shop has 4 users, 4 films and a table of star ratings where most cells are empty. Which film should we show Ann next? Nobody told us what "action" or "romance" mean — can the machine discover such tastes by itself?

Intuition

Collaborative filtering says: people who agreed in the past will agree again. Ann and Bob both loved film 1 and hated film 4, so Bob's opinion on film 2 is a good guess for Ann's. Matrix factorisation makes this precise by giving every user and every item a short list of hidden numbers — latent factors, like (how much action, how much romance). A user's predicted rating for an item is how well their taste vector points the same way as the item's feature vector: a dot product. We never name the factors; training discovers whichever directions best explain the observed ratings. Imagine users and items as points in a shared map: a user is happy with items that lie in their direction.

Build it up: factorising a 4×4 ratings matrix by hand

Rows = users U1–U4, columns = items I1–I4, "·" means unrated (1–5 stars).

I1I2I3I4
U153·1
U24··1
U311·5
U4·154

We have 11 observed ratings out of 16. We look for user vectors p_u\in\mathbb R^2 and item vectors q_i\in\mathbb R^2 such that \hat r_{ui}=p_u\cdot q_i is near the observed rating, using only observed cells in the loss. Start from small arbitrary values:

p₁=(0.5, 0.2), p₂=(0.4, 0.1), p₃=(0.3, 0.6), p₄=(0.1, 0.5); q₁=(0.6, 0.1), q₂=(0.2, 0.3), q₃=(0.1, 0.8), q₄=(0.4, 0.5).

Predictions are tiny (e.g. \hat r_{11}=0.5\cdot0.6+0.2\cdot0.1=0.32 vs. true 5). Over the 11 observed cells the RMSE is \sqrt{\tfrac1{11}\sum(r-\hat r)^2}=\mathbf{3.06} stars — useless, as expected.

Worked example · one SGD update

Take observed cell (U1, I1): r=5, \hat r=0.32, error e=r-\hat r=4.68. Learning rate α=0.1, regularisation λ=0.02. Update both vectors using their old values:

p_1\leftarrow p_1+\alpha(e\,q_1-\lambda p_1): first entry 0.5 + 0.1·(4.68·0.6 − 0.02·0.5) = 0.7798; second 0.2 + 0.1·(4.68·0.1 − 0.02·0.2) = 0.2464.

q_1\leftarrow q_1+\alpha(e\,p_1-\lambda q_1): 0.6 + 0.1·(4.68·0.5 − 0.02·0.6) = 0.8328; 0.1 + 0.1·(4.68·0.2 − 0.02·0.1) = 0.1934.

New prediction: 0.7798·0.8328 + 0.2464·0.1934 = 0.697, error 4.30 (was 4.68). One step moved the prediction toward 5; sweeping over all observed cells for many epochs drives the RMSE down.

Ranking, not just rating

Shops show a list, so quality is judged on the ranking. Suppose we recommend 5 items and the user liked those at positions 1 and 3 (relevance 1,0,1,0,0). Precision@5 = 2/5 = 0.4. NDCG@5 rewards putting hits near the top: \mathrm{DCG}=\sum_k \frac{rel_k}{\log_2(k+1)}=1+\tfrac{1}{\log_2 4}=1.5; the ideal order (1,1,0,0,0) gives 1+0.631=1.631; so NDCG = 1.5/1.631 = 0.920. Always evaluate by a held-out set (and in time order for real systems), not on the training ratings.

Implicit feedback. Mostly you have clicks, views and purchases, not stars. A missing click does not mean "dislike". Treat observed interactions as positives with confidence weights and sample unobserved items as negatives (ALS with confidence, or BPR: "the clicked item should score higher than a random one").

Cold start. A new user has no row; a new item has no column, so no vector can be learned. Fixes: recommend by popularity (item averages here: I3 5.0, I1 3.33, I4 2.75, I2 1.67), ask a few onboarding questions, use content features (genre, text, image embeddings) to predict a vector, and use bandits to explore. In the lab, drop a new user onto the map: their vector starts at the average and moves as ratings arrive.

The formal definition

\min_{P,Q}\ \sum_{(u,i)\in\Omega}\big(r_{ui}-p_u\!\cdot q_i\big)^2+\lambda\big(\lVert p_u\rVert^2+\lVert q_i\rVert^2\big)
Ωthe set of observed (user, item) pairs; unobserved cells are ignored
p_u, q_ik-dimensional latent vectors of user u and item i (the parameters)
p_u\cdot q_ipredicted rating (dot product)
λregularisation strength, prevents overfitting sparse users
e, αerror r-\hat r and learning rate in the SGD step above (the gradient of the squared error is −2e·q; the factor 2 is folded into α)

Related maths: this is a low-rank approximation, see SVD; the optimisation is gradient descent.

Pipeline viewOffline: collect interaction logs, train factors (cost O(|Ω|·k) per epoch), evaluate with a time-based split. Stored: one k-vector per user and per item (users × k floats — for 10 M users and k=64 about 2.5 GB), usually in a vector index / feature store. Online: retrieval (nearest items to the user's vector, via approximate nearest neighbour search) then a re-ranking model, then business rules. Re-train regularly: tastes and catalogues move. Prefer factorisation for dense interaction logs; prefer content-based or hybrid models when items are new or interactions are sparse.

Common traps
  • Treating missing ratings as zeros (they are unknown, not "dislike").
  • Random train/test split of interactions leaks the future; use time-based splits per user.
  • Optimising RMSE while the product shows a ranked list; check ranking metrics too.
  • Feedback loops: you only observe what you recommended, so popular items get more popular.
Where it shows up in AI
  • The Netflix-prize winners were ensembles of matrix factorisation. Modern two-tower models learn user and item embeddings with neural networks and train them with contrastive losses — the same dot-product idea as here.
  • Item embeddings (word2vec-style "item2vec") and learning-to-rank (NDCG) power e-commerce, music and news feeds.

Check yourself

1. Why is the loss summed only over observed cells?
Missing cells have no ground truth; counting them as 0 would teach the model that unseen items are disliked.
2. If the error e is negative (we over-predicted), which way does p_u move?
Away from q_i, since the update adds α·e·q_i with e<0 (ignoring regularisation), reducing the dot product.
3. A list has hits at positions 2 and 3 only (0,1,1,0,0). Compute NDCG@5.
DCG = 1/log₂3 + 1/log₂4 = 0.631 + 0.5 = 1.131; ideal = 1.631; NDCG = 0.693.

Takeaways

  • Predicted rating = dot product of learned user and item vectors; fit by SGD on observed ratings only.
  • Judge by RMSE and, more importantly, ranking metrics on time-split held-out data.
  • Cold start needs popularity, content features or exploration; you store vectors and serve via nearest-neighbour search.
8.4

Time-series forecasting

The questionA shop has three years of quarterly sales. How many units should it stock next year — and how sure can it be? Why would shuffling the rows and doing a normal 80/20 split give a dangerously flattering test score?

Intuition

A time series is data where order is the information. Almost every series is a blend of three things: a slow trend (growth), a repeating seasonality (Christmas peaks, weekly cycles) and noise you cannot predict. Forecasting means separating the predictable parts, extending them, and honestly widening your uncertainty as you look further ahead — a weather forecast for tomorrow is sharper than for next month.

Build it up: decomposition by hand

yearQ1Q2Q3Q4year mean
1101481211
21216101413
31418121615

Trend: the year means 11, 13, 15 grow by 2 per year. Seasonal effect: subtract each year's mean and average per quarter: Q1 = (−1−1−1)/3 = −1; Q2 = +3; Q3 = −3; Q4 = +1 (they sum to 0, as they should). Residual = value − trend − season = 0 here because the toy data is noise-free; real data leaves a noisy remainder (the lab adds noise). Additive model: y_t=T_t+S_t+\varepsilon_t; when seasonal swings grow with the level, use multiplicative (T_t\times S_t, or take logs).

Forecast year 4: trend continues to mean 17, so Q1..Q4 = 17−1, 17+3, 17−3, 17+1 = 16, 20, 14, 18. Compare two naive baselines you must always beat: naive (repeat the last value: 16,16,16,16) and seasonal naive (repeat last year: 14,18,12,16, which ignores growth and is off by 2 everywhere).

Turning a series into a supervised table: lag features

To use any ML model (a gradient-boosted tree, say) we build features from the past — lags — and only from the past:

ttarget ytlag1 yt−1lag4 yt−4quarter
5121210Q1

The first four rows of the series have no lag-4, so they are dropped. Add rolling means (mean of last 4), calendar features (quarter, holiday) and known future inputs (planned promotions). One-step forecasting predicts t+1 using real observed lags. Multi-step (horizon h) either (a) feeds forecasts back as lags (recursive: errors accumulate) or (b) trains one model per horizon (direct).

Worked example · time-aware validation

Rows 1–12. A random 75/25 split might put quarter 9 in the test set and quarter 10 in the training set: the model sees the future. Correct: train on t = 1…8, validate on 9…12. Or a rolling origin: train 1–8 → test 9; train 1–9 → test 10; train 1–10 → test 11; … then average the errors. In the lab the split is a movable vertical line; training data must always be left of it, and a gap is added if features look further back than the horizon.

Stationarity and ARIMA intuition

A series is (weakly) stationary if its mean, variance and autocorrelation do not drift. Trend and seasonality break this; the cure is differencing: y_t-y_{t-1} turns a straight-line trend into a constant (11,13,15 → 2,2), and a seasonal difference y_t-y_{t-4} removes the yearly cycle. ARIMA(p,d,q) = Auto-Regression on the last p values + d differencings + Moving-average of the last q forecast errors. The simplest, AR(1) = ARIMA(1,0,0), says: tomorrow's gap from the mean is a fraction φ of today's gap, plus fresh noise.

Worked example · ARIMA(1,0,0) one step

Mean μ = 2, φ = 0.6, noise σ² = 0.25, last observation yT = 3.0.

1 step: \hat y_{T+1}=\mu+\varphi(y_T-\mu)=2+0.6\cdot1=2.6, sd = σ = 0.5.
2 steps: 2 + 0.6²·1 = 2.36, variance σ²(1+φ²) = 0.25·1.36 = 0.34, sd 0.58.
3 steps: 2 + 0.216 = 2.216, variance σ²(1+φ²+φ⁴) = 0.372, sd 0.61.

The forecast decays toward the mean, and the uncertainty widens toward the series' long-run spread σ/√(1−φ²) = 0.625. For a trending (non-stationary) series the fan widens without limit.

The formal definition

y_t=T_t+S_t+\varepsilon_t,\qquad y_t-\mu=\varphi\,(y_{t-1}-\mu)+\varepsilon_t,\ \ \varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)
\operatorname{Var}\big(y_{T+h}\mid y_T\big)=\sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}\varphi^{2j}
T_t,S_t,\varepsilon_ttrend, seasonal and noise components at time t
μ, φlong-run mean and autoregressive coefficient (|φ| < 1 for stationarity)
hforecast horizon (steps ahead)
σ²variance of the one-step noise; sets how wide the fan starts
Pipeline viewPreprocessing: regular timestamps, missing-value handling, outlier/holiday flags, differencing or log transform; build lag/rolling features only from data available at prediction time. Stored: model parameters plus the recent history window (the last max-lag values) needed at serving — the serving system must keep it fresh. Training is cheap (ARIMA/ETS: seconds; GBM on lags: minutes); one model per series or a global model across thousands of series. Monitor forecast error by horizon and retrain on a schedule. Prefer simple statistical models for few, short series; global ML/deep models for many related series with covariates.

Common traps
  • Random-split leakage and features that peek forward (a rolling mean centred on t uses the future).
  • Not beating the (seasonal) naive baseline; an unimpressive model can look good on MAPE alone.
  • Reporting only a point forecast: decisions (stock levels) need intervals.
  • Recursive multi-step forecasts silently compounding their own errors; structural breaks (a pandemic) invalidate history.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Demand and energy-load forecasting (gradient boosting on lag features, Prophet, ETS/ARIMA), sensor anomaly detection, finance.
  • Deep models: LSTM/GRU, temporal convolutions, DeepAR, N-BEATS, Temporal Fusion Transformers, and pretrained time-series foundation models.

Check yourself

1. In the table, forecast quarter 1 of year 4 by seasonal-naive and by trend+season. Which is better if growth continues?
Seasonal-naive 14; trend+season 17−1 = 16. Trend+season, since the series grows by 2 per year.
2. For AR(1) with μ=2, φ=0.6, y_T=3, what is the 4-step forecast?
2 + 0.6⁴·1 = 2.1296.
3. Why are lag features for t built only from yt−1, yt−2…, and why must the validation set come after the training set?
At prediction time only the past exists; using the future (or training on later data than you test on) leaks information that will not be there in production and inflates the score.

Takeaways

  • Series = trend + seasonality + noise; forecast the first two and quantify the third.
  • Lag features + time-ordered validation make any ML model a forecaster; always compare to naive baselines.
  • Uncertainty must widen with the horizon; serving needs the recent history window.
8.5

Bayesian machine learning

The questionYou have only three measurements. A normal fit gives one slope, 1.04 — but is the real slope 1.0, or 0.5, or 1.5? A model that says "the answer is 1.04" with no sense of doubt is dangerous when the data is small or the stakes are high. How can a model say how unsure it is?

Intuition

Ordinary training picks one best parameter. The Bayesian view keeps all plausible parameters at once, each weighted by how believable it is. You start with a prior: what you believed before seeing data ("slopes are probably near 0, within ±1"). Each data point is a likelihood: how well each candidate slope explains it. Multiply them and renormalise and you get the posterior: your updated belief, narrower than before. More data → narrower posterior. The model's prediction is then an average over all plausible parameters, and the spread of that average is the model's honest uncertainty — small near the data, large far away from it.

(Background: MAP and Bayes in the statistics module.)

Build it up: Bayesian linear regression with one parameter

Model: y=wx+\varepsilon, noise variance σ² = 1, prior w\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2=1). Data: (x,y) = (1, 1.2), (2, 1.9), (3, 3.2). The useful trick: work with precision (1/variance) — precisions simply add.

Prior precision = 1/τ² = 1, prior mean 0. Each point adds precision x^2/\sigma^2 and shifts the mean:

afterprecisionvariance 1/precisionmean
prior110
point 1: x=1,y=1.21 + 1 = 20.51.2/2 = 0.600
point 2: x=2,y=1.92 + 4 = 60.1667(1.2+3.8)/6 = 0.833
point 3: x=3,y=3.26 + 9 = 150.0667(1.2+3.8+9.6)/15 = 0.973

The mean = (Σ x·y/σ²)/precision = 14.6/15. The posterior is w\mid\text{data}\sim\mathcal N(0.973,\,0.0667): sd 0.258. Compare plain least squares: 14.6/14 = 1.043, and the posterior mean is 0.973: shrunk toward the prior mean 0 — which is exactly ridge regression with λ = σ²/τ² = 1 (14.6/(14+1)). The Bayesian answer adds what ridge lacks: the variance.

Worked example · predictive uncertainty

A new input x* has prediction mean x*·0.973 and variance x_*^2\cdot0.0667+\sigma^2 (uncertainty in the slope, magnified by x*, plus irreducible noise).

The band widens as we extrapolate: the model "knows what it does not know". With a bias term as well the band is narrowest near the data's centre and flares on both sides.

Gaussian processes (intuition). Instead of a handful of parameters, put a prior directly on functions: "nearby inputs have similar outputs" (a kernel decides how similar; see kernels). Conditioning on data pins the function down near observations and leaves it free elsewhere: the band is thin at the data and wide in gaps. Cost: O(N³) to train, so GPs suit small data (hundreds to few thousand points) — exactly where uncertainty matters. Bayesian optimisation, which tunes hyper-parameters using the GP's uncertainty, is a major use. For graphical-model aspects see Bayes nets.

p(w\mid\mathcal D)=\frac{p(\mathcal D\mid w)\,p(w)}{p(\mathcal D)}\ \propto\ \text{likelihood}\times\text{prior}
s_N^{-1}=\tau^{-2}+\sigma^{-2}\sum_i x_i^2,\qquad m_N=s_N\,\sigma^{-2}\sum_i x_iy_i,\qquad p(y_*\mid x_*)=\mathcal N\!\big(m_Nx_*,\ x_*^2s_N+\sigma^2\big)
\mathcal Dthe observed data
p(w), \tau^2prior belief about the parameter and its variance (how strongly you believe it is near 0)

Pipeline viewPreprocessing: scale features (priors are scale-sensitive). Stored: the distribution of parameters (mean vector + covariance matrix — O(d²) memory) instead of one vector; for GPs, the whole training set and a factorised kernel matrix (O(N²) memory, O(N³) training, O(N) per prediction mean). Online learning is natural: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior — one new point updates the stored precision and mean (as in the table). Serve mean and an interval; route high-uncertainty cases to a human or a safer default. For deep networks, approximations (ensembles, MC-dropout, Laplace) are used.

Common traps
  • A prior is an assumption: a badly wrong, too-narrow prior is hard for data to overcome. State and test it.
  • Confusing a credible interval (probability about w, given the data) with a frequentist confidence interval.
  • Predictive uncertainty has two parts — uncertainty about parameters (shrinks with data) and noise (does not).
  • Exact Bayesian inference rarely exists outside simple models; approximations can be overconfident.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Gaussian-process regression and Bayesian optimisation (hyper-parameter tuning), Thompson sampling in bandits and recommenders, naive Bayes, Kalman filters.
  • Weight decay = a Gaussian prior; deep ensembles and MC-dropout give approximate uncertainty for medical and autonomous-driving models.

Check yourself

1. With prior τ² = 1 and σ² = 1, what is the posterior precision after observing one point at x = 2?
1 + 2²/1 = 5 (variance 0.2).
2. Why does the predictive sd grow with |x*|?
The slope uncertainty s_N is multiplied by x*², so errors in w matter more far from the origin; the noise term σ² stays constant.
3. How does the posterior mean 0.973 relate to ridge regression?
It is the ridge (MAP) solution with λ = σ²/τ² = 1: Σxy/(Σx²+λ) = 14.6/15.

Takeaways

  • Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior; with Gaussians, precisions add and the posterior narrows with every point.
  • Predictive uncertainty = parameter uncertainty (grows away from data) + noise.
  • You store distributions, not points; GPs give uncertainty on small data at cubic cost.
8.6

Causal thinking & experiments

The questionCustomers who got the new "recommended for you" widget buy 20% more. Did the widget cause the extra sales — or did it just go to the customers who were already buying more? A prediction model cannot answer this; it only sees patterns.

Intuition

Machine learning predicts what usually goes with what. A decision needs what happens if I change something. The gap between them is confounding: a hidden factor that influences both the "treatment" and the outcome. Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together; the confounder is summer. The gold standard cure is randomisation: flip a coin to decide who gets the treatment. A coin cannot know about summer or customer loyalty, so on average the two groups are alike in everything except the treatment, and any difference is caused by it. That is an A/B test.

Build it up

Worked example · confounding and Simpson's paradox (kidney-stone study, Charig et al. 1986)

Two treatments, A (open surgery) and B (a less invasive one), success counts:

small stoneslarge stonesoverall
A81/87 = 93.1%192/263 = 73.0%273/350 = 78.0%
B234/270 = 86.7%55/80 = 68.8%289/350 = 82.6%

A is better for small stones and better for large stones, yet B looks better overall! Reason: stone size (severity) is a confounder. Doctors gave the hard large-stone cases mostly to A (263 of A's 350 patients) and the easy ones mostly to B (270 of 350), so A's overall rate is dragged down by the difficulty of its patients, not by the treatment. Neither group-size mix nor the overall average is "wrong" arithmetic; which view to trust depends on the causal story: here size comes before treatment, so compare within sizes. Randomisation would balance sizes and remove the problem.

Sample size for an A/B test. Current conversion 10%; we care about detecting an absolute lift of 2 points (to 12%), with false-positive rate α = 5% (two-sided, z = 1.96) and power 80% (z = 0.84):

n\approx\frac{2\,(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})^2\,\bar p(1-\bar p)}{\delta^2}=\frac{2\,(1.96+0.84)^2\cdot0.11\cdot0.89}{0.02^2}\approx 3{,}840\text{ per group}

(with the unrounded z-values 3,842; \bar p=0.11 is the average of 10% and 12%.) Halving δ quadruples n — small effects are expensive to detect.

Worked example · the z-test

Run it with n = 4,000 per group: control converts 400 (10.0%), treatment 468 (11.7%). Observed lift 0.017. Pooled rate = 868/8000 = 0.1085. Standard error under "no effect": \sqrt{0.1085\cdot0.8915\cdot(2/4000)}=0.00695. Then z=0.017/0.00695=2.44, two-sided p ≈ 0.015 < 0.05: statistically significant. A 95% confidence interval for the lift: 0.017 ± 1.96·0.00695 = [0.34, 3.06] percentage points — consistent with a lift anywhere from tiny to large. Report the interval, not only "significant".

The peeking problem. The 5% false-positive guarantee holds only if you look once, at the planned n. If you check daily and stop as soon as p < 0.05, a no-effect test will "win" far more than 5% of the time (the p-value random-walks across the line). Fix the sample size in advance, or use sequential methods built for continuous monitoring.

Uplift (heterogeneous effects). The average effect hides who is affected. Imagine four customer types, with the treatment (discount email) vs control conversion rates (illustrative numbers):

typetreatedcontrolupliftdecision
persuadables12%4%+8 ptstarget
sure things30%30%0skip (wasted discount)

sleeping dogs6%9%−3 ptsavoid (annoyed)

An ordinary model ranking by "probability to buy" would pick the sure things. An uplift model predicts the difference in outcome with vs without treatment, e.g. by training on randomised data with the two-model approach (P(buy | treated) minus P(buy | control)), or a single model with treatment interactions, and targets the persuadables. It needs randomised (or carefully de-confounded) data.

The formal definition

\mathrm{ATE}=\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)],\qquad \widehat{\mathrm{ATE}}=\bar y_T-\bar y_C,\qquad z=\frac{\hat p_T-\hat p_C}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)\,(1/n_T+1/n_C)}},\qquad \tau(x)=\mathbb E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid x]
Y(1), Y(0)outcomes the same person would have with and without treatment — only one is ever observed (the "fundamental problem of causal inference")
ATEaverage treatment effect; randomisation makes \bar y_T-\bar y_C an unbiased estimate
\hat ppooled conversion rate used to compute the standard error under "no effect"
zhow many standard errors the observed difference lies from zero; |z| > 1.96 ↔ p < 0.05
τ(x)uplift: treatment effect for people with features x
Pipeline viewAn experiment needs a randomisation unit (user id hashed to bucket, stable across visits), assignment logging (who saw what, when), an exposure event, the outcome metrics, and guard-rail metrics (latency, errors). Stored: the assignment table and raw event logs for the analysis window; results go in an experiment registry. Serving: the application reads the bucket and returns variant A or B; the model service may run as challenger vs champion (see Module 9: canary, shadow, A/B rollout). Check the sample ratio (a 50/50 split that arrives 52/48 signals a bug) before reading results.

Common traps
  • Reading a correlation in observational data as an effect; always ask "what else could drive both?"
  • Peeking, running many metrics/segments until one is significant (multiple comparisons), and stopping early on a lucky streak.
  • Interference: users influence each other (marketplaces, social feeds), breaking the "independent groups" assumption.
  • Statistical significance is not business significance; look at the interval's size and cost.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Every model launch is judged by an online A/B test, not by offline accuracy. Recommenders and ranking changes are measured on real conversion.
  • Uplift modelling in marketing and medicine, off-policy evaluation, causal forests/double ML, and contextual bandits that trade exploration against exploitation.

Check yourself

1. With baseline 10% and target 12%, what happens to the required n if you want to detect a 1-point lift instead?
About four times larger: the formula has δ² in the denominator (≈ 14,700 per group with p̄ = 0.105).
2. In the kidney-stone table, why can B have the higher overall success rate while A wins in both strata?
A treated mostly the harder large stones (263/350), B mostly the easy small ones (270/350). Overall rates are weighted averages with different weights.
3. Why does randomising remove confounding?
The coin is independent of every other variable, observed or hidden, so the groups are alike in expectation on everything except the treatment.

Takeaways

  • Prediction is not causation: confounders create misleading patterns (Simpson's paradox).
  • Randomise, fix the sample size in advance (n ≈ 2(z+z)²p̄(1−p̄)/δ²), test once and report the interval.
  • Uplift models target who is changed by the treatment; store assignments and exposure logs.
