2.1

Rate of change & limits

The questionA car's odometer tells you where the car is (how far it has driven), not how fast it is going. You glance at the odometer at 3:00:00 and again at 3:00:01 and subtract: that gives an average speed over one second. But the speedometer shows the speed right now, at one instant, and in a single instant the car travels zero distance in zero time. How can "distance ÷ time" even make sense — and why does a machine-learning model care about the speed at which its error changes as a knob is turned?

Intuition

Start with what you can compute: an average rate of change. If the odometer shows 9 m at second 3 and 16 m at second 4, the car covered 7 m in 1 s, an average of 7 m/s. Nothing mysterious: change in position ÷ change in time.

The trouble is that the average hides what happened inside that second. The car may have been accelerating, so it was slower at the start and faster at the end. To learn the speed at second 3 we shrink the window: look at second 3 to 3.1 instead, then 3 to 3.01, then 3 to 3.001. Each window gives an average speed, and — this is the key observation — those averages settle toward a single number. We define the instantaneous speed to be the number they settle toward.

There is a picture for this. Draw position against time. The average rate over a window is the slope of the straight line joining the two points of the curve at the ends of the window (a secant line, from the Latin "to cut"). As the window shrinks, the second point slides along the curve toward the first, and the secant pivots until it just touches the curve at one place — the tangent. The slope of the tangent is the instantaneous rate of change.

A second picture, even more powerful: zoom in. Look at any smooth curve through a microscope. Where it looked bent, it now looks straighter; zoom further and it is indistinguishable from a straight line. That line's slope is the derivative. Calculus is the study of curves by pretending that, up close, they are straight. The lab below lets you do exactly that.

Build it up

Let a car's distance from the start (in metres) after t seconds be s(t)=t^2. What is its speed at t=3? We compute the average speed over a window that starts at 3 and lasts h seconds, for smaller and smaller h.

Worked example: speed at t = 3

The distance at t=3 is s(3)=9. For each h, average speed = \dfrac{s(3+h)-s(3)}{h}.

The averages 7, 6.1, 6.01, 6.001 march toward 6. We can see why with algebra: (3+h)^2-9 = 6h+h^2, so the average speed is \frac{6h+h^2}{h}=6+h. As h becomes tiny, 6+h becomes 6. The speed at t=3 is 6 m/s.

Notice two things. First, we never set h to 0 — that would give 0/0, which means nothing. Instead we asked what the expression approaches. Second, the algebra simplified the fraction before letting h shrink. That is the standard method: simplify until the "bad" division by h disappears.

The limit idea

A limit is the number an expression gets closer and closer to as its input gets closer and closer to some value — without necessarily ever reaching it. We write \lim_{h\to0}(6+h)=6, read "the limit of 6 + h as h approaches 0 is 6". The expression need not even be defined at the target: (6h+h^2)/h is undefined at h=0, yet its limit is perfectly fine. Think of walking toward a door: the limit is where the door is, even if you only ever get within a millimetre.

We require the approach to work from both sides (positive and negative h), and that is what makes some points special, as we will see shortly.

The formal definition

f'(a)\;=\;\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}
f(a)the value of the function (the position, the loss…) at the input a
ha small step away from a; may be positive or negative
f(a+h) − f(a)the change in output caused by the step
(f(a+h) − f(a)) / hthe difference quotient: average rate of change = slope of the secant
limh→0"what does it approach as h shrinks to (but is never exactly) zero"
f′(a)the derivative of f at a: the slope of the tangent. Read "f prime of a". Also written \frac{df}{dx}(a) (Leibniz notation: "a tiny change in f per tiny change in x").

Units. The derivative carries units "output per input": metres per second here; "units of loss per unit of weight" in a model. Sign. f'(a)>0: increasing the input increases the output. f'(a)<0: it decreases it. f'(a)=0: flat to first order (a peak, a valley or a pause). A big |f′| means the output is very sensitive to the input.

Worked example: slope of a loss

A one-weight model has loss L(w)=(w-2)^2 (best weight is 2). At w=5 the loss is 9. The difference quotient is \frac{(5+h-2)^2-9}{h}=\frac{6h+h^2}{h}=6+h\to6, so L'(5)=6: raising w by a tiny amount raises the loss six times as fast. To lower the loss we move the other way. With a step size 0.1 we update w\leftarrow5-0.1\cdot6=4.4, and the loss becomes (4.4-2)^2=5.76, down from 9. That single idea — read the slope, step against it — is gradient descent.

When there is no derivative

Zooming in only works if the curve does straighten out. A function is differentiable at a point if the difference quotient has a limit there. It fails at a corner: for f(x)=|x| at 0, secants from the right have slope +1 and from the left −1, so there is no single answer; zoom in as much as you like and the corner stays a corner. It also fails at jumps (no continuous curve at all) and at vertical tangents (the slope is infinite). The ReLU activation \max(0,x) has exactly such a corner at 0, which we will meet again.

Common traps
  • "h → 0" does not mean "h = 0". Setting h to zero gives 0/0. The limit asks what the fraction approaches.
  • The derivative is a new function, not a number you read off the curve's height. A curve can be high with a slope of 0 (top of a hill) or at zero height with a steep slope.
  • Do not take h absurdly small on a computer. With h = 10−16, f(a+h) and f(a) are equal in floating point and you get 0/h = 0. The best h for a numerical derivative is around 10−5 (with central differences). This is why we compute derivatives analytically (next lessons) and use numerics only to double-check.
  • A function can be continuous without being differentiable (|x| has no gaps, yet a corner at 0).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Training = following a slope. The loss L(w) measures how wrong the model is; L'(w) says how the loss changes as weight w changes. Gradient descent repeats w\leftarrow w-\eta L'(w).
  • Gradient checking. To test that a hand-written backward pass is correct, engineers compare it with the numerical difference quotient (L(w+h)-L(w-h))/2h — exactly this lesson.
  • ReLU's corner. Networks built on \max(0,x) are not differentiable at 0. Frameworks simply pick a value (0) at that single point, and training works fine because inputs essentially never land there exactly.

Check yourself

1. For f(x)=x^2, what is the average rate of change between x = 2 and x = 2.5? What does the derivative at 2 turn out to be?
Average: (6.25-4)/0.5=4.5. The derivative: \frac{(2+h)^2-4}{h}=4+h\to4, so f'(2)=4. (The window of width 0.5 gave 4 + 0.5 = 4.5, as the formula 4 + h predicts.)
2. You approximate the slope of f(x)=x^3 at x = 1 with h = 0.01. What number do you get, and is it above or below the true slope 3?
(1.01^3-1)/0.01=(1.030301-1)/0.01=3.0301, slightly above 3 (the error is about 3h + h² = 0.0301).
3. Why is f(x)=|x| not differentiable at 0 even though its graph has no gaps?
The difference quotient is |h|/h, which equals +1 for positive h and −1 for negative h. The two sides disagree, so no single limit exists. Geometrically, the corner never straightens however far you zoom.

Takeaways

  • Average rate = change in output ÷ change in input = slope of a secant.
  • The derivative is the limit of that slope as the window shrinks: the slope of the tangent, the "speed at an instant".
  • Smooth curves look straight when zoomed in; the derivative is the slope of that straight line.
  • f'(a) in "output units per input unit"; its sign says up or down, its size says how sensitive. Training reads this slope of the loss and steps against it.
2.2

Derivative rules

The questionComputing a derivative from the limit definition took us a page of algebra for the simple function t^2. A neural network is a formula with millions of terms. Nobody differentiates it by taking limits. What is the short list of facts and rules from which every derivative can be assembled — the same list a deep-learning library has built in?

Intuition

Think of Lego. You do not memorise every possible model; you learn the few kinds of brick (power, exponential, sine…) and a few ways of snapping bricks together (add, scale, multiply, divide, nest). Differentiation works the same way: we derive the derivative of each brick once, and derive the rule for each kind of connection once. After that, any formula built from those pieces can be differentiated mechanically. This lesson gives the bricks, plus the connections "add", "scale", "multiply" and "divide"; the connection "nest one function inside another" gets its own lesson next, because it is the most important one in all of AI.

Build it up

Constants and lines. A constant never changes, so its slope is 0: (c)'=0. A line mx+b has constant slope m, so (mx+b)'=m.

The power rule. For x^n: (x^n)'=n\,x^{n-1}. Why? Expand (x+h)^n. The first term is x^n, the next is n x^{n-1}h, and every later term contains h^2 or higher. Subtract x^n, divide by h: you get nx^{n-1} plus terms that still contain a factor h and vanish in the limit. Check with n=2: (x+h)^2=x^2+2xh+h^2 gives 2x — which matches the 6 we found at x = 3. It also holds for negative and fractional powers: (1/x)'=-1/x^2 and (\sqrt x)'=\frac1{2\sqrt x}.

Scaling and adding. Derivatives pass through sums and constant multiples: (af+bg)'=af'+bg' (the technical word is linearity). If the profit is 3 × (sales) minus 2 × (returns), its rate of change is 3 × (rate of sales) minus 2 × (rate of returns).

The product rule. Picture a rectangle with width f and height g, both growing. Its area is fg. When the sides grow by \Delta f and \Delta g, the new area gains a strip along the width (f\,\Delta g), a strip along the height (g\,\Delta f), and a tiny corner \Delta f\,\Delta g that is negligible. Dividing by the step: (fg)'=f'g+fg'. "Derivative of first times second, plus first times derivative of second."

The quotient rule. From the product rule one gets \left(\frac fg\right)'=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}, valid where g\ne0.

The special bricks.

  • e^x is the function whose slope equals its height: (e^x)'=e^x. (That is why e is special: it is the base where this works with no extra factor.)
  • The natural log undoes e^x, and (\ln x)'=1/x for x>0: the bigger x is, the flatter the log curve.
  • (\sin x)'=\cos x and (\cos x)'=-\sin x, with x in radians (the slope of the sine wave is the cosine wave, shifted a quarter turn).
  • The sigmoid \sigma(x)=\frac1{1+e^{-x}}: by the quotient rule, \sigma'(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^2}=\sigma(x)\,(1-\sigma(x)). (We used (e^{-x})'=-e^{-x}; the minus sign is justified formally in the next lesson.) Its slope is always at most 1/4, reached at x = 0.
  • \tanh: \tanh'(x)=1-\tanh^2(x), at most 1.
  • ReLU: \max(0,x) has slope 0 for x < 0 and 1 for x > 0, and no derivative exactly at 0 (software uses 0 there).
Worked example: three derivatives, with numbers

(a) Polynomial. f(x)=3x^4-2x+5. Term by term, f'(x)=12x^3-2. At x = 2: 12\cdot8-2=94.

(b) Product. f(x)=x^2e^x. With first = x^2 (derivative 2x) and second = e^x (derivative e^x): f'(x)=2xe^x+x^2e^x=(2x+x^2)e^x. At x = 1: 3e\approx8.1548.

(c) Quotient. f(x)=\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}. Numerator derivative 1, denominator derivative 2x: f'(x)=\frac{1\cdot(1+x^2)-x\cdot2x}{(1+x^2)^2}=\frac{1-x^2}{(1+x^2)^2}. At x = 2: \frac{1-4}{25}=-0.12 (the curve is slightly falling there).

(d) Sigmoid. \sigma(2)=0.8808, so \sigma'(2)=0.8808\times0.1192=0.1050; and \sigma'(0)=0.5\times0.5=0.25, the steepest the sigmoid ever gets.

The formal rules

\begin{aligned}(x^n)'&=nx^{n-1} &(af+bg)'&=af'+bg' \\ (fg)'&=f'g+fg' &\Big(\tfrac fg\Big)'&=\tfrac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}\\ (e^x)'&=e^x &(\ln x)'&=\tfrac1x\\ (\sin x)'&=\cos x &(\cos x)'&=-\sin x\\ \sigma'&=\sigma(1-\sigma) &\tanh'&=1-\tanh^2\end{aligned}
f, gtwo functions of x; f', g' are their derivatives
a, b, nconstants (numbers that do not depend on x)
eEuler's number ≈ 2.71828
lnnatural logarithm (log base e; written log in most ML code)
σthe sigmoid 1/(1+e^{-x}), squashing numbers into (0, 1)

Lab

  2. Orange dots are the formula you typed. When they sit exactly on the green line, your derivative is right and the readout turns green.

Common traps
  • The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives. For x\cdot x the right answer is 2x, not 1\cdot1=1. The same goes for quotients.
  • Power rule vs exponential. x^2 (variable base, constant exponent) uses 2x. 2^x (constant base, variable exponent) is a different animal: its derivative is 2^x\ln2, not x2^{x-1}.
  • Sign in the quotient rule. It is f'g-fg' in that order; swapping them flips the sign.
  • Rules apply to what is inside the pieces as given. \sin(x^2) is not \cos(x^2); the inner function matters (next lesson).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Activation derivatives are the table above. ReLU's derivative is the cheap 0-or-1 mask; sigmoid's is \sigma(1-\sigma)\le\tfrac14; tanh's is 1-\tanh^2\le1. These numbers decide how strongly error signals survive passing through a layer (see the vanishing-gradient discussion next lesson).
  • Automatic differentiation (PyTorch, JAX, TensorFlow) is exactly this: every primitive operation (+, *, exp, log, sin…) ships with its own derivative rule, and the library combines them for you. The lab's green curve is computed by the same engine.
  • Loss functions. The derivative of the log-loss -\ln p is -1/p (from (\ln x)'=1/x): confident wrong answers, where p is tiny, have enormous slopes.

Check yourself

1. Differentiate 5x^3-x and evaluate at x = 1.
15x^2-1, which is 14 at x = 1.
2. Find the slope of xe^x at x = 0.
Product rule: 1\cdot e^x+x\cdot e^x=(1+x)e^x. At 0 this is 1.
3. Find the derivative of 1/x at x = 2 using the quotient rule, and say why the sigmoid's slope can never exceed 1/4.
Quotient rule with numerator 1: \frac{0\cdot x-1\cdot1}{x^2}=-\frac1{x^2}, which is −1/4 at 2. For the sigmoid, \sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma) is the product of two numbers in (0, 1) that add to 1; such a product is largest when both equal ½, giving ¼.

Takeaways

  • Every derivative is built from a handful of bricks (powers, exp, ln, sin/cos, σ, tanh, ReLU) and rules (sum, scale, product, quotient, chain).
  • Power rule (x^n)'=nx^{n-1}; product f'g+fg'; quotient (f'g-fg')/g^2.
  • \sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\le\frac14, \tanh'=1-\tanh^2, ReLU' is 0 or 1.
  • Autodiff libraries are this table plus the chain rule, applied automatically.
2.3

The chain rule

The questionA neural network computes \hat y=f_3(f_2(f_1(x))): layers nested inside layers. The loss depends on the weights of layer 1 only through everything that happens in layers 2 and 3. How does a tiny change of one weight at the very bottom of this stack change the loss at the very top — and how can a computer work this out for a billion weights without going insane?

Intuition

Exchange rates. Suppose 1 euro buys 1.1 dollars, and 1 dollar buys 150 yen. How many yen does 1 euro buy? You multiply the rates: 1.1\times150=165. Nobody finds that surprising. The chain rule is the same fact about rates of change: if turning knob x moves the middle quantity u at some rate, and moving u moves the output y at another rate, then the overall rate of y per x is the product of the two rates.

Another picture: three gears. Turning the first gear once turns the second 3 times (rate 3) and the second turns the third 2 times per turn (rate 2); so the third spins 3 × 2 = 6 times per turn of the first. The rates multiply along the chain. In the lab below you can see this: a small wiggle goes in at x, is stretched by the first machine, and stretched again by the second.

Build it up

Name the pieces. An inner machine u=g(x) feeds an outer machine y=f(u); together y=f(g(x)), a composition (Module 0, Functions). Nudge the input by a small \Delta x. Close to a point, each machine is almost a straight line (lesson 2.1), so

\Delta u\approx g'(x)\,\Delta x,\qquad \Delta y\approx f'(u)\,\Delta u\;=\;f'(u)\,g'(x)\,\Delta x

Divide by \Delta x and let it shrink: \frac{dy}{dx}=f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x). The one subtle point: the outer derivative must be evaluated at the inner value u=g(x), because that is where the outer machine actually operates.

Worked example 1: y = sin(x²) at x = 0.8

Inner: u=x^2, so u=0.64 and g'(x)=2x=1.6. Outer: y=\sin u, so y=\sin0.64=0.5972 and f'(u)=\cos u=\cos0.64=0.8021. Chain rule: dy/dx=0.8021\times1.6=1.2834.

Check by brute force: at x = 0.81, y=\sin(0.6561)=0.61003. The difference quotient is (0.61003-0.59720)/0.01=1.2836 — essentially the same number.

Worked example 2: three links, y = e^{sin(x²)} at x = 1

Break it into u=x^2, v=\sin u, y=e^v. Rates: du/dx=2x=2; dv/du=\cos u=\cos1=0.5403; dy/dv=e^v=e^{\sin1}=2.3198. Multiply: 2\times0.5403\times2.3198=2.5068. A longer chain is just a longer product.

Worked example 3: one neuron, by hand (this is backprop)

A neuron with input x = 1, weight w = 2, bias b = −1 computes z=wx+b, then a=\sigma(z), and the loss against target t = 1 is L=(a-t)^2.

Forward (compute and remember): z=2\cdot1-1=1; a=\sigma(1)=0.7311; L=(0.7311-1)^2=0.0723.

Backward (multiply local rates, from the loss downward): \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}=2(a-t)=-0.5379; \frac{\partial a}{\partial z}=\sigma(1)(1-\sigma(1))=0.1966; \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}=x=1. So \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=-0.5379\times0.1966\times1=-0.1058. The negative sign says: increase the weight to lower the loss. (Likewise \partial L/\partial b=-0.1058, because \partial z/\partial b=1.)

That second calculation is backpropagation. In the forward pass we compute and store every intermediate value. In the backward pass we walk from the output to the input, multiplying each machine's local rate onto the running product. Each local rate is simple (a number from the table in lesson 2.2); the chain rule glues them into the derivative of the whole thing. When the network has billions of weights, the same backward sweep computes all their derivatives, reusing the shared products instead of redoing them.

Branches add. If x feeds the output through two different paths (as in y=x\cdot x, where both factors depend on x), the contributions of the paths add: that is the product rule again. Residual connections in modern networks are exactly such a second path.

Vanishing gradients. A chain of many layers multiplies many local rates. If each is below 1, the product collapses: sigmoid slopes are at most 0.25, so ten sigmoid layers can shrink the signal by 0.25^{10}\approx10^{-6}. If each is above 1, it explodes. Much of the history of deep learning (ReLU, careful initialisation, residual connections, normalisation) is the effort to keep this product near 1.

The formal statement

\frac{d}{dx}f\big(g(x)\big)=f'\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}
g(x)the inner function; its output u is the input of the outer function
f′(g(x))the derivative of the outer function, evaluated at the inner function's output (not at x!)
g′(x)the derivative of the inner function
dy/du · du/dxLeibniz form: the "du" appears to cancel as in fractions. A helpful mnemonic, although the real justification is the linear-approximation argument above.

Labs

Try this
  1. Watch the three gauges. The wiggle at x is stretched by machine g (local rate shown in the box) and again by machine f. The caption states the numbers at every moment; press Pause or Step ¼ wiggle to examine one instant.
  2. Check the readout: \Delta u/\Delta x equals g′, \Delta y/\Delta u equals f′, and their product matches the measured \Delta y/\Delta x. Shrink the wiggle size to see the match tighten.
  3. Change the outer machine to sigmoid(u) and the base point to −2 or 2: the sigmoid is nearly flat there, local rate ≈ 0.1, and the wiggle all but disappears — a vanishing gradient in miniature.
  4. Then explore the computational graph (second lab): press forward and backward and compare each local derivative with the numbers you computed by hand.
Common traps
  • Forgetting the inner derivative. \frac{d}{dx}\sin(2x)=2\cos(2x), not \cos(2x).
  • Evaluating the outer derivative at x instead of at g(x). In example 1 you need \cos(0.64), not \cos(0.8).
  • Confusing composition with a product. \sin(x)\cdot x^2 (a product, product rule) is different from \sin(x^2) (a composition, chain rule).
  • Treating "cancelling" literally. dy/du\cdot du/dx is not an algebra of fractions in general (and in several variables it becomes matrix multiplication).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Backpropagation is the chain rule. It is the reason deep learning is practical: one backward sweep yields every weight's derivative at about the cost of one forward pass.
  • Vanishing and exploding gradients are products of many local rates drifting toward 0 or ∞. They motivate ReLU, LSTM/GRU gates, residual connections, batch/layer normalisation and gradient clipping.
  • Residual connections y=x+F(x) add a second path with local rate 1, so the derivative is 1+F'(x) and signals survive through hundreds of layers.
  • Autodiff tapes (PyTorch's autograd) record the forward operations and replay the chain rule in reverse.

Check yourself

1. Differentiate (3x+1)^5 at x = 0.
Outer u^5: 5u^4; inner 3x+1: 3. At x = 0, u = 1: 5\cdot1\cdot3=15.
2. Differentiate e^{-x^2} and evaluate at x = 1.
Outer e^u (derivative e^u), inner -x^2 (derivative -2x): -2xe^{-x^2}. At 1: -2/e=-0.7358.
3. A 10-layer sigmoid network: if every local rate were at its maximum 0.25 and the weights contribute a factor of 1, how large is the end-to-end rate? What does that tell you about training the lowest layer?
0.25^{10}\approx9.5\times10^{-7}. The bottom layer's gradient is about a millionth of the top layer's, so it learns extremely slowly — the vanishing-gradient problem.

Takeaways

  • Rates of change multiply along a chain: dy/dx=(dy/du)(du/dx), with the outer derivative evaluated at the inner value.
  • Backprop = forward pass (store values) + backward pass (multiply local derivatives).
  • Branches add, chains multiply; long chains can vanish or explode.
2.4

Partial derivatives & the gradient

The questionA model's loss depends not on one knob but on many: the weights w_1,\dots,w_n. Turning knob 1 alone changes the loss at some rate; turning knob 2 alone, at another. If you may turn all knobs a little at once, in which combination should you turn them to reduce the loss as fast as possible?

Intuition

You are standing on a hillside in fog, with a compass. The height of the ground depends on two numbers: how far east (x) and how far north (y) you are. Walk exactly east and measure how steeply the ground rises under your feet: that is the partial derivative with respect to x. Walk exactly north and measure again: the partial with respect to y. Each tells you the slope along one compass axis while the other coordinate stays frozen.

But you are free to walk northeast, or any direction. It turns out that these two numbers are enough to predict the slope in every direction, and that bundling them into a vector (f_x,f_y) gives an arrow that points straight uphill, in the direction of steepest climb — the gradient. Its length is how steep that steepest climb is. Walk against it to descend fastest. That arrow is the compass of machine learning.

Build it up

Partial derivative = ordinary derivative with the other variables frozen. For f(x,y)=x^2+3y^2, to find the change per unit of x, treat y as a constant: \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2x (the 3y^2 is a constant and its derivative is 0). Likewise \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=6y. The curly \partial ("partial", "del") is a reminder that other variables exist and are being held fixed.

The gradient stacks all the partials into one vector:

\nabla f(\mathbf p)=\begin{pmatrix}\partial f/\partial x_1\\ \vdots\\ \partial f/\partial x_n\end{pmatrix}\Big|_{\mathbf p}
Worked example: the gradient of x² + 3y² at (1, 2)

Value: f=1+12=13. Partials: f_x=2x=2, f_y=6y=12. So \nabla f(1,2)=(2,12): the surface is rising 2 units per step east and 12 units per step north, so mostly "north". Its length \|\nabla f\|=\sqrt{2^2+12^2}=12.17: the steepest slope available from this point, in the direction (0.164,\,0.986).

Slope in any direction. Walk in the direction of a unit vector \mathbf u (length 1). Near the point the surface is almost a tilted plane (this is the zoom-in idea of 2.1 in two dimensions): f(\mathbf p+h\mathbf u)\approx f(\mathbf p)+h\,\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u. So the slope in direction u, the directional derivative, is the dot product D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u=\|\nabla f\|\cos\theta, where θ is the angle between u and the gradient (the dot-product formula of Module 0/1). Reading the cosine:

  • θ = 0 (walk along ∇f): \cos=1, the slope is the largest possible, \|\nabla f\|. Steepest ascent.
  • θ = 180° (walk against ∇f): \cos=-1, the most negative slope. Steepest descent.
  • θ = 90° (walk perpendicular): slope 0. You are walking along a contour line (a level curve of constant f). So the gradient is always perpendicular to the contour through the point.
Worked example: directions from (1, 2)

Gradient (2, 12). Walk along \mathbf u=(0.6,0.8) (unit length, since 0.36 + 0.64 = 1): D_{\mathbf u}f=2(0.6)+12(0.8)=10.8. The maximum possible is 12.17 (direction of ∇f), and a walk perpendicular, \mathbf u=(12,-2)/12.17, gives 2\cdot12-12\cdot2=0.

One descent step. Take step size \eta=0.05 against the gradient: (1,2)-0.05\,(2,12)=(0.9,\,1.4). Then f=0.81+3(1.96)=6.69, down from 13. (A tiny step would lower f by about \eta\|\nabla f\|^2=0.05\times148=7.4; the actual drop is 6.31 because the surface curves, a topic for lesson 2.6.)

Worked example: the loss of one data point

A line model \hat y=wx+b with w = 1, b = 0 sees the example x = 2, y = 5. Prediction 2, error −3, loss L=(wx+b-y)^2=9. By the chain rule: \partial L/\partial w=2(wx+b-y)\cdot x=2(-3)(2)=-12, \partial L/\partial b=2(-3)(1)=-6. The gradient (-12,-6) says: increasing both w and b lowers the loss, and w matters twice as much (its input x is 2).

The formal definition

\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf p)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(\mathbf p+h\,\mathbf e_i)-f(\mathbf p)}{h},\qquad \nabla f=\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\Big),\qquad D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u
f(x₁,…,xₙ)a function of several inputs that returns one number (a height, a loss)

eithe unit step along axis i (1 in slot i, 0 elsewhere): nudge only coordinate i
∂f/∂xithe partial derivative: slope when only xi changes

Common traps
  • The gradient points uphill. To minimise a loss you subtract it: \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla L. Adding it makes the loss grow.
  • The gradient lives in the input (parameter) space, not on the graph of f. It is a vector with one entry per variable, not a point on the surface.
  • Steepest depends on how you measure distance. "Steepest descent" is relative to the ordinary (Euclidean) length of a step. If one weight is measured in thousands and another in thousandths, the gradient's direction is dominated by the awkward scale: this is why feature scaling and adaptive optimisers (Adam) matter.
  • Gradient zero does not mean minimum. It can also be a maximum or a saddle (try it in the lab).
  • ∂ is not d. \partial f/\partial x freezes the other variables; the total change when everything moves is a sum over all variables.
Where it shows up in AI
  • \nabla_\theta L is the thing training computes. With a model's parameters \theta\in\mathbb R^P (P up to billions), the gradient has one entry per parameter; backprop produces all of them at once. Gradient descent, SGD, momentum and Adam all update θ using it.
  • Mini-batches. The true gradient is an average over every training example (an expectation); SGD estimates it from a small batch, which is why it is noisy but cheap.
  • Gradients with respect to inputs. Take \nabla_{\mathbf x}L instead of \nabla_{\theta}L: it tells you which pixels most affect the prediction (saliency maps), and nudging the image along it is how adversarial examples (FGSM) fool classifiers.
  • Contours and conditioning. Narrow valleys, where contours are long thin ellipses, make the gradient point mostly across the valley, not along it; that is the zig-zag you saw.

Takeaways

  • A partial derivative is a one-variable derivative with the other variables held fixed.
  • The gradient ∇f stacks all partials; it points to steepest ascent, its length is the steepest slope, and it is perpendicular to contours.
  • Slope along a unit direction u is ∇f · u = ‖∇f‖ cos θ.
  • Training updates \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla_\theta L.
2.5

Jacobian & Hessian

The questionTwo things the gradient cannot yet describe. (1) A network layer takes a vector in and gives a vector out; how do we take the derivative of that? (2) The gradient is only a slope. It does not say how quickly the slope itself changes — whether the landscape is a gentle bowl or a knife-edge canyon — and that decides how bold a step we can take. What objects capture each?

Intuition

Jacobian: the derivative of a map. Imagine a rubber map of a city being stretched and twisted onto a table. Draw a tiny circle on the original. After the deformation, close to that spot, the circle becomes a tiny ellipse — the deformation is nearly linear when you zoom in (the same zoom trick as lesson 2.1, now with inputs and outputs that are both 2D). A linear map is a matrix. The matrix that best describes the stretching at that spot is the Jacobian. In the lab, you can see the circle turn into an ellipse and the Jacobian's columns showing where the two input directions go.

Hessian: the derivative of the slope. On a road, the first derivative tells how steeply it climbs and the second derivative tells how it curves: curving up like a bowl, curving down like a hilltop. With two or more input variables, curvature depends on the direction you look, so we need a whole matrix of second derivatives: the Hessian. Its eigenvectors (Module 1) are the directions of strongest and weakest curvature; its eigenvalues are the curvatures there.

Build it up: the Jacobian

Take a map with two inputs and two outputs, F(x,y)=(u(x,y),\,v(x,y)). Each output has its own gradient. Stack those gradients as the rows of a matrix:

J=\begin{pmatrix}\partial u/\partial x&\partial u/\partial y\\ \partial v/\partial x&\partial v/\partial y\end{pmatrix}

Column 1 says "if I nudge x by one unit, the output moves by this vector"; column 2 says the same for y. A small step \mathbf d in the input produces an output change F(\mathbf p+\mathbf d)-F(\mathbf p)\approx J\,\mathbf d: the linear approximation of 2.1 and 2.4, with the number f'(a) replaced by a matrix.

Worked example: polar to Cartesian

The map F(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,\;r\sin\theta) turns a radius and angle into a point. The partials: J=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}. At r=2,\theta=\pi/2 (the point (0, 2)): J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\1&0\end{pmatrix}. Reading columns: increasing r by δ moves the point by (0, δ) — straight up, away from the origin; increasing θ by δ moves it by (−2δ, 0) — sideways along the circle, with a speed of 2 because the radius is 2. The determinant is 0\cdot0-(-2)(1)=2, equal to r: tiny patches of area get scaled by a factor of r (the familiar "area element" r\,dr\,d\theta).

Special cases you will see constantly. A linear layer \mathbf y=W\mathbf x has Jacobian W itself. An element-wise activation \mathbf a=\sigma(\mathbf z) has a diagonal Jacobian with \sigma'(z_i) on the diagonal (each output depends only on its own input). A function with one output has a Jacobian with a single row — which is the gradient (as a row).

The chain rule for vectors. For a composition F=G\circ H, the Jacobians multiply as matrices: J_F=J_G\,J_H (the same "multiply the local rates" as lesson 2.3, with matrices now). Backprop multiplies these from the loss end backward; because the loss is a single number, the leftmost factor is a row vector, so each step is a cheap vector-matrix product (a vector–Jacobian product) and the full Jacobians are never formed.

Build it up: the Hessian

Differentiate the gradient once more. For f(x,y) the second partials form the Hessian:

H=\begin{pmatrix}\partial^2f/\partial x^2&\partial^2f/\partial x\partial y\\ \partial^2f/\partial y\partial x&\partial^2f/\partial y^2\end{pmatrix}

For nice (continuously differentiable) functions the order of differentiation does not matter, so H is symmetric. Its meaning: if you walk in direction u (unit), the curvature of the path (second derivative along it) is \kappa=\mathbf u^{\!\top}H\,\mathbf u. This quantity is always between the smallest and largest eigenvalues of H, and it equals them along the eigenvectors.

Worked example: a saddle, f = x²y at (1, 2)

First partials f_x=2xy,\;f_y=x^2. Second: f_{xx}=2y=4,\;f_{xy}=2x=2,\;f_{yy}=0. So H=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&0\end{pmatrix}. Trace 4, determinant 0-4=-4, so \lambda=2\pm\sqrt{4+4}=4.83,\;-0.83. One positive, one negative: the surface curves up in one direction and down in another there — a saddle shape (like a mountain pass).

Worked example: a bowl and a Newton step

For f=x^2+3y^2 the Hessian is H=\mathrm{diag}(2,6) everywhere: curvature 2 along x and 6 along y; the ratio 6/2 = 3 is the condition number (how elongated the bowl is). Newton's method uses curvature to choose the step: \mathbf p\leftarrow\mathbf p-H^{-1}\nabla f. From (1, 2): \nabla f=(2,12), H^{-1}\nabla f=(2/2,\,12/6)=(1,2), so the new point is (0, 0) — the exact minimum in one step, because a quadratic is exactly its own second-order model (next lesson).

Classifying a flat spot. Where \nabla f=0: both eigenvalues positive → minimum (bowl); both negative → maximum (hilltop); mixed signs → saddle; a zero eigenvalue → flat in some direction (the test is inconclusive without higher derivatives).

The formal definitions

J_F(\mathbf p)_{ij}=\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j}(\mathbf p),\qquad H_f(\mathbf p)_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}(\mathbf p),\qquad \kappa(\mathbf u)=\mathbf u^{\!\top}H\mathbf u
F : ℝⁿ → ℝᵐa vector function with n inputs and m outputs
JFthe Jacobian, an m × n matrix: row i is the gradient of output Fi; column j is the sensitivity to input xj. (Some books use the transpose.)
det Jlocal volume (area) scaling factor when m = n; its sign tells whether orientation flips
Hfthe Hessian, an n × n symmetric matrix of second partials of a scalar function f
λeigenvalues of H: the principal curvatures
uᵀHuthe curvature of f along the unit direction u

Common traps
  • Shape conventions. A Jacobian of \mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m is m × n in this course (the "numerator layout"). Many libraries and papers use the transpose; always check shapes.
  • The Jacobian is only a local picture. It is the best linear approximation near one point; far from it (see the big circle) the true map bends.
  • The full Hessian is enormous. A model with P = 10⁹ parameters has a Hessian with 10¹⁸ entries — impossible to store. Methods use Hessian–vector products or diagonal / low-rank approximations.
  • Zero gradient + positive eigenvalues = minimum, but one negative eigenvalue = saddle. In high dimensions saddles are far more common than true minima.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Layer derivatives. Every layer's Jacobian (W, or \mathrm{diag}(\sigma')) is what backprop multiplies together; frameworks implement "vector–Jacobian products".
  • Change of variables in generative models. Normalising flows transform a simple distribution through an invertible map, and the density gets multiplied by |\det J| — the area-scaling factor you saw here.
  • Second-order optimisation. Newton's method, L-BFGS and natural-gradient methods use curvature to set step sizes per direction; Adam's per-weight scaling is a crude diagonal curvature stand-in.
  • Sharp vs flat minima. The largest Hessian eigenvalue of the loss measures how sharp a minimum is; flatter minima tend to generalise better, an idea behind sharpness-aware minimisation. The largest eigenvalue also limits the usable learning rate (next lesson).

Check yourself

1. A layer maps ℝ³ → ℝ². What shape is its Jacobian, and what is the Jacobian of F(x,y)=(x+y,\;xy) at (1, 2)?
2 × 3 (outputs × inputs). For the second part: u=x+y has gradient (1, 1); v=xy has gradient (y,x)=(2,1). So J=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix}, determinant 1-2=-1 (areas keep their size but orientation flips).
2. What is the Hessian of f=x^2+y^2, and is the origin a minimum, a maximum or a saddle?
H=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}, eigenvalues 2, 2 (both positive): a minimum; the bowl is equally curved in every direction.
3. Why is it a problem to compute the full Hessian of a neural network?
It has P × P entries, where P is the number of parameters (millions to billions), so storing or inverting it is impossible. In practice one uses Hessian–vector products, diagonal or low-rank approximations, or avoids second derivatives entirely.

Takeaways

  • Jacobian = matrix of first partials of a vector map: the best local linear approximation (a circle becomes an ellipse); det J is the area scaling.
  • Hessian = symmetric matrix of second partials of a scalar function: curvature in every direction (\mathbf u^{\!\top}H\mathbf u); eigenvalues are principal curvatures.
  • Backprop multiplies Jacobians; Newton-type methods use the Hessian; its biggest eigenvalue sets how sharp a minimum is and how large a step is safe.
2.6

Linear & quadratic approximation (Taylor)

The questionWhen training a model we only ever know the loss landscape at the point where we stand: its height, its slope, its curvature. Yet we must decide on a step. How good a prediction of the landscape can we build from just those local facts — and how large a step can we take before the prediction becomes untrustworthy and training explodes?

Intuition

You want to draw a map of a mountain, but you may only stand in one spot and take measurements. What would you record? First, your height: the crudest map is "the ground is flat at this height everywhere". Next, the slope: now the map is a tilted plane, much better close by. Then the curvature: a gentle bowl or dome, better still. Each extra measurement lets you draw a more faithful map, but only near where you stand; far away, any such map can mislead.

That is a Taylor approximation: replace a complicated function by a simple polynomial built from its derivatives at one point. Order 0 is a constant, order 1 a line, order 2 a parabola (which is all we need for optimisation), and so on. Use the lab to build the stack up one order at a time.

Build it up

Pick an expansion point a. A polynomial c_0+c_1(x-a)+c_2(x-a)^2+\cdots that matches f at a in value and in its first n derivatives is a good local copy. Matching the value forces c_0=f(a). Matching the slope forces c_1=f'(a). Matching the second derivative: the polynomial's second derivative at a is 2c_2, so c_2=f''(a)/2. In general the k-th derivative of (x-a)^k is k! (that is 1·2·3···k), so c_k=f^{(k)}(a)/k!.

f(x)\approx\underbrace{f(a)}_{\text{height}}+\underbrace{f'(a)(x-a)}_{\text{slope}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12f''(a)(x-a)^2}_{\text{curvature}}+\cdots+\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n
Worked example 1: sin x around 0

Derivatives at 0: sin → 0, cos → 1, −sin → 0, −cos → −1, then repeating. So \sin x\approx x-\frac{x^3}{6}+\frac{x^5}{120}. Test at x = 0.5 (true \sin0.5=0.479426):

ordervalueerror
1 (line) 0.50.5000000.0206
3: 0.5 − 0.125/60.4791670.00026
5: + 0.03125/1200.4794270.0000015

Each new term shrinks the error roughly a hundredfold, because the error is about the size of the first neglected term.

Worked example 2: e^x at 0, evaluated at x = 1

Every derivative of e^x at 0 is 1, so T_n(x)=1+x+\frac{x^2}{2}+\cdots+\frac{x^n}{n!}. At x = 1: orders 0 through 6 give 1, 2, 2.5, 2.6667, 2.7083, 2.7167, 2.7181 against the true e = 2.71828. A formula for e from nothing but derivatives at one point!

Not forever. A Taylor series may only work inside a limited distance from a. The function 1/(1-x) equals 1+x+x^2+\cdots, but only for |x| < 1: outside that radius, adding more terms makes things worse. Try it in the lab with the preset 1/(1-x). Functions with corners (abs(x)) are approximated badly at the corner at every order.

Several variables: the model every optimiser uses

For a loss L(\boldsymbol\theta) and a small step \mathbf d, the same construction gives

L(\boldsymbol\theta+\mathbf d)\approx L(\boldsymbol\theta)+\nabla L\cdot\mathbf d+\tfrac12\,\mathbf d^{\!\top}H\,\mathbf d

The first-order part says what gradient descent assumes: the landscape is a tilted plane, so go downhill. The second-order part says how the landscape curves. Gradient descent picks \mathbf d=-\eta\nabla L. Substituting, with \mathbf g=\nabla L:

L(\boldsymbol\theta-\eta\mathbf g)\approx L(\boldsymbol\theta)-\eta\|\mathbf g\|^2+\tfrac12\eta^2\,\mathbf g^{\!\top}H\mathbf g

The middle term is the gain, shrinking the loss in proportion to η. The last is the price of curvature, growing with η2. For small η the gain wins; for large η the price wins and the loss goes up. Minimising this expression over η gives the best step \eta^*=\frac{\|\mathbf g\|^2}{\mathbf g^{\!\top}H\mathbf g}, which is simply 1/\lambda if the gradient points along an eigenvector with curvature λ. Going further, any η above about 2/\lambda makes the loss grow along that direction, which is the origin of the rule "learning rate must be below 2 / (largest curvature)".

Worked example: how big a step? f(w) = 3w²

One weight, starting at w = 1. Gradient 6w, curvature \lambda=f''=6. The update w\leftarrow w-\eta\cdot6w=(1-6\eta)\,w multiplies w by 1-6\eta each step:

ηfactor 1 − 6ηbehaviour
0.10.4smooth decay: 1 → 0.4 → 0.16 → …
1/6 = 0.167 (= 1/λ)0lands exactly on the minimum in one step
0.2−0.2overshoots but still shrinks (oscillating decay)
1/3 (= 2/λ)−1bounces between +1 and −1 forever
0.5−2diverges: 1 → −2 → 4 → −8 …

The safe window is 0<\eta<2/\lambda=1/3. For a multi-weight bowl, the stiffest direction (the largest eigenvalue) sets the limit, while the flattest direction then moves slowly: the zig-zag from lesson 2.4.

Newton's method goes one step further: it minimises the second-order model exactly, jumping to \boldsymbol\theta-H^{-1}\nabla L (lesson 2.5's example). It needs H, which is why it is expensive for big networks.

The formal statement

T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k,\qquad f(x)-T_n(x)\approx\frac{f^{(n+1)}(a)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}
Tnthe Taylor polynomial of order (degree) n around a

k!factorial: 1·2·…·k (and 0! = 1)
(x − a)the distance from the expansion point; higher orders matter less when it is small
errorroughly the first term you left out; shrinks fast near a

Common traps
  • Taylor approximations are local. A higher order is not always better far from a (see 1/(1-x)). The "trust region" is limited by the nearest singularity or non-smooth point.
  • Real losses are not exact quadratics. The rule η < 2/λ is a local guide; curvature changes as you move, so the safe rate changes through training.
  • Order vs. number of terms. The sin series has only odd powers; its "order-2" approximation equals the order-1 one.
  • Factorial matters. The coefficient is f^{(k)}(a)/k!, not just f^{(k)}(a). Forgetting k! gives wildly wrong polynomials.
  • Piecewise-linear networks (ReLU) have a zero Hessian almost everywhere; curvature there lives in the kinks, so second-order reasoning must be applied with care.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Why gradient descent works at all. It is the first-order Taylor model: for a small enough step, L(\theta-\eta g)\approx L-\eta\|g\|^2<L.
  • Learning-rate limits. Stability needs \eta<2/\lambda_{\max}(H). Empirically, training often hovers at this "edge of stability", and learning-rate warm-up is partly a response to curvature early in training.
  • Newton, L-BFGS, natural gradient, trust-region methods are all built on the second-order model.
  • Linearisation arguments (adversarial examples via a first-order step, neural-tangent-kernel analysis, influence functions) assume the network is locally a tilted plane.
  • Numerics. Functions like log1p(x) and expm1(x) use Taylor-style expansions to stay accurate for tiny x where log(1+x) loses digits.

Check yourself

1. Write the order-2 Taylor polynomial of cos x around 0 and use it for x = 0.2.

2. For f(w)=5w^2, what learning rate reaches the minimum in one step, and what happens with η = 0.25?
Curvature f''=10. The update factor is 1-10\eta: η = 0.1 gives 0 (one step). With η = 0.25 the factor is −1.5, so |w| grows by 1.5× each step and flips sign: it diverges (and 0.25 > 2/10 = 0.2 as predicted).
3. Why does the quadratic term of the multivariable Taylor expansion make the loss increase for large step sizes, even when the gradient points downhill?
The gradient only guarantees that a tiny step goes downhill (gain \eta\|g\|^2, linear in η). The curvature cost \tfrac12\eta^2g^{\top}Hg grows with η2, so for large enough η it exceeds the gain: you overshoot the valley and land higher on the opposite slope.

Takeaways

  • Taylor polynomial: match height, slope, curvature… at one point: T_n=\sum f^{(k)}(a)(x-a)^k/k!. Good near a, not forever.
  • Gradient descent = first-order model; Newton = second-order model.
  • Step size: gain linear in η, curvature cost quadratic; stability needs \eta<2/\lambda_{\max}, and η = 1/λ is ideal along a single direction.
2.7

Integrals

The questionYou know the car's speed at every moment (a speedometer log). How far did it drive? Equivalently: a probability density tells you how concentrated probability is near each value, yet the chance of landing in a range must be a total. How do you add up infinitely many infinitesimal pieces?

Intuition

If a car drives at a constant 20 m/s for 5 s, the distance is 20 × 5 = 100 m, which is also the area of a rectangle of height 20 and width 5 on a speed-versus-time graph. If the speed changes, the area under the curve is still the distance: cut the time into many short slices, treat the speed in each slice as roughly constant, and add up (speed × slice width). The thinner the slices, the more exact the total. The integral is what this sum becomes when the slices are infinitely thin.

The idea is the mirror image of lesson 2.1. A derivative takes position and extracts speed; an integral takes speed and rebuilds position by accumulating. They are opposites, and the fundamental theorem of calculus makes that precise.

Build it up

Riemann sums. To estimate the area under f between a and b: split [a,b] into n slices of width \Delta x=(b-a)/n, pick a sample point x_i in each slice (left end, right end or middle), and add the rectangle areas f(x_i)\Delta x.

Worked example: the area under x² from 0 to 2

Use n = 4, so \Delta x=0.5 and the slices start at 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5.

Left endpoints: heights 0, 0.25, 1, 2.25 → sum 3.5 → area 3.5\times0.5=1.75 (too low: the curve rises, so each rectangle lies under it).

Right endpoints: heights 0.25, 1, 2.25, 4 → sum 7.5 → 3.75 (too high).

Midpoints: at 0.25, 0.75, 1.25, 1.75 → heights 0.0625, 0.5625, 1.5625, 3.0625 → sum 5.25 → 2.625 (very close).

With n = 100, the left sum is 2.6268 and the midpoint sum is 2.6666. They squeeze in on 8/3=2.6667.

The limit of these sums as n\to\infty is the definite integral. In the notation, the sum symbol Σ has become a stretched S, \int, and the slice width Δx has become the infinitesimal dx: "a continuous Σ". Areas below the axis count negatively, so the integral is a signed area, a net total of accumulation (distance driven forward minus backward).

The fundamental theorem. Define the area function A(t)=\int_a^t f(x)\,dx: the area collected from a up to t. How quickly does A grow as t increases a tiny bit? The new sliver has width Δt and height about f(t), so \Delta A\approx f(t)\Delta t, i.e. A'(t)=f(t). The rate at which the area grows equals the height of the curve. Reading it backwards: if you can find any function F whose derivative is f (an antiderivative), then \int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a), with no sums at all.

Worked example: using the theorem

An antiderivative of x^2 is F(x)=x^3/3 (its derivative is x^2). So \int_0^2x^2dx=F(2)-F(0)=\tfrac83-0=2.6667 — exactly what the rectangles were approaching. Also: \int_0^3(2x+1)dx=[x^2+x]_0^3=12.

Worked example: a probability density

A random variable X in [0, 1] has density p(x)=2x (larger values more likely). Check it is a valid density: \int_0^12x\,dx=[x^2]_0^1=1 ✓. The probability that X < 0.5 is \int_0^{0.5}2x\,dx=0.25. The expected value (mean), a probability-weighted average, is E[X]=\int_0^1x\cdot2x\,dx=\big[\tfrac23x^3\big]_0^1=\tfrac23, above the midpoint 0.5 as expected. The expectation is exactly "Σ value × probability" with Σ turned into ∫ (Module 3).

Handy facts. Linearity: \int(af+bg)=a\!\int\!f+b\!\int\!g. Splitting: \int_a^b=\int_a^c+\int_c^b. Reversing the limits flips the sign. Substitution is the chain rule backwards. Many natural integrals have no formula (the bell curve e^{-x^2} has no elementary antiderivative, although its total area \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx=\sqrt\pi is known by a clever trick); then we integrate numerically with the very sums above, or by Monte Carlo (random sampling).

The formal definition

\int_a^bf(x)\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_i)\,\Delta x,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\int_a^tf(x)\,dx=f(t),\qquad \int_a^bf(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)\;\;(F'=f)

Common traps
  • Integral = signed area. The total distance driven versus the net displacement differ if the speed goes negative; the "area" below the axis subtracts.
  • Forgetting the constant in an indefinite integral: \int2x\,dx=x^2+C. In definite integrals it cancels.
  • A density is not a probability. p(x) can exceed 1 (a narrow spike); only \int p\,dx over a range is a probability, and it is at most 1.
  • Not every function has a neat antiderivative. That does not mean the integral does not exist; it just needs numerics.
  • The dx matters. Changing variables (e.g. from metres to kilometres) rescales both f and dx; forgetting the factor gives a wrong total.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Probability densities. Every continuous distribution (Gaussian, exponential…) lives through integrals: probabilities are areas, the CDF is the area function A(t) from the lab, the PDF is its derivative.
  • Expectations and the loss. "Expected loss" E[\ell]=\int\ell(x)p(x)\,dx is a continuous Σ. Training estimates it with an average over a batch, i.e. Monte Carlo integration: sample points, average the heights.
  • Normalising constants. Many models define an unnormalised density and must divide by Z=\int\tilde p(x)\,dx (the partition function); that intractable integral is the central difficulty of energy-based models and Bayesian posteriors.
  • Variational inference, KL divergence, entropy are integrals of log-densities (Modules 3–5); AUC of an ROC curve is an integral; diffusion models and neural ODEs integrate differential equations over time with exactly the numerical sums of this lesson.

Check yourself

1. Compute \int_0^3(2x+1)\,dx by finding an antiderivative.
F(x)=x^2+x, so F(3)-F(0)=12-0=12. (Check with geometry: a trapezoid under the line from height 1 to 7 over a width of 3: \tfrac{1+7}{2}\cdot3=12.)
2. Let A(x)=\int_0^xt^2\,dt. What is A′(x), and what is A(3)?
By the fundamental theorem A'(x)=x^2 (no integration needed). Also A(x)=x^3/3, so A(3)=9.
3. With four left-endpoint rectangles for \int_0^2x^2dx you get 1.75. Is the exact value larger or smaller, and why?
Larger: the exact value is 8/3 ≈ 2.667. Because x² is increasing, the left-endpoint rectangle in each slice sits below the curve and misses area. Right endpoints (3.75) overshoot; midpoints (2.625) nearly cancel the two errors.

Takeaways

  • An integral is a continuous sum: area under a curve = limit of Σ f(xi)Δx; below-axis area counts negatively.
  • Fundamental theorem: the area function has derivative f, and \int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a). Integration and differentiation undo each other.
  • Probabilities, expectations and normalising constants are integrals; machine learning approximates them by sampling averages.
