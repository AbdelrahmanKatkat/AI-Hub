Module 7 · Foundation models & geospatial embeddings

A foundation model is trained once on a huge archive of unlabelled imagery and then reused for many tasks. Here you learn how such a model is pretrained by hiding parts of the picture, how it reads a pixel's history through time, how its outputs become embeddings you can search with, how to fine-tune it with few labels, and how to find a pretrained model in a STAC catalogue without reading its code.

7.1

Self-supervised pretraining on Earth imagery

The questionA national archive holds ten million Sentinel-2 tiles, but only 2,000 of them have hand-drawn land-cover labels. Labelling is slow and expensive. Can a model learn what Earth looks like from the 9,998,000 unlabelled tiles, so that a few labels are enough later?

Intuition: a cloze test for pictures

Think of the exercise schoolchildren do with a sentence where some words are blanked out: "The river flows into the ___." To fill the gap you use the rest of the sentence. Nobody had to label the sentence; the text itself supplies the answer. Masked image modelling does the same with pictures. Cover some patches of an image, ask the model to predict what is under them, and score it against the hidden truth. The label is free because the image was already there.

Why does this teach a model anything useful about Earth? To fill a hidden field of crops you must know that fields are often rectangular, that a river continues across a gap, that bare soil is brighter in red than water, and that near-infrared behaves differently from visible light. Predicting the missing part forces the model to learn these regularities. That is what we want the encoder to keep for later tasks.

Satellite images add a twist: they have several bands (wavelengths). A tile with red, green and near-infrared is a stack of three pictures that must agree. Predicting the hidden near-infrared from the visible red and green teaches spectral relations, such as how healthy vegetation separates the two.

Build it up: one tiny tile, step by step

Step 1, the tile. Take a 4 × 4 pixel tile with three bands (red, green, near-infrared) at 10 m resolution. It holds 4\cdot4\cdot3=48 numbers. Each band is one 4 × 4 grid, with values as reflectance between 0 and 1.

Step 2, the patches. Cut the tile into non-overlapping patches of 2 × 2 pixels. That gives \frac{4}{2}\cdot\frac{4}{2}=4 patches (top-left P1, top-right P2, bottom-left P3, bottom-right P4). Each patch holds 2\cdot2\cdot3=12 numbers. Transformers, including the Vision Transformer you met in Computer Vision, treat each patch as one token.

Step 3, the mask. Hide a fraction of patches. Masked-autoencoder methods such as MAE (natural images) often hide about 75% of patches, because neighbouring patches are so alike that a lower ratio makes the task too easy. Here 75% of 4 patches is 3, so the encoder sees only P1 and must reconstruct P2, P3 and P4. In numbers: 12 of the 48 values are visible and 36 are hidden.

Step 4, the reconstruction. The encoder reads the visible tokens and a decoder writes out pixel values for every masked patch. Only the masked pixels count in the loss; the visible ones were given as input and copying them would teach nothing.

Worked example: a baseline reconstructor

To see the arithmetic, use the simplest possible "model": fill every hidden pixel of a band with the average of the visible patch in that same band. A trained network does far better, but the loss is computed the same way.

Visible patch P1, per band (the four pixels of P1):

bandP1 pixelsmean (prediction)
red0.20, 0.22, 0.21, 0.230.2150
green0.10, 0.12, 0.11, 0.130.1150
NIR0.50, 0.52, 0.51, 0.530.5150

The hidden values (P2, P3, P4) for red are 0.40, 0.42, 0.41, 0.43 | 0.60, 0.62, 0.61, 0.63 | 0.30, 0.32, 0.31, 0.33. Each squared error is (x-\hat x)^2 against the prediction 0.2150. Summing the 12 squared errors for red gives 0.8415. Green's 12 hidden values give the same sum, 0.8415, and near-infrared gives 0.5615, because its values are closer to its visible mean.

Total over the 36 hidden values: 0.8415+0.8415+0.5615=2.2445. Dividing by 36 gives the masked mean squared error \mathcal L=0.06235. A better model would lower this number; the visible patch contributes nothing to it.

Where this sits in the pipeline: this is the Model stage, during pretraining on an unlabelled archive. The output of pretraining is the encoder weights, which will later be stored and described in a STAC MLM record (Module 6).

Try this
  1. Press Play and watch the sequence: the tile is hidden patch by patch, then the baseline model fills the holes one at a time. Read the caption at each step.
  2. Drag the mask ratio slider from 25% to 90%. At low ratios the hidden patches sit next to visible ones and are easy to guess; at high ratios the guesses fall apart. Notice the loss in the readout.

Common traps
  • A low reconstruction loss is not automatically a good encoder. Predicting smooth blur gives low loss. Check downstream performance on a few labels, not only the pretraining loss.
  • Clouds in the archive. A cloud is a hidden region that the model can learn to paint. Mask or exclude cloudy tiles before pretraining, or the encoder learns cloud texture as "Earth".
  • Mixing masked and unmasked normalisation. Normalising statistics must come from the pretraining data only and be applied identically later. Mismatched normalisation silently degrades fine-tuning.
  • Band order. If the model was pretrained on (B02, B03, B04, B08) and you feed (B04, B03, B02, B08), it still runs, but its features are wrong. Read the band list in the model record.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Vision Transformers (Computer Vision, Module 5): the patch-and-token idea used here is the one from the ViT lesson; masked pretraining is covered in self-supervised vision.
  • Geospatial foundation models adapt masked pretraining to multispectral and multi-temporal stacks. Several research groups have published such models; look up the exact bands, patch size, pretraining archive and licence of any model you consider on its own model card, because details differ between them.
  • Other pretext tasks (contrastive learning on two views of the same place, predicting a location or date) follow the same logic: the label is manufactured from the data.

Check yourself

1. A tile has 48 numbers (4 × 4 pixels × 3 bands) and patches are 2 × 2. With a 75% mask, how many numbers does the encoder see?
4 patches; 75% masked means 3 hidden and 1 visible. The visible patch holds 2·2·3 = 12 numbers, so the encoder sees 12 of 48 (25%) and must predict 36.
2. Why is the loss computed on masked values only?
Visible values were given to the model as input. Including them would reward copying the input, which teaches nothing. The task is to predict what was hidden.
3. The baseline predicts red = 0.2150 everywhere in the hidden area. Why would a trained model usually beat this?
A trained model uses spatial context (edges, field boundaries, the other bands) and learned textures, so its predictions can differ from the visible mean in the right direction. The baseline ignores where a patch is and what sits next to it.

Takeaways

  • Masked image modelling makes labels from the imagery itself: hide patches, predict them, score only the hidden values.
  • For a 4 × 4 × 3 tile with 2 × 2 patches and a 75% mask, 36 of 48 numbers are hidden; the loss averages their squared errors (here 0.06235 for the baseline).
  • A low pretraining loss is necessary but not sufficient; evaluate the encoder on real downstream tasks.
7.2

Time series of imagery

The questionA single image of a field in June shows green. Is it maize, grassland or a young orchard? Two fields can look identical on one date and differ completely over the season: maize is bare in spring, green in summer and harvested in autumn. How do we let a model see the season, when clouds hide some of the dates?

Intuition: every pixel has a history

Stop looking at a satellite image as a picture and look at one pixel instead. Over a year it is a sequence of numbers, one per visit of the satellite, that rises in spring as the crop grows, peaks in summer and falls at harvest. That rise and fall is its phenology: the timing of the life cycle, and it is a strong fingerprint of what covers the ground.

A temporal stack is the same idea for a whole tile. Instead of one image with C bands, you have T dates, each with C bands on an H × W grid. Its shape is T\times C\times H\times W. The model can then look along the time axis (does this pixel green up in April?) as well as across space (are its neighbours doing the same?).

The complication is clouds. An optical satellite cannot see through cloud, so some dates are unusable at some pixels. Radar (Sentinel-1) sees through cloud, which is one reason stacks often combine optical and radar dates.

Build it up: four dates, one gap

Step 1, the observations. Take one pixel and four acquisition dates. Its NDVI (the vegetation index from Module 4, defined as (\text{NIR}-\text{Red})/(\text{NIR}+\text{Red})) is observed on days 0, 10, 20 and 40 of a season. On day 20 a cloud covers the pixel, so that value is missing.

datedayNDVI
100.31
2100.45
320missing (cloud)
4400.70

Step 2, the naive fill. The simplest rule is to average the neighbouring observations: (0.45+0.70)/2=0.575. This ignores the calendar, and it is wrong here, because the neighbours are not evenly spaced: 0.45 was observed 10 days before the gap, while 0.70 was observed 20 days after it.

Step 3, the time-aware fill. Linear interpolation places the missing value on the straight line between the two known points, in proportion to time. The fraction of the way from day 10 to day 40 at which day 20 sits is \frac{20-10}{40-10}=\frac13. So the estimate is

\hat y(20)=0.45+(0.70-0.45)\cdot\frac{1}{3}=0.45+0.0833=0.5333

The 0.5333 value is the one to use. The naive index-based rule gave 0.575, which overstates the greenness by 0.042 because it treats a 10-day step and a 30-day step as if they were the same.

Worked example, in full

Known points: (t_a,y_a)=(10,0.45) and (t_b,y_b)=(40,0.70). Query time t=20. Slope \frac{0.70-0.45}{40-10}=\frac{0.25}{30}=0.008333 per day. Then 0.45+0.008333\cdot(20-10)=0.45+0.08333=0.53333. The estimate is 0.533 (rounded).

Where this sits in the pipeline: this is Preprocessing: gap filling before the stack goes into the model. The filled value should be flagged in a quality layer, so that the model and the map user know which numbers were observed and which were interpolated.

The formal definition

\hat y(t)=y_a+\bigl(y_b-y_a\bigr)\frac{t-t_a}{t_b-t_a},\qquad t_a<t<t_b
tthe day we want to estimate (here 20)
ta, tbthe days of the observations on either side of the gap (10 and 40)
ya, ybthe NDVI values observed on those days (0.45 and 0.70)
\hat y(t)the estimated value at time t

A temporal stack is then just X\in\mathbb R^{T\times C\times H\times W} with a companion mask M\in\{0,1\}^{T\times H\times W} saying which values were observed. The model can learn from the mask as well as from the values.

Lab: a pixel's year, with gaps

Try this
  1. Press Play. The true phenology curve is drawn first, then the satellite visits one by one; some are removed by clouds. Watch the orange gap-fill line appear as linear segments between the observations.
  2. Raise the cloud probability to 60%. Notice long stretches with no observation and how the straight-line guess drifts away from the true curve.
  3. Change the number of dates from 4 to 12 with the same season. More dates reduce the error of the interpolation.
  4. Edit the true curve formula, for example to a double peak 0.2+0.3*exp(-((t-100)/25)^2)+0.3*exp(-((t-240)/25)^2). A crop with two cuts looks like this; linear interpolation can miss the peaks if they fall inside a gap.
Common traps
  • Interpolating across a winter gap. A straight line from a summer peak to an autumn value invents green-up that never happened. Limit the maximum gap you fill, or fill with a model that knows the season.
  • Index spacing instead of time spacing. As the worked example shows, ignoring the calendar biases the estimate. Always store the acquisition date with each observation.
  • Cloud shadows and haze. Cloud masks miss thin cloud and shadows, which lower NDVI and look like a dip in the curve. Use the quality bands of the product, not only a single threshold.
  • Mixing sensors. Two satellites are not identical. Cross-calibrate before stacking, or the step at the switch-over date becomes a fake change.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Crop-type mapping with time-series classifiers (1D convolutional networks, LSTMs and transformers over the per-pixel NDVI or band curves) is a standard use: the timing of the curve separates crops that look alike on one date.
  • Change detection over years of stacks (a forest that stops greening is a warning sign) uses the same time axis.
  • Temporal attention lets a transformer weigh the dates that matter for a class (for example the flowering month) and ignore the clouded ones; the mask above is passed to it so that missing dates are ignored, not treated as zero.

Check yourself

1. Using the worked example, what is the time-aware estimate for day 30 if the observations are 0.45 at day 10 and 0.70 at day 40?
0.45 + 0.25 · (30−10)/30 = 0.45 + 0.25 · 0.6667 = 0.6167.
2. Why is the naive average (0.45 + 0.70)/2 = 0.575 the wrong estimate for day 20?
Because the observations are not evenly spaced in time. Day 20 is one third of the way from day 10 to day 40, not halfway. Linear interpolation in time gives 0.533.
3. A pixel is cloudy on every date between day 60 and day 200. What should a careful pipeline do with it?
Not fill a 140-day gap with a straight line. Flag the period as unobserved, lean on the other dates or on radar data, or leave the value as missing for the model to handle through its mask.

Takeaways

  • A pixel over time is a curve (phenology); a temporal stack is T\times C\times H\times W.
  • Fill gaps by linear interpolation in time: \hat y(t)=y_a+(y_b-y_a)\frac{t-t_a}{t_b-t_a}. Here 0.5333, not the index-based 0.575.
  • Keep a mask of what was observed, and do not interpolate across long gaps.
7.3

Embeddings & similarity search

The questionA disaster-response team has one photo of a flooded farm in a district they know well, and they want every other place in a 30-million-tile archive that looks like it, without first writing a rule for "flooded farmland". How can a machine compare two tiles by how they look, and then find the closest ones quickly?

Intuition: a fingerprint and a compass

A pretrained encoder turns each tile into a list of numbers, an embedding. Tiles that look alike get lists that point in similar directions; tiles that look different point elsewhere. The embedding is a fingerprint of the tile, and it is much shorter than the tile itself: a 768-number vector instead of 150,000 pixel values.

To compare two fingerprints we need a measure. The most useful one for embeddings is the angle between them, not their distance. Imagine two arrows from the origin. If they point the same way, the places they describe are alike, whatever the length of the arrows. The cosine similarity measures exactly that angle: 1 means the same direction, 0 means perpendicular, −1 means opposite.

Length can carry nuisance information. A tile that is brighter, or a model that outputs bigger numbers for some images, would look "more similar" to everything under a plain dot product. Normalising each embedding to unit length removes that effect, and then the dot product is the cosine.

Build it up: three tiles, by hand

Take three toy 3-number embeddings (real ones have hundreds of numbers, but the arithmetic is the same):

A=(3,\,4,\,0),\qquad B=(1,\,2,\,2),\qquad C=(-2,\,1,\,2)

Step 1, the lengths. \lVert A\rVert=\sqrt{9+16+0}=5, \lVert B\rVert=\sqrt{1+4+4}=3, \lVert C\rVert=\sqrt{4+1+4}=3.

Step 2, unit-normalise (divide each vector by its length, so it has length 1):

\hat a=\left(\tfrac35,\tfrac45,0\right)=(0.6,\,0.8,\,0),\quad \hat b=\left(\tfrac13,\tfrac23,\tfrac23\right),\quad \hat c=\left(-\tfrac23,\tfrac13,\tfrac23\right)

Check: 0.6^2+0.8^2=1; (1/3)^2+(2/3)^2+(2/3)^2=1/9+4/9+4/9=1; (4/9)+(1/9)+(4/9)=1.

Step 3, the cosines are dot products of unit vectors:

Worked example

\cos(A,B)=\hat a\cdot\hat b=0.6\cdot\tfrac13+0.8\cdot\tfrac23+0\cdot\tfrac23=0.2+0.5333=0.7333 (equal to \frac{11}{15}).

\cos(A,C)=\hat a\cdot\hat c=0.6\cdot(-\tfrac23)+0.8\cdot\tfrac13+0=-0.4+0.2667=-0.1333 (equal to -\frac{2}{15}).

\cos(B,C)=\hat b\cdot\hat c=(\tfrac13)(-\tfrac23)+(\tfrac23)(\tfrac13)+(\tfrac23)(\tfrac23)=\tfrac49=0.4444.

Reading: A and B point most nearly the same way (0.73), B and C are moderately related (0.44), and A and C are almost perpendicular (−0.13). In terms of distance on the unit sphere, \lVert\hat a-\hat b\rVert=\sqrt{2-2\cos}=\sqrt{0.5333}=0.7303.

Build it up: finding the nearest tiles

Now the search. A query tile q=(2,1,2) is compared with five catalogue embeddings, E1 = (1,0,0), E2 = (2,1,1), E3 = (0,3,4), E4 = (2,2,1) and E5 = (−1,2,0). The nearest-neighbour search computes a similarity for every catalogue item and keeps the top k (here k = 2).

The query has length 3. Cosine = raw dot ÷ (3 × length of E). The top-2 by cosine are E2 (0.9526) and E4 (0.8889). Ranking by the raw dot product gives E3 (11) and E4 (8) instead, because E3 is simply longer. That is the nuisance that normalisation removes.

Where this sits in the pipeline: this is the Inference stage of a search service: encode the query once, then scan (or index) the catalogue of embeddings. For millions of tiles, the scan is replaced by an approximate index (for example a graph or a product-quantised index in a vector library), which trades a little accuracy for a large speed-up.

The formal definition

\cos(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\lVert\mathbf u\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf v\rVert}\qquad\text{and, for unit vectors,}\qquad \cos(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)=\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v
\operatorname{top}\text{-}k(q)=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{k\text{ items}}\ \cos(\mathbf q,\mathbf e_i)
u·vdot product: the sum of products of matching entries, \sum_i u_i v_i
‖u‖length (Euclidean norm) of u: \sqrt{\sum_i u_i^2}
qthe query embedding (the tile you clicked on)
eithe embedding of catalogue tile i
top-kthe k items with the highest similarity

Lab: tiles on a sphere

Try this

  2. Click a dot. It becomes the query (white ring). The top-k most similar tiles light up one after another, joined by arcs whose brightness shows the cosine.

Common traps
  • Skipping normalisation. The raw dot product mixes angle with length, as the table shows (E3 jumps ahead of E2). Normalise, or state clearly that you use a length-aware score.
  • Believing one similarity number. A cosine of 0.9 between two tiles means they are close in the encoder's space, not that they are the same land cover. Check matches visually and, when possible, against labels.
  • Nuisance features. If the encoder was trained on images with strong seasonal or sensor differences, similarity follows the season and the sensor. Pick query and catalogue tiles from the same date range, or use an encoder trained to ignore them.
  • Exact search that does not scale. A brute-force scan is fine for 10,000 tiles and too slow for 30 million on a laptop. Plan an index early, and measure recall against the exact answer.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Search by example: "find more tiles like this one" is an embedding query. Many EO catalogues and vector-database products work this way.
  • Retrieval-augmented and multimodal models match a text or image query to a database of embeddings by cosine, the same way as here.
  • Kernels: the dot product of feature vectors is the basis of kernel methods; see the kernels lesson in Advanced Mathematics for the same geometry.

Check yourself

1. Two embeddings are (2, 4) and (1, 2). What is their cosine similarity, and why?
1. The second is exactly half the first, so they point in the same direction. Cosine ignores length: (2·1 + 4·2)/(√20 · √5) = 10/10 = 1.
2. Using the worked example, what is the distance between the unit vectors for A and C?
cos(A,C) = −2/15 = −0.1333, so the distance is √(2 − 2·(−0.1333)) = √2.2667 = 1.5055.
3. Why does the raw dot product rank E3 above E2 for query q = (2, 1, 2), when cosine ranks E2 above E3?
E3 is longer (length 5 against √6 ≈ 2.45), so its raw dot product (11) is bigger than E2's (7), even though E2 points more nearly the same way as q (cosine 0.9526 against 0.7333).

Takeaways

  • An embedding is a fingerprint; cosine similarity compares directions and ignores length.
  • For unit vectors the dot product equals the cosine, and distance is \sqrt{2-2\cos}.
  • Search is "score all, keep the top k"; at scale, use an approximate index and measure what it misses.
7.4

Fine-tuning foundation models

The questionA flood-mapping agency has a pretrained encoder with about 86 million parameters and only 200 labelled tiles. If it trains all 86 million numbers on 200 tiles, the model can memorise the tiles. If it trains only a final classifier, it may not adapt to water in turbid rivers. Which parts should we train, and how many numbers does each choice touch?

Intuition: keep the general knowledge, adjust the specific part

Early layers of a pretrained encoder learn general things: edges, textures, the difference between smooth and rough ground, the spectral signature of water. Later layers combine them into concepts closer to the target task. When the new task is similar, you can keep most of the encoder and adjust only the top. When it is very different (new bands, new sensor), you adjust more.

There are three common choices, from cheapest to most flexible:

  • Linear probing: freeze the whole encoder and train only the final classifier. Fast, very little to overfit, but it cannot change the features.
  • Partial fine-tuning: freeze the first blocks and train the last few blocks plus the classifier. The most common middle ground.
  • Adapters: freeze every original weight and insert small trainable bottleneck modules into each block. They add a few percent of parameters and keep the original model intact, so one encoder can serve many tasks.

Then there is the question of the learning rate: even when a layer is trainable, a large step can destroy what it learned. A common rule is to use a smaller learning rate for earlier layers and a larger one for the head.

Build it up: counting a ViT-B-like encoder

A ViT-B/16 (12 blocks, token width D=768, MLP width M=3072, 16 × 16 patches) is a useful reference. Count its parameters from its design. Each number below is an exact integer count, not a rounded figure.

Step 1, one transformer block.

  • Attention (query, key, value and output projections, each D×D with bias): 4D^2+4D=4\cdot589{,}824+3{,}072=2{,}362{,}368.
  • MLP (D→M and M→D, with biases): 2DM+M+D=4{,}718{,}592+3{,}072+768=4{,}722{,}432.
  • Two LayerNorms (each has a scale and a shift of width D): 4D=3{,}072.
  • Block total: 2{,}362{,}368+4{,}722{,}432+3{,}072=7{,}087{,}872.

Step 2, the whole encoder. Twelve blocks: 12\times7{,}087{,}872=85{,}054{,}464. Add the patch embedding (16·16·3 inputs times 768 outputs, plus 768 biases): 590{,}592. Add the class token (768), the position embeddings (197 positions × 768 = 151,296) and the final LayerNorm (1,536). Total: 85,798,656, about 85.8 M.

Step 3, the head. A classifier with 10 classes on the 768-dimensional output adds 768\cdot10+10=7{,}690, so the model with its head has 85,806,346 parameters. A 1000-class ImageNet head would add 769,000, giving 86,567,656, which is the familiar "about 86 M" figure for ViT-B/16.

Changing the bands. Pretrained on three bands, the patch embedding has 590,592 parameters. If your data has 12 bands, the patch embedding becomes 16\cdot16\cdot12\cdot768+768=2{,}360{,}064. This is the one layer you must re-create (and train) when the input changes; its new weights can be initialised by repeating or averaging the pretrained ones.

Where this sits in the pipeline: Model stage, during fine-tuning on the labelled set. The final checkpoint must be recorded with which layers were frozen, which is needed to reproduce it and to describe its trainable parts honestly.

The formal definition

\theta=(\theta_{\text{frozen}},\theta_{\text{train}}),\qquad \min_{\theta_{\text{train}}}\ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal L\bigl(f(x_i;\theta_{\text{frozen}},\theta_{\text{train}}),y_i\bigr)
θall parameters of the model (here 85.8 M)
θfrozen, θtrainthe split: frozen values are never updated, trainable ones are
Nthe number of labelled examples (200 tiles)
ℒthe loss on one example, for example cross-entropy for classes
f(xi; θ)the model's output for tile i

The gradient is computed only for \theta_{\text{train}}. Freezing saves memory for optimiser states and gradients, but the forward pass through frozen blocks still costs the same, and back-propagation still has to pass through any trainable block above them. Parameter counts are not the same as compute.

Lab: the fine-tuning dial

Try this

  2. Switch to adapters and vary the bottleneck width r from 4 to 256. The count grows linearly in r; find where it passes 5% of the model.
  3. Press Play to see the illustrative learning curves for each option. Notice that more trainable parameters start faster but can overfit with 200 tiles. The curves are schematic: they show shapes, not measured accuracy.
  4. Set the number of labelled tiles to 50 and then 2,000. The best choice moves towards more trainable parameters as the labels grow.
Common traps
  • Fine-tuning everything on a tiny set. A large encoder can memorise a few hundred tiles. Use a frozen or adapter setting first and only unfreeze when validation rises.
  • One learning rate for all layers. A rate that suits the new head can wreck the pretrained blocks. Use smaller rates lower down.
  • Mismatched normalisation. Use the pretraining statistics, not the statistics of your small dataset, unless you have a reason to change them.
  • Reporting only the parameter count. Trainable parameters say nothing about the forward compute or memory, and a frozen encoder still has to be run on every image.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Linear probing versus fine-tuning is the standard way to measure how good a pretrained representation is in vision research.
  • Parameter-efficient methods (adapters, low-rank adaptation) let one large encoder serve many tasks, each with a small stored module; this is how most large language models are customised.
  • Geospatial foundation models are usually adapted this way: freeze or adapt the encoder, train a segmentation or classification head on the local labels, and report which option was used.

Check yourself

1. How many parameters does a linear classifier from 768 features to 10 classes have, including biases?
768 × 10 + 10 = 7,690.
2. Why does freezing the first 8 blocks not make the forward pass cheaper?
Every image still passes through all 12 blocks in the forward direction; freezing only skips computing and storing gradients and optimiser state for the frozen weights.
3. You change from 3 to 12 input bands with 16 × 16 patches. How many parameters does the new patch embedding have, and why must it be re-created?
16·16·12·768 + 768 = 2,360,064. Its input size depends on the number of bands, so the pretrained 590,592-parameter layer cannot be reused as it is; its weights can be initialised from the pretrained ones and then trained.

Takeaways

  • Choose how much to train by how different your task is and how many labels you have: head only, last blocks, or adapters.
  • A ViT-B/16-like encoder is 85.8 M parameters (86.6 M with a 1000-class head); freezing the first 8 blocks leaves 33.05% trainable.
  • Count parameters and compute separately, and use a lower learning rate for the pretrained layers.
7.5

Finding pretrained models via STAC MLM

The questionYou need a model that labels land cover on 10 m Sentinel-2 imagery from four bands (blue, green, red and near-infrared). A catalogue lists 300 models. Downloading each one to read its README would take a week. How do you search by what the model does, and check that your data can be fed to it, before downloading a single weight file?

Intuition: a model's label on the shelf

A shop does not ask you to open every box to find a kettle. Each box has a label: the product, its voltage, its size, its warranty. A model record is the same idea for a trained model. It is a small STAC Item, a standard JSON description (Module 2), that says what the model is for (its task), what it expects as input (which bands, what size of patch, what units), what it produces, how big it is, and where its weights and code live. With the MLM extension those fields have agreed names, so a search tool can filter every model in the catalogue in the same way.

Two things must match before a model can run on your data: the band list (same bands, same order, same names) and the resolution the model was trained at. A third, often forgotten, is the input scale: a model trained on reflectance from 0 to 1 will give nonsense on raw digital numbers from 0 to 10,000.

Build it up: five records, three filters

Here is a small synthetic catalogue (the records are invented for this lesson; they are not real published models). Each row is a simplified view of a record's MLM fields.

recordtaskinput bandsGSD (m)parameters
R1 synth-lc-seg-asemantic-segmentationB02 B03 B04 B08 B11 B121024.1 M
R2 synth-scene-cls-bscene-classificationB02 B03 B04 B081011.2 M
R3 synth-lc-seg-csemantic-segmentationB04 B08106.8 M
R4 synth-lc-seg-dsemantic-segmentationB02 B03 B04 B083024.1 M

You want: task = semantic-segmentation, bands include B02, B03, B04 and B08, and GSD at most 10 m.

Worked example: the filter, stage by stage

Filter 1, task. Keep records whose task is semantic-segmentation: R1, R3, R4, R5. R2 is a scene classifier and is removed. 4 remain.

Filter 2, bands. Keep records whose input bands include {B02, B03, B04, B08}. R3 has only B04 and B08, so it is removed. R1, R4 and R5 pass. 3 remain.

Filter 3, resolution. Keep GSD ≤ 10 m. R4 is at 30 m and is removed. R1 and R5 pass. 2 remain: R1 and R5.

Only now do you open the two records. Compare their parameter counts (24.1 M and 41.5 M) and the MLM fields that matter for running them.

Step 4, the compatibility check. Searching is not enough: the data you hold must fit the model. Write down the dataset's bands, resolution and scale, and compare them with the model's mlm:input. Three outcomes are possible: compatible (same bands, same GSD, same scale); needs resampling (same bands but a different GSD, which is allowed only if the record says the model accepts it and you resample first); and incompatible (a band is missing or the scale differs by a factor that you cannot correct).

For R1, a 10 m four-band dataset with reflectance scaled to 0–1 is compatible on the four required bands. The model also needs B11 and B12, which are 20 m bands in Sentinel-2: they must be resampled to 10 m, so the check reads "needs resampling" unless you supply them at 10 m.

The formal definition

\text{compatible}(D,M)=\bigl[\,\mathcal B_M\subseteq\mathcal B_D\,\bigr]\wedge\bigl[\,s_D=s_M\,\bigr]\wedge\bigl[\,g_D=g_M\ \text{or resampling declared}\,\bigr]

𝓑M, 𝓑Q, 𝓑Dsets of band names: the model's inputs, the bands the search requires, and the bands your data has

Common traps
  • Searching by name. Names are free text and often say nothing about bands or resolution. Search by task, bands and GSD fields.
  • Band order and naming. B8 (10 m, NIR) and B8A (20 m, narrow NIR) are different bands. The same wavelength can carry different names in different catalogues. Match names to the model's list, and then the order.
  • Scale mismatch. Digital numbers (0–10,000) and reflectance (0–1) differ by a factor 10,000. The model still runs and returns confident nonsense. Read the scaling in the record and in your pipeline.
  • Trusting the claims. A record states parameter counts and input shapes. Check them against the artifact once downloaded (Module 6 covers validation), and check the licence before reuse.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Model catalogues for Earth observation publish records like these so that a pipeline can pick a model by task and inputs, the same way a data catalogue picks a scene.
  • Reproducible inference: a stored record lets someone run the model a year later with the same band list, normalisation and tiling, without asking the original author.
  • Model families: a fine-tuned model can link to its base model with a derived-from link (Module 6), so the lineage of a foundation model is visible.

Check yourself

1. Using the worked example's filters, how many of the five records would remain if the task filter were dropped but the band and GSD filters kept?
Bands ⊇ {B02, B03, B04, B08} keeps R1, R2, R4, R5 (R3 is removed). GSD ≤ 10 m then removes R4, leaving R1, R2 and R5: three records.
2. Your data is 10 m, has B02–B08, but its values are digital numbers from 0 to 10,000. R1 expects reflectance 0–1. Is the match compatible?
No. The bands and GSD match, but the scale does not. Divide by 10,000 first (after radiometric correction), and then the record can be checked as compatible.
3. Why is "B8" not safely interchangeable with "B8A" when matching a record?
B8 is a broad near-infrared band at 10 m, while B8A is a narrower band at 20 m. They sample different parts of the spectrum at different resolutions, so a model trained on one is not guaranteed to work on the other.

Takeaways

  • A model record is a STAC Item with MLM fields: task, input bands and shape, resolution, parameters and weights.
  • Search in stages (task, bands, resolution), then check compatibility of your data with the record, including scale.
  • Treat every claim in a record as something to verify once you have the artifact.
