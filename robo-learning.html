Module 7 · Learning for robots

Some robot skills are easier to show than to program: how to pour, how to walk over gravel, how to recover after a slip. This module covers the two main ways a robot learns them, imitation (copy a teacher's demonstrations, then fix the mistakes the copy makes) and reinforcement learning (try actions, keep what earns reward), and then how to train safely in simulation and how to write a reward that means what you intend.

Where this sits in the loop. Learning happens in the learn stage of the pipeline: data is collected while the robot runs, a model is trained offline (often on a GPU), and the trained policy is then executed in the act loop at the controller's rate. 7.1

Imitation learning and behaviour cloning

The questionA delivery robot must keep itself in its lane. A skilled human has driven the route while we recorded every steering command. Can we train a model on those recordings, so that the robot copies the human? And if the copy fits the recordings almost perfectly, why might it still drive off the road?

Intuition

A learner driver learns partly by watching a teacher. The teacher sees a situation (the car has drifted a little to the left) and responds (a gentle turn to the right). If the learner has seen many such pairs, it can respond the same way to a new drift. This is imitation learning, and its simplest form, behaviour cloning (BC), is just supervised learning: the input is the state the robot observes, the output is the action the expert took, and the training data are the recorded pairs.

The catch is that the teacher only ever shows the situations the teacher gets into. A skilled driver rarely drifts far from the lane, so the recordings contain almost no examples of what to do when the car is far off track. The learner has never been taught how to recover from that position, and a small mistake can carry it there.

Build it up, one piece at a time

Step 1: a demonstration is a list of state–action pairs. Let the state be the lateral error e (metres from the lane centre) and the action be the steering command u (metres per second of lateral velocity). A recorded demonstration is then a list of pairs (e_i, u_i).

Step 2: choose a learner. The simplest learner is a straight line through the origin, u = w\,e, with one parameter w (a negative w means "steer back towards the centre"). Its output depends on the input only through the single number e.

Step 3: fit the parameter. Choose w so that the learner's commands are as close as possible to the expert's, measured by squared error summed over the demonstrations. For a straight line through the origin, the best value has a closed form.

Worked example: fitting three demonstrations by hand

Three recorded pairs: (e, u) = (1.0,\,-1.2),\;(0.5,\,-0.4),\;(-0.5,\,0.8). The least-squares slope is

w=\frac{\sum_i e_i u_i}{\sum_i e_i^2}=\frac{(1.0)(-1.2)+(0.5)(-0.4)+(-0.5)(0.8)}{1.0^2+0.5^2+(-0.5)^2}=\frac{-1.2-0.2-0.4}{1.5}=\frac{-1.8}{1.5}=-1.20

So the learner is u = -1.2\,e: at e = 1.0 m it steers at -1.2 m/s. Now roll it out with a time step of \Delta t = 0.5 s from e_0 = 1.0 m, using e_{t+1} = e_t + \Delta t\,u_t:

e_1 = 1.0 + 0.5(-1.2\cdot 1.0) = 0.40 \text{ m},\qquad e_2 = 0.40 + 0.5(-1.2\cdot 0.40) = 0.16 \text{ m}

The error shrinks by a factor of 0.4 per step, which is what we want. The learner was fitted only on three points, and the rollout used only the learner's own predictions, not the demonstrations. The gap between those two things is the subject of the rest of this lesson.

Step 4: why a good fit can still fail. Suppose the learner makes a small error at one step and ends up at e = 2.5 m, a position the expert never showed. The learner now has no training data there. If its polynomial fit is wrong out there, it can push the robot further away, into states that are even less familiar. Each small error moves the robot into a region where the next error is larger. This is compounding error, and it is the central problem of behaviour cloning. Ross, Gordon and Bagnell (2011) showed that, in the worst case, the gap between the expert's performance and the cloned policy can grow roughly with the square of the horizon T, not with T itself.

The formal definition

Given a dataset of demonstrations \mathcal D = \{(s_i, a_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}, behaviour cloning chooses parameters \theta that minimise an average loss:

\hat\theta = \arg\min_{\theta}\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\big(\pi_\theta(s_i),\, a_i\big)

For the straight-line learner with squared loss, \ell = (\pi_\theta(e)-u)^2, this gives w = \sum e_i u_i / \sum e_i^2, the formula above.

s, astate (what the robot observes) and action (what the expert commanded)
\mathcal Dthe set of N demonstrated state–action pairs
\pi_\thetathe learned policy, a function from states to actions with parameters \theta
\ellthe loss for one pair, here squared error
e, u, wlateral error (m), steering command (m/s), and the single learned gain (1/s)
\Delta tthe time between control steps (s)

Common traps
  • Low training loss does not mean a good robot. The loss is measured on states the expert visited. Closed-loop performance is measured on the states the learner visits, and the two sets differ.
  • Flexible models extrapolate badly. A high-degree polynomial can fit the demonstrations almost exactly and still explode a short distance outside them, as the lab shows.
  • Demonstrations need the right state. If the expert's view and the robot's sensors differ, the policy learns a relation that does not exist on the robot.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Early autonomous driving used behaviour cloning from human steering: ALVINN (Pomerleau, 1989) learned to steer from camera images and a human driver's commands.
  • Manipulation policies trained from teleoperated demonstrations use the same supervised objective, often with image inputs and action chunks (Module 8).
  • Behaviour cloning is the usual first stage before reinforcement learning or DAgger: it gives the learner a starting point that already moves in roughly the right direction.
Pipeline placement. Learn stage (offline training); the trained policy then runs in the act loop, typically at 10–50 Hz for a visuomotor policy and faster for a low-level joint controller. Stores: the demonstration episodes (states, actions, timestamps) and the policy weights.

Check yourself

1. Using the worked example, what slope would the fit give if the third demonstration were (−0.5, 0.2) instead of (−0.5, 0.8)?
The numerator becomes (1.0)(−1.2) + (0.5)(−0.4) + (−0.5)(0.2) = −1.2 − 0.2 − 0.1 = −1.5. The denominator is still 1.5, so w = −1.5 / 1.5 = −1.00.
2. Name two different reasons the learner can end up in states the expert never demonstrated.
Any two of: a small early error from the learner's own imperfect fit; an external disturbance such as a gust or a bump; a start position that was never in the demonstrations; and a difference between the expert's and the robot's dynamics, so the same command produces a different state.
3. Why is a degree-3 polynomial risky here even though it fits the demonstrations better than a straight line?
Its extra terms are only constrained by the data inside the demonstrated band. Outside the band the cubic term dominates and the predicted command can grow in the wrong direction, so small training gains become large errors at the edges.

Takeaways

  • Behaviour cloning is supervised learning from (state, action) pairs recorded from an expert.
  • The loss is measured on the expert's states, but the robot is judged on its own states; compounding error is the gap between them.
  • Fit the simplest model that the data support, and check the learner outside the training region before trusting it.
7.2

Interactive imitation (DAgger)

The questionThe cubic learner from the last lesson fails when it starts 2.5 m off the lane, because the demonstrations never showed that region. We could record more demonstrations, but there are infinitely many places a robot can drift to. Is there a way to collect exactly the states the learner actually reaches, and still get the right action for each one?

Intuition

Think of a student essay. A teacher who reads the final essay only sees polished work and cannot tell where the student went wrong. A better teacher reads the student's draft, marks the paragraph where the argument turned, and writes what the next paragraph should say. The marking happens on the student's own draft, so it addresses the mistakes the student really makes.

DAgger (Dataset Aggregation, Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, 2011) does exactly this for a robot. Let the current learner drive, record the states it reaches, ask the expert what it would have done in each of those states, add the labelled states to the dataset, and retrain. Repeat. Over the iterations the dataset covers the states the learner actually visits, so the compounding error has less room to grow.

Build it up: distribution shift, then the loop

Step 1: the two distributions. Plain behaviour cloning trains on states drawn from the expert's distribution, written d_{\pi^*}. The learner, when deployed, visits states drawn from its own distribution, d_{\pi}. When these differ, the learner is being tested on states it was never trained on. This mismatch is called distribution shift.

Step 2: close the gap by labelling the learner's states. We cannot make the expert's distribution equal the learner's in advance, but we can add samples from the learner's distribution and label them with the expert's action. Once the dataset contains those samples, the learner is trained on the states it actually produces.

Step 3: iterate. Each iteration rolls out the current learner, labels the visited states, aggregates them with all previous data and refits. Early iterations fix the largest gaps; later ones refine the policy around the states it now visits.

Worked example: one DAgger iteration in the lab's toy

The initial demonstrations are 3 recorded trajectories of 8 states each, so |\mathcal D_0| = 24 labelled states. The DAgger iteration rolls the current cubic learner out for 12 steps from each of five start points (−2.5, −1.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.0 m). Each rollout visits 13 states including its start, so the iteration adds

5 \times 13 = 65 \text{ newly labelled states},\qquad |\mathcal D_1| = 24 + 65 = 89.

After refitting, the learner started at 2.5 m ends at 0.007 m after 12 steps, from a start that the first cubic had sent off the chart. After four iterations the dataset has 284 states and the same three test starts end within 0.007 m of the lane. These numbers were computed with the same procedure the lab uses.

The formal definition

Let \pi^* be the expert, which must be queryable: given any state, it returns an action. DAgger repeats, for i = 1, 2, \dots:

\mathcal S_i = \{\,s_t : s_t \text{ visited by } \pi_i\,\},\qquad \mathcal D_i = \mathcal D_{i-1}\cup\{(s,\pi^*(s)) : s \in \mathcal S_i\},\qquad \pi_{i+1} = \arg\min_{\pi}\, \sum_{(s,a)\in\mathcal D_i}\ell\big(\pi(s),a\big)
\pi^*the expert policy, which can be asked for an action in any state
\pi_ithe learner after iteration i
\mathcal S_ithe set of states visited when the learner \pi_i is run
\mathcal D_ithe aggregated dataset of states labelled by the expert, after iteration i
\ellthe same per-sample loss as behaviour cloning

Common traps
  • The expert must be queryable. DAgger needs labels for states the learner reaches. A human teleoperator, a planner that can run on the logged state, or a slower but reliable controller can provide them. A recording alone cannot.
  • The learner's rollouts are risky. Early learners are poor, so iterations on real hardware need a safety monitor, a speed limit and a human able to take over (Module 9).
  • Labels must match the robot's observations. If the expert labels from a privileged state the robot cannot see, the learner is asked to do the impossible.
Where it shows up in AI
  • DAgger is the standard fix for compounding error in imitation learning; human-gated variants let a person take over during a rollout and provide the correction as the label.
  • Self-driving and lane-keeping research has used interactive correction in this way, because the failures that matter are the ones the learner produces itself.
  • The same idea appears in language models as iterative fine-tuning on the model's own outputs, labelled by a judge: the data follow the model's distribution, not only a fixed corpus.
Pipeline placement. Learn stage, run as a data loop: each iteration uses the robot (or a simulator) in the act loop and the expert in the learn stage. The cost of each iteration is robot time on hardware or simulation time in the simulator, so the number of iterations is the budget to plan. Stores: the aggregated labelled dataset with the iteration number of each sample.

Check yourself

1. In DAgger, who provides the action label for a state that the learner visited?
The expert policy π*, queried on that state. The learner's own action is not used as the label, because the point is to correct it.
2. Why does DAgger help more than simply recording a much longer demonstration?
A longer demonstration still follows the expert's distribution, so it mostly adds more examples of states the expert already visits. DAgger adds states from the learner's own distribution, which is where the learner will be tested.
3. From the worked example, how many labelled states are in the dataset after two DAgger iterations in the toy?
24 demonstrated states plus 65 per iteration: 24 + 65 + 65 = 154 states.

Takeaways

  • DAgger labels the learner's own visited states, so training matches the distribution where the learner is tested.
  • It needs a queryable expert, and its early rollouts must be run safely.
  • Typically a few iterations reduce the error sharply; the dataset grows with each one.
7.3

Reinforcement learning for control

The questionA charging robot is not given a recording of how to reach the charger. It is only told that reaching the charger is good and falling into the stairwell is very bad. How can it work out, from reward alone, which move to make in each cell of the floor?

Intuition

Imagine a corridor with a goal at one end and a pit at the other. Every cell has a value: how much reward you expect to collect from there if you keep moving sensibly. A cell next to the goal is valuable, because one step gets you the reward. A cell two steps away is also valuable, but a little less, because the reward is one step further away and each move costs a little. Value flows backwards from the goal, one step at a time, the same way heat spreads through a bar of metal.

Reinforcement learning formalises this. The robot is an agent in a Markov decision process (MDP): at each step it observes a state, chooses an action, receives a reward and moves to a new state. Its goal is to maximise the total return, the discounted sum of rewards, where future rewards count a little less than immediate ones.

Build it up: return, value, and the Bellman backup

Step 1: the return. A trajectory of rewards r_0, r_1, r_2, \dots has return G = \sum_t \gamma^t r_t, where the discount \gamma \in [0,1) sets how far ahead the agent cares. With \gamma = 0.9 a reward 10 steps away is worth 0.9^{10} \approx 0.35 of the same reward now.

Step 2: the value. The value of a state is the expected return from it: V(s)=\mathbb E[G\mid s_0=s]. If we knew the values, the best action in a state would be the one whose reward plus discounted next-state value is largest. The equation that says this is the Bellman optimality equation.

Step 3: find the values by repeated backups. Start with all values at zero. Sweep over the states, and for each one replace its value with the best one-step lookahead. This is value iteration. Each sweep extends the known values one step further from the goal.

Worked example: three sweeps on the corridor

Cells 0 (pit) to 5 (goal) on a line. Entering the goal pays +1, entering the pit pays −1, both end the episode (their value is 0), and every move costs 0.04. Take \gamma = 0.9. Cell 4 is next to the goal. Its best move is right, with reward 1 - 0.04 = 0.96 and no future value, so

V_1(4) = \max\big(\underbrace{-0.04 + 0.9\cdot V_0(3)}_{\text{left}},\;\underbrace{0.96 + 0.9\cdot 0}_{\text{right, goal}}\big) = 0.96

Cell 3 in sweep 2 sees cell 4 with value 0.96: V_2(3) = \max(-0.04 + 0.9\cdot 0,\; -0.04 + 0.9\cdot 0.96) = 0.824. In sweep 3, cell 2 sees cell 3: V_3(2) = -0.04 + 0.9\cdot 0.824 = 0.7016. Cell 1, next to the pit, is at first worse, because moving left costs -1-0.04 and moving right gets -0.04 from cell 2, so it keeps moving right. The table after three sweeps is [0,\,-0.108,\,0.702,\,0.824,\,0.960,\,0] (cell 1 is still settling, as the lab shows).

Step 4: why this is "learning" and not just planning. The backup above assumed we knew the transition rule (what happens when the robot moves left) and the reward function. A real robot usually does not. Reinforcement learning methods estimate these quantities from experience, and in the end must trade off exploration (trying an action to learn about it) against exploitation (using what is already known to earn reward). The lessons 7.4 and 7.5 deal with that trade-off.

The formal definition

V^*(s)=\max_{a}\Big[\,R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V^*(s')\Big]

Value iteration applies the right-hand side repeatedly, starting from V_0 = 0, until the values stop changing. The optimal policy is then the action that achieves the maximum in each state.

s, a, s′state now, action chosen, state after the action
R(s,a)expected immediate reward for taking a in s (here: +1 or −1 on entry to a terminal cell, minus the move cost)
P(s'\mid s,a)probability of landing in s′ (deterministic in the lab, so this is 0 or 1)
\gammadiscount factor, between 0 and 1; small values make the agent short-sighted
V^*(s)optimal value of state s: the best expected return from s

Try this
  1. Press Step several times. Each press is one sweep. Watch the goal's +1 reach cell 4, then cell 3, then cell 2, and read the numbers against the worked example.
  2. Set the discount γ to 0.5. Run to convergence. Values now fall off roughly by half per step away from the goal, because future reward is worth so little.
  3. Set the cost per move to 0. With γ = 0.9 the converged values are then powers of 0.9: 1, 0.9, 0.81 and 0.729 for the cells one, two, three and four moves from the goal.
  4. Set the cost to 0.2. The values shrink, but cell 1 still points right. The pit pays −1, and walking towards the goal is always better than that, so the arrow never prefers the pit. Explain why using the worked example.
Common traps
  • The discount is a modelling choice. With γ close to 1 the agent plans far ahead and values grow large and slow to converge; with γ too small it ignores the goal. Pick γ from the task's time horizon.
  • Sparse rewards are slow. If reward only arrives at the goal, most sweeps change nothing far from it. Learning from sparse reward needs many samples, which is the reason shaping is tempting (lesson 7.6).
  • Value is not a probability. A value of 0.96 is an expected discounted reward, not a chance of success, and it depends on the reward scale.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Locomotion and manipulation policies are trained by RL in simulation, where the agent can afford millions of trials.
  • Value functions and backups underlie planning in games and robotics, and model-free relatives of this backup learn values from experience alone.
  • Language models trained with RL from human feedback use the same objects (return, value, advantage) on sequences of tokens.
Pipeline placement. Learn stage (offline, usually in simulation); the resulting policy runs in the act loop. The value table or network is updated per sweep or per batch of experience. Stores: the reward specification, the transition model or simulator, and the value or policy weights.

Check yourself

1. In the worked example, why is V₁(4) = 0.96 and not 1?
Cell 4 moves into the goal, which pays +1, but every move also costs 0.04. The goal is terminal, so there is no future value. The net is 1 − 0.04 = 0.96.
2. With γ = 0 the value of each state is just the best immediate reward. Which cell in the corridor is then the only one with a positive value?
Cell 4, which is one step from the goal. Its best immediate reward is 0.96. Every other cell has a best immediate reward of −0.04 or less, because only a move into the goal or the pit has a reward other than the move cost.
3. Why can the planning backup not be used directly on a real robot whose dynamics are unknown?
The backup needs P(s′|s,a) and R(s,a) for every action. On a real robot these must be estimated from experience, which needs exploration: trying actions to learn their outcomes, while still earning reward.

Takeaways

  • An MDP is states, actions, transitions, rewards and a discount; the return is the discounted sum of rewards.
  • Value iteration applies the Bellman optimality backup until values stop changing, and the best action follows from the values.
  • When the model is unknown, the robot must explore; that trade-off is what the next lessons address.
7.4

Policy gradients and PPO

The questionA walking policy is a neural network with millions of weights θ. It outputs joint torques, and the only feedback is a single number at the end of an episode: how far the robot walked before it fell. How do we change θ so that the robot walks further, when we cannot differentiate through the simulator and the feedback is one sparse number?

Intuition

Imagine trying a few different ways of stepping. Each time, you notice how it went. If a step pattern gave you a better outcome than you expected, you do it a little more often next time; if it went worse than expected, a little less. Nobody tells you the correct joint angles. You only increase the probability of the actions that were followed by good results.

The probability and gradient background is in Mathematics · Probability and Gradient descent; the policy as a neural network is in Machine Learning · Neural networks.

That is a policy gradient method. The policy is a probability distribution over actions, \pi_\theta(a\mid s). We sample actions from it, see the returns, and nudge θ to make good actions more probable. The key quantity is the advantage: how much better an action turned out than the average action in that state.

Build it up: from a two-action coin to PPO

Step 1: a softmax policy. Give each action a score (a logit) \theta_k and turn the scores into probabilities with the softmax: \pi_k = e^{\theta_k}/\sum_j e^{\theta_j}. Raising a logit raises that action's probability.

Step 2: the REINFORCE update. For a sampled action a with return R, the gradient of the log-probability with respect to the logits is \nabla_\theta \log\pi(a) = \mathbf e_a - \boldsymbol\pi, where \mathbf e_a is 1 in position a and 0 elsewhere. The update is \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha R\,(\mathbf e_a - \boldsymbol\pi).

Worked example: one REINFORCE step

Two actions, logits \theta = (0, 0), so \boldsymbol\pi = (0.5, 0.5). Sample action 2 (the second one), which returns R = 2. With learning rate \alpha = 0.1:

\mathbf e_2 - \boldsymbol\pi = (0-0.5,\;1-0.5) = (-0.5,\;0.5),\qquad \theta \leftarrow (0,0) + 0.1\cdot 2\cdot(-0.5,\;0.5) = (-0.1,\;0.1)

The new probabilities are \pi_2 = e^{0.1}/(e^{-0.1}+e^{0.1}) = 0.550, up from 0.500. One good outcome moved the policy by 5 percentage points. Had the return been negative, the same formula would have lowered the probability of action 2.

Step 3: a baseline reduces noise. A return of 2 is only good if the average return is lower. Subtract a baseline b, often the running average of returns, so the update uses R - b. The expected gradient does not change when b does not depend on the sampled action, but the variance of the estimate falls, and learning is faster and steadier. The bandit lab below lets you turn it off to see the difference.

Step 4: PPO limits how far one update can move the policy. A large step can ruin a good policy, because the samples were collected with the old policy. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) measures the change as the ratio r = \pi_\theta(a\mid s)/\pi_{\text{old}}(a\mid s) and clips it, so that the objective gives no extra reward for moving the ratio beyond 1\pm\varepsilon. Worked numbers:

Worked example: the clipped objective

Take \varepsilon = 0.2. For a good action with advantage A = 2 and ratio r = 1.3 (the new policy is 30% more likely to take it): the unclipped term is 1.3\cdot 2 = 2.6, the clipped term is 1.2 \cdot 2 = 2.4, and the objective takes the minimum, 2.4. Once r is above 1.2, further increases earn nothing, so the gradient is zero and the step stops pushing.

For a bad action with A = -2 and r = 0.7: unclipped 0.7\cdot(-2) = -1.4, clipped 0.8\cdot(-2) = -1.6, minimum -1.6. Pushing the probability of a bad action down beyond 0.8 earns nothing more either.

The formal definition

\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,A(s,a)\big]
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t,\;\operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big]
J(\theta)expected return of the policy with parameters θ
\pi_\theta(a\mid s)probability the policy assigns to action a in state s
A(s,a), \hat A_tadvantage: return of the action minus a baseline (the value of the state); \hat A_t is a sample estimate
r_t(\theta)ratio of new to old probability of the sampled action
\varepsilonclip range, typically a small number such as 0.1 to 0.3
\alphalearning rate, the step size of the update

Common traps
  • Sign errors. The update adds the advantage times the gradient of log-probability. Flipping the sign makes the policy learn to avoid good actions.
  • Clipping is not a trust region. PPO's clip discourages large ratio changes but does not guarantee monotonic improvement; in practice you also monitor the KL divergence or the ratio statistics.
  • Sample efficiency. On-policy methods such as PPO must discard old samples after each update. Robots pay for every sample in time and wear, so the simulation budget is usually the binding constraint.
Where it shows up in AI
  • PPO is widely used to train locomotion policies for legged robots in simulation, and to fine-tune large language models from human preference signals.
  • REINFORCE, its baseline variants and the log-derivative trick are the basis of most policy-gradient methods in robotics.
  • The softmax policy in the bandit lab is the same object as the output layer of a discrete-action neural policy (Machine Learning, neural networks).
Pipeline placement. Learn stage (training in simulation or on logged data); the policy then runs in the act loop. Training batches are typically thousands of environment steps; the deployed policy runs at the control rate of the robot. Stores: rollout buffers (states, actions, rewards, old log-probabilities) and policy weights.

Check yourself

1. With A = 2, ε = 0.2 and r = 1.5, what is the PPO objective for this sample, and does its gradient with respect to r vanish?
Unclipped: 1.5 × 2 = 3.0. Clipped: 1.2 × 2 = 2.4. The minimum is 2.4, which is the clipped term, and the gradient is zero because the clip is active for a positive advantage above 1 + ε.
2. Why does subtracting a baseline not change the expected policy gradient, but still help?
The baseline term has zero expectation when it does not depend on the sampled action, since the expected value of ∇log π is zero. It reduces the variance of the sampled estimate, so each update is less noisy.
3. In the REINFORCE worked example, what happens to the probability of action 2 if the sampled return had been −2 instead of +2?
The update flips sign: θ becomes (0.1, −0.1), so π₂ = 0.450, down from 0.500. A bad outcome lowers the probability of the action that produced it.

Takeaways

  • A policy gradient raises the log-probability of actions in proportion to their advantage.
  • A baseline lowers the variance of the estimate without biasing it.
  • PPO's clipped ratio limits how far one update moves the policy; the clip is the safety margin, not a guarantee.
7.5

Simulation training and sim-to-real transfer

The questionMillions of trials are needed to teach a legged robot to recover from a slip. Doing that on hardware would take years and break legs. So we train in a simulator, where the mass, friction and motor delay are only approximately right. Why would a policy that is perfect in the simulator fail on the real machine, and what can we do about it?

Intuition

A pilot who has only ever flown one aircraft in one set of weather is in trouble on the first flight of a different aircraft. A pilot trained on many aircraft and many winds has learned the principles that hold across them. The same is true of a policy. If the simulator is the only world the policy sees, the policy learns the quirks of that world. If the simulator is randomised, so that the mass, friction and damping change from episode to episode, the policy must work across all of them, and the real robot is hopefully one more sample from the same family.

Build it up: the reality gap and two ways to close it

Step 1: the gap. The reality gap is the difference between the simulator's dynamics and the real robot's. Sources include unmodelled friction, motor saturation and delay, sensor noise, and a link mass that is off by 10%.

Step 2: system identification. Measure the real robot's parameters (a mass, a damping, a motor constant) by fitting the simulator to logged data. This narrows the gap, but measurements have error, and some effects (contact, cable drag) are hard to model at all.

Step 3: domain randomisation. Instead of trusting one set of parameters, draw them from a distribution and train across all of them. The policy then learns to work for every world in the range, which should include the real one.

Worked example: the first control step of the lab's robot

A reaching task: a 1 kg cart starts at x=0 m and must reach x = 1 m. The controller is a PD law u = K_p(x_{\text{target}} - x) - K_d\,\dot x with K_p = 16 N/m, K_d = 6 N·s/m, and an actuator limit of 6 N. At the first step x = \dot x = 0, so the controller asks for 16 \times 1 = 16 N, which saturates to 6 N. The acceleration is

\ddot x = \frac{u - c\,\dot x}{m} = \frac{6 - 0}{1.0} = 6.0\ \text{m/s}^2.

The same first command on a 2 kg robot gives 6/2 = 3.0 m/s². The controller has not changed, but the robot responds differently. With the damping at 0.5 N·s/m, the simulated nominal robot reaches x = 0.187 m at 0.25 s, 0.571 m at 0.5 s, 0.964 m at 1.0 s and 1.009 m at 1.5 s. A real robot at 1.6 kg reaches only 1.062 m at 1.5 s, which is outside the 0.05 m success band, so the same policy fails.

The formal definition

Let \xi be the simulator's physical parameters, drawn from a distribution p(\xi). Domain randomisation trains the policy to maximise the expected return across that distribution:

\max_\theta\; \mathbb E_{\xi\sim p(\xi)}\big[J(\pi_\theta;\,\xi)\big]

System identification instead estimates the real parameters from logged data y_{1:T} by least squares:

\hat\xi = \arg\min_{\xi}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\big\lVert y_t - \operatorname{sim}_t(\xi)\big\rVert^2
\xisimulator parameters: here mass m (kg) and damping c (N·s/m)
p(\xi)the distribution the parameters are drawn from during training
J(\pi;\xi)the return of policy π in the world with parameters ξ
y_t, \operatorname{sim}_t(\xi)logged real measurement at time t, and the simulated prediction with parameters ξ
K_p, K_dproportional and derivative gains of the PD controller (N/m and N·s/m)

Lab: many worlds, one controller

Try this
  1. Keep the width at ±0.4 kg and the real mass at 1.0 kg. Most simulated worlds succeed and the real robot succeeds.
  2. Widen the randomisation to ±0.8 kg. Count how many worlds fail. The controller was tuned for one mass, so the extremes are where it breaks.
  3. Set the real mass to 1.6 kg with the width at 0.4 kg. Watch the blue real-robot marker stop short of the green band. The real mass lies outside the simulated range, and this fixed controller fails there.
  4. Set the width to 0 and move the real mass. The policy only works near the mass it was tuned for; randomisation is what buys robustness.
Common traps
  • Randomise the right things. Mass and friction are the obvious choices. Latency, sensor noise, motor saturation and actuator backlash often matter more, and an unrandomised effect is a gap the policy cannot cover.
  • Too much randomisation makes a timid policy. A policy trained across a huge range becomes slow and conservative, because it must be safe in the worst world.
  • Passing in simulation is not evidence of transfer. Hold out a set of real trials, use a hardware safety stop, and report success rates with confidence intervals (Module 8, embodied evaluation).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Legged locomotion policies are commonly trained in simulation with randomised masses, friction, motor strength and terrain, then deployed on hardware.
  • Dexterous manipulation work has used randomised visuals and physics in simulation to transfer grasping and in-hand skills to real hands.
  • Autonomous-driving and drone teams combine simulation, randomised scenes and logged real-world data, because each source alone has a gap.
Pipeline placement. Learn stage (simulator training on GPUs, identification from logged data on the robot) and the deploy stage (the trained policy runs at the robot's control rate, with a safety layer from Module 9). Stores: the simulator configuration and parameter ranges, identification logs, and the policy weights with the parameter ranges they were trained on.

Check yourself

1. In the worked example, what acceleration does the first step give on a 2 kg robot, and why does the controller need no retuning to see that?
a = 6 / 2 = 3.0 m/s². The controller only sees the position and velocity errors, and the force it commands is the same; the robot's response depends on its mass, which the policy does not measure. This is exactly why mass errors show up as behaviour differences.
2. Why is system identification alone not enough, even when it is accurate?
Measured parameters have error, and some effects cannot be captured by a few parameters (contact, cable drag, wear). Randomisation around the identified values covers the residual uncertainty that identification leaves.
3. In the lab, the real robot fails at 1.6 kg while the simulated worlds at ±0.4 kg mostly pass. Why does the lab not simply let you widen the range until the real robot passes, and what would a real team do?
The lab's controller is fixed: it only tests the same controller across worlds, so widening the range shows more failures, not a fix. A real team would retrain the policy with randomisation that covers the real mass (or identify the mass and narrow the range around it), then re-test on hardware with a safety stop. More simulation steps on the same narrow range would not close the gap.

Takeaways

  • The reality gap is the difference between the simulated and real dynamics; it cannot be eliminated, only managed.
  • Domain randomisation trains across a range of simulated worlds; system identification narrows that range using real data.
  • Transfer is an empirical claim: test on hardware, with a safety layer and honest success rates.
7.6

Reward design and safety constraints

The questionYou tell a cleaning robot "+1 for every tile you clean". The robot learns to clean the same tile over and over, or to push dirt back onto clean tiles, because that is what the reward says to do. You meant "clean the floor". How do you write a reward that means what you intend, and how do you keep a robot away from hazards that you did not put into the reward at all?

Intuition

A reward is a message from you to the optimiser, and the optimiser reads it literally. If the message leaves something out, the optimiser is free to ignore it, because it does not know you care. The gap between what you wrote and what you wanted is called reward misspecification, and when an agent exploits it, the behaviour is called reward hacking. Nobody has to be malicious. A proxy that is easy to measure ("tiles cleaned", "distance covered") is usually an imperfect stand-in for the goal ("the floor is clean and nobody got hurt").

Build it up: a proxy, a true reward and a budget

Step 1: write the proxy. Suppose the goal is to reach the goal cell, and every move costs 1. The proxy reward is +20 at the goal and -1 per move. The designer forgot the hazard cells.

Step 2: add what you care about. The true reward subtracts a harm h each time the robot enters a hazard cell. Now the two rewards can disagree, and the question becomes how large h should be.

Step 3: prefer a budget to a penalty. A penalty trades hazards against progress at a rate you must guess. A constraint states the requirement directly: maximise the proxy return, subject to at most d hazard cells. A constrained RL method then finds the best policy that respects the budget, instead of hoping that the penalty happens to be large enough.

Worked example: two routes in a 9 × 5 grid

The fast route is straight across the middle: 8 moves, and it crosses 2 hazard cells. The safe route makes a detour through the bottom row: 12 moves, no hazards. Returns are the goal reward minus move costs, minus h for each hazard:

\text{proxy: fast } 20-8 = 12,\qquad \text{safe } 20-12 = 8\qquad\Rightarrow\text{the proxy prefers fast}
\text{true: fast } 12-2h,\qquad \text{safe } 8 \qquad\Rightarrow\text{fast wins only when } h<2

The routes tie at h = 2. With h = 3 the fast route is worth 12-6 = 6 against 8 for the safe route, so the true objective prefers the detour. A hazard budget of zero removes the fast route entirely and gives the safe route at any h.

The formal definition

Let R be the proxy reward the robot is trained on and C a cost that counts hazard contacts. Constrained RL solves

\max_{\pi}\; J_R(\pi)\quad\text{subject to}\quad J_C(\pi) \le d,\qquad J_X(\pi)=\mathbb E_\pi\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t X(s_t,a_t)\Big]

and, via a Lagrange multiplier \lambda \ge 0, optimises J_R(\pi) - \lambda\,(J_C(\pi)-d), raising λ whenever the constraint is violated.

R, Cproxy reward (what the robot is trained on) and cost (what must stay within a budget)
J_X(\pi)expected discounted total of quantity X under policy π
dthe budget: the largest acceptable expected cost, e.g. "hazard contacts per episode at most 0"
\lambdaLagrange multiplier (non-negative): the price of violating the constraint
hharm per hazard cell in the lab's true reward

Lab: the proxy picks the fast route

Try this
  1. Keep the training reward on "proxy" and the budget at 2. The robot takes the fast route. Its true return, shown in the readout, is 12 − 2h; at h = 3 it is 6, below the safe route's 8.
  2. Switch the training reward to "true". The robot now takes the safe route at h = 3. Find the harm value where the choice flips (h = 2).
  3. Set the budget to 1 and then 0 with the proxy reward. The fast route is removed by the constraint even though the proxy prefers it. Note that the constraint, not the reward, is what changed the behaviour.
  4. Press Step to see one move at a time and note where the robot passes the hazard cells on the fast route.
Common traps
  • Shaping can change the optimum. Adding a bonus for progress can make the robot prefer the bonus to the goal. Potential-based shaping (Ng, Harada and Russell, 1999) adds a difference of a potential function and leaves the optimal policy unchanged; other shaping terms do not.
  • A huge penalty is not a safety guarantee. A very large harm can make learning slow or unstable, and it still only trades off the hazard against other rewards. A hard constraint or a separate safety filter says what must hold.
  • Missing terms are invisible. The robot cannot avoid harms that no term describes. Review what the reward does not count, not only what it does.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Reward hacking is the best-known failure of learned objectives: agents exploit gaps in a reward model or a simulator's scoring, whether a game score or a learned preference model.
  • Constrained policy optimisation is used when the safety requirement is a budget (collisions, torque limits, energy) rather than a preference.
  • Learned robot policies are paired with safety filters that enforce joint and speed limits whatever the policy outputs; this is covered in Module 9 (safe control filters).
Safety honesty. A reward that is well designed in simulation is not a certified safety case. Any learned policy on real hardware needs independent limits, a watchdog and a human able to stop it (Module 9). Do not treat a reward as the only safety mechanism.
Pipeline placement. Learn stage (reward and constraints are specified before training, and reviewed after evaluation); the deploy and monitor stage checks that the reward's measured behaviour matches the intent. Stores: the reward and constraint specification, its version, and the evaluation logs used to find hacks.

Check yourself

1. Under the proxy reward in the worked example, which route is preferred and by how much?
The fast route, by 12 − 8 = 4 return units (12 against 8), because it saves four moves and the proxy ignores hazards.
2. At what harm value h do the two routes tie under the true reward, and what does a budget of zero do?
They tie at h = 2, since 12 − 2h = 8. A budget of zero hazard cells removes the fast route completely, so the safe route is chosen for any h, including h = 0.
3. Why is a constraint often preferred to a very large penalty for hazards?
A constraint states the requirement directly and is checked against the budget, while a penalty only trades the hazard against other rewards at a rate that depends on the penalty's size. A huge penalty can also make the reward landscape hard to learn from, so a constraint plus a safety filter is more transparent.

Takeaways

  • The robot optimises the reward you wrote, not the goal you meant; check for what the reward leaves out.
  • Constraints state safety requirements directly and do not depend on guessing a penalty's size.
  • Reward design is one safety layer among several; hazards must also be handled by filters and monitoring.
