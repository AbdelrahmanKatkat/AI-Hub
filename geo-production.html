Module 9 · Deploying GeoAI responsibly at scale

A model that maps one tile is a demonstration. A map of a whole country, updated every year, is a production system with a budget, a version number, a monitoring plan and a duty of care towards the people whose locations appear in it. This module does the arithmetic, builds the pipeline and shows where it can hurt someone.

9.1

Processing at scale

The questionA ministry wants a 10 m land-cover map of a country of 300,000 km², refreshed every year. How many tiles is that, how many machine-hours and dollars does one pass cost, and what happens to a week of work when one machine crashes at tile 40,000?

Intuition

A country is a giant jigsaw. Each piece, a tile, can be classified without waiting for its neighbours, so if you have many workers you hand each one a piece and the jigsaw finishes many times faster. That is the whole idea of scaling out. Three habits make it work in practice:

  • Split the input into tiles small enough that one worker can hold one tile in memory.
  • Parallelise with one shared queue of tile jobs that many workers pull from. A slow worker simply takes fewer tiles.
  • Checkpoint each finished tile. When a machine dies, only the unfinished work is repeated.

One habit is specific to Earth observation: read only what you need. Thanks to the Cloud-Optimised GeoTIFF layout from Module 1, a worker asks the archive for the bytes of its own window and nothing else. The country is never copied onto one machine. The rest of this lesson turns those habits into numbers.

Build it up

Step 1: how many tiles? Start from the pixel. At 10 m, one pixel covers 10 m × 10 m = 100 m² = 0.0001 km². A tile of 256 × 256 pixels is therefore 2.56 km on a side, which is 2.56^2 = 6.5536\ \text{km}^2. Each square kilometre holds 1/6.5536 = 0.15259 such tiles.

Worked example: the country with no overlap

Area A = 300{,}000\ \text{km}^2. Tiles, if packed perfectly: 300{,}000 / 6.5536 = 45{,}776.37, rounded up to 45,777 tiles. This is a lower bound: real countries have ragged borders, and edge tiles are only partly full.

Overlap. Models usually see some context around the tile border, so neighbouring tiles overlap. Take a 26-pixel overlap (10% of 256). Each tile then owns a stride of 256 − 26 = 230 pixels, which is 2.30 km, so it owns 2.30^2 = 5.29\ \text{km}^2. The count becomes 300{,}000/5.29 = 56{,}710.8, rounded up to 56,711 tiles. This is the number the rest of the lesson costs.

A bounding box is what you actually cut from. For a rectangle of 700 km × 550 km (a country that fills only part of its box), the same 10% overlap gives 305 \times 240 = 73{,}200 tiles. Roughly a quarter of those lie in the sea or across the border and are skipped by a country mask. The gap between the lower bound (56,711) and the box count (73,200) is exactly why real estimates always add an allowance for edges.

Step 2: what does one pass cost? Cost is always the same kind of sum: how much machine time, how much storage, how many requests, plus the people. Write the assumptions down first; they are the part you will argue about, and the part you can change.

  1. Throughput. One worker processes 1 tile per second, including reads, inference and writes (illustrative).
  2. Price. One worker costs $0.90 per hour (illustrative, for a small GPU or a fat CPU instance).
  3. Passes. Three full passes: a development run, one re-run after fixing a bug, and the production run.
  4. Output. Two bands of 8-bit values at 10 m (class and confidence), stored as Cloud-Optimised GeoTIFF with DEFLATE compression at an assumed 3:1 ratio, plus 33% extra for overviews (Lesson 9.3). Storage costs $0.023 per GB-month (illustrative).
  5. Requests. Six archive requests per tile (one per date), at $0.40 per million requests (illustrative). Data inside the same region has no egress charge (assumed).
  6. Quality control. An analyst reviews 1% of tiles, 3 minutes each, at $45 per hour (illustrative).
  7. Not included: engineering time, cloud credits, retries from outages, and maintenance.
Worked example: total cost for one year

Compute. Machine time per pass: 56{,}711\ \text{tiles} \div 1\ \text{tile/s} = 56{,}711\ \text{s} = 15.753\ \text{worker-hours}. Cost per pass: 15.753 \times \$0.90 = \$14.18. Three passes: 3 \times 14.18 = \$42.53.

Wall-clock time. With 20 workers in parallel: 56{,}711 / 20 = 2{,}836\ \text{s} \approx 47\ \text{min}. The same work on one worker takes 15.75 hours. Parallelism shortens the clock; it does not change the machine-hours.

Storage. Pixels: 300{,}000\ \text{km}^2 \times 10{,}000\ \text{px per km}^2 = 3.0\times10^9 px. Raw bytes at 2 bands of 1 byte: 6.0 GB. After 3:1 compression: 2.0 GB. With overviews (+33%): 2.66 GB. Monthly cost 2.66 \times 0.023 = \$0.061, so about $0.73 per year.

Requests. 56{,}711 \times 6 = 340{,}266 requests at $0.40 per million: $0.14.

Quality control. 1% of 56,711 is 568 tiles. At 3 minutes each that is 568 \times 3 = 1{,}704\ \text{min} = 28.4\ \text{h}, costing 28.4 \times 45 = \$1{,}278.00.

Total. 42.53 + 0.73 + 0.14 + 1{,}278.00 = \$1{,}321.40 for the year. Per square kilometre: 1{,}321.40 / 300{,}000 = \$0.0044, about 0.44 cents per km². Compute alone is 14.18 / 300{,}000 = \$0.0000473 per km², roughly 0.005 cents.

The lesson hidden in the numbers. Quality control is 96.7% of the bill. Machine time is cheap. Reviewing tiles by a person is expensive. A pipeline that spends its engineering effort on GPU speed while leaving the review process manual is optimising the wrong thing.

Step 3: checkpoints. A job of 56,711 tiles over 47 minutes will sometimes fail: a spot instance is reclaimed, a read times out, a bug surfaces at tile 40,000. A checkpoint is a record, written durably, that says which tiles are finished. The rule is simple: write each output file before you mark its tile as done, and on restart skip every tile already marked. Without checkpoints, a crash at tile 40,000 repeats 40,000 tiles: 40{,}000 / 20\ \text{workers} = 2{,}000\ \text{s} \approx 33\ \text{min} of wall time and 40{,}000/3600 \times \$0.90 = \$10.00 of machine time. With a checkpoint every 1,000 tiles, the most you can lose is one interval: 1,000 tiles, or 50 seconds of wall time across 20 workers.

Scheduling has one more subtlety. If you cut the work into n_c equal chunks and give them to w workers, the wall-clock time is \lceil n_c / w\rceil rounds of one chunk each. Workers that finish early wait idle, but they are still billed if you pay for the instance rather than the task. That gap is the reason the lab below shows two costs.

Where it shows up in AI
  • Product families at continental and global scale such as ESA WorldCover (10 m) and Dynamic World are produced by tiled, parallel processing on cloud infrastructure, not on a laptop.
  • Pipeline view. This lesson sits across the stages Acquire (range reads from the archive), Preprocess (tiling), Inference (GPU batches per tile) and Publish (writing the mosaic). Each worker stores its tile output in the chip store, so a restart can see what exists.

Check yourself

1. A rectangle of 200 km × 150 km at 10 m is cut into 256-pixel tiles with no overlap. How many tiles, and why is that more than area ÷ 6.5536?
200 km = 20,000 px, and 20,000 / 256 = 78.125, so 79 tiles across. 150 km = 15,000 px, and 15,000 / 256 = 58.6, so 59 down. That gives 79 × 59 = 4,661 tiles. Area ÷ 6.5536 is 4,577.6, so the rectangle needs about 83 more: the edge tiles are only partly full, but they still cost a full tile.
2. Fifty-six thousand seven hundred eleven tiles, 1 tile per second per worker, 20 workers at $0.90 per worker-hour. How long does one pass take in wall-clock time, and what does it cost in compute?
56,711 / 20 = 2,835.6 s ≈ 47.3 minutes. Machine time is 56,711 / 3,600 = 15.753 worker-hours, costing $14.18. The same compute on one worker would take 15.75 hours at the same total price. Parallelism changes the clock, not the bill.
3. You checkpoint every 500 tiles and the job crashes at tile 40,000 with 20 workers. What is the most work you can lose, and how long does it take to redo?
At most one interval of 500 tiles is redone. Across 20 workers that is 500 / 20 = 25 seconds of wall time. Without the checkpoint you would redo 40,000 tiles, about 33 minutes at 20 workers and $10.00 of machine time.

Takeaways

  • A country becomes a count of tiles: N=\lceil A/((P-O)g)^2\rceil. Overlap and edges add tiles, and tiles are what you pay for.
  • Machine-hours are conserved by parallelism; the wall-clock time shrinks. Idle billed workers at the end make the real bill higher than the ideal one.
  • Checkpoint each tile, read only the bytes you need, and count quality control in the budget: for a 10 m national product it dominates.

Common traps
  • Cheap per tile is not cheap per country. A compute cost of $0.00025 per tile, multiplied by 56,711 tiles, is an answer, but it omits three passes, the review of 568 tiles and the storage of the product for years.
  • Forgetting the overlap. Ten per cent overlap adds about 24% more tiles than the bare area count (56,711 vs 45,777). Estimates that leave it out are low by that much.
  • Idle workers are billed. Paying for an instance is paying for its idle minutes too. Shut workers down when the queue is empty, or size the pool so the last round is full.
  • More workers may hit a shared limit. If every worker reads the same bucket, request limits or bandwidth can flatten the speed-up long before the cost curve bends.

9.2

Workflow tools: openEO, Dask & serverless

The questionYour three-line NDVI script runs in two seconds on one tile on your laptop. Run on a whole country, it would try to load about 300 GB into memory and crash. How can the same recipe run over the whole country, without anyone writing a loop over tiles and without loading everything at once?

Intuition

Think of the difference between a recipe and a meal. A recipe lists the steps: take the red band, take the near-infrared band, subtract, divide, average. Nobody has cooked anything yet. A lazy workflow works the same way: when you write ndvi = (nir - red) / (nir + red), nothing is computed. The library records a graph of tasks, and it cooks only when you ask for the final number. Asking is the one moment of cost.

The second idea is the jigsaw from Lesson 9.1, now inside a single array. A large dataset is stored and processed as a grid of chunks. An operation runs chunk by chunk. At any moment the machine holds only a few chunks, not the whole country. This is what makes a 300 GB problem fit in a few gigabytes of memory.

Two tools put these ideas into practice. Dask is a Python library that builds these chunked task graphs and runs them on one machine or a cluster. openEO is a standard for describing the same kind of workflow as a list of processes (load a collection, mask, compute an index, reduce over time, save) that is sent to a back-end running next to the data archive. You describe what; the back-end decides how and where. Serverless functions take a third route: one small function per tile, triggered by a queue and paid per invocation, which suits bursty work such as an emergency.

Build it up: a three-chunk example you can compute by hand

Take a tiny raster: two rows and six columns, with a red and a near-infrared band. Cut it into three chunks of two columns each. A cloud (or a bad pixel) is any pixel with red above 0.27. Here are the values and the NDVI of each pixel, with \text{NDVI}=\frac{\text{NIR}-\text{Red}}{\text{NIR}+\text{Red}} computed for every pixel.

Worked example: the mean, done in chunks

Step 1, each chunk is reduced to two numbers, a sum and a count. Chunk A has four valid pixels: S_A = 0.6667+0.5862+0.6508+0.6610 = 2.5647, n_A = 4. Chunk B has two cloudy pixels, so only column 4 counts: S_B = 0.7460+0.7846 = 1.5306, n_B = 2. Chunk C: S_C = 0.6842+0.2308+0.6491+0.3333 = 1.8974, n_C = 4.

Step 2, the wrong way. Average the averages: (0.6412 + 0.7653 + 0.4744)/3 = 0.6270. Each chunk counts once, whatever its size, so chunk B's two pixels weigh as much as chunk A's four. This is wrong whenever chunks hold different numbers of valid pixels.

Step 3, the right way. Add the sums and the counts first, then divide: \bar{x}=\dfrac{S_A+S_B+S_C}{n_A+n_B+n_C}=\dfrac{5.9927}{10}=0.59928.

Step 4, check it. Ignoring the mask (using all twelve pixels) gives 0.5113: the two cloudy pixels have low NDVI, so leaving them in pulls the answer down. A lazy workflow gives the same answer as an eager one. What differs is how much memory is used on the way.

This is why reductions in chunked systems are written as partial sums that combine: each chunk emits (S_k, n_k), and a final task adds them. Dask's nanmean and openEO's reducers do exactly this internally.

What the code looks like. The sketch below uses Dask on NumPy arrays that are already in memory. The real archive version would use the same operations on arrays opened lazily from a COG or Zarr store; the lab shows the same graph with the three chunks.

import numpy as np
import dask.array as da

red = da.from_array(red_np, chunks=(256, 256))   # lazy: nothing is read yet
nir = da.from_array(nir_np, chunks=(256, 256))
ndvi = (nir - red) / (nir + red)                 # still lazy: only a graph of tasks
ndvi = da.where(red > 0.27, np.nan, ndvi)        # cloud mask, also lazy
mean = da.nanmean(ndvi).compute()                # the only line that does the work

An openEO workflow has the same shape, but the processes are named by the standard rather than by a library. The sketch below follows the openEO Python client; check the exact process names and parameters with your back-end, because they vary by service.

cube = connection.load_collection("SENTINEL2_L2A", bands=["B04", "B08", "SCL"],
                                  spatial_extent=country_bbox, temporal_extent=["2026-06-01", "2026-08-31"])
cube = cube.mask(scl_is_cloud)          # cloud mask from the scene classification band
ndvi = cube.ndvi(nir="B08", red="B04")  # index computed per pixel, per date
summer = ndvi.reduce_dimension(dimension="t", reducer="mean")
job = summer.save_result(format="GTiff")  # the back-end runs it where the data lives

The formal definition

\bar x=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}S_k}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_k},\qquad S_k=\sum_{p\in\text{chunk }k,\ \text{valid}}x_p,\qquad n_k=\#\{p\in\text{chunk }k:\text{valid}\}
Knumber of chunks (3 in the example)
xpthe NDVI value of pixel p
validpixels that pass the mask (not cloud, not no-data)
Sk, nkthe partial sum and count of chunk k: the only numbers that move between tasks

A lazy graph is a directed acyclic graph G whose nodes are operations and whose edges carry chunks. Calling compute() asks the scheduler to evaluate the nodes needed for the requested output, in an order that respects the edges, and to free each intermediate chunk as soon as its consumers have used it. Those two rules, demand-driven and free after use, set the memory peak.

Where it shows up in AI
  • Pangeo-style stacks (xarray with Dask) run large climate and satellite reductions this way on cluster schedulers.
  • openEO back-ends next to the Copernicus archives let a workflow written once run over a national extent on the provider's infrastructure, so only the result is downloaded.
  • Pipeline view. Acquire (lazy range reads), Preprocess (mask, normalise), Inference (per-tile model calls inside the graph) and Post-process (reduce) are one graph, executed by the scheduler. The publish step writes the result as a COG.

Check yourself

1. For the 2×6 raster above, what is the mean NDVI, and what is the mean of the chunk means? Which one is right, and why?
Correct: (2.5647 + 1.5306 + 1.8974) / 10 = 0.59928. The mean of chunk means is 0.6270, which is wrong because chunk B has two valid pixels and chunks A and C have four: the average of averages gives each chunk equal weight, not each pixel.
2. The lazy run peaks at one chunk held in memory, while the eager run peaks at twelve. Why?
Lazy evaluation runs each chunk through read, mask, NDVI and reduce before starting the next chunk, and frees each stage's output once the next stage has used it. Eager evaluation finishes every chunk's read before any mask, every mask before any NDVI, and so on, so the outputs of all three chunks at all four stages are held together: 3 × 4 = 12 chunk outputs at the peak.
3. Why is a serverless function per tile a poor fit for a model with a 40 GB weight file?
Each invocation must start, load the weights and fit them in memory. Loading 40 GB per tile dominates the time and cost, and many functions cannot hold that much memory at once. A long-running worker that loads the model once and then processes a stream of tiles amortises the load, and serverless is better suited to small models or to pre-processing.

Takeaways

  • Lazy means the recipe is recorded first and cooked only when the result is requested; the cost moves to one visible moment.
  • Chunked reductions combine partial sums and counts. Averaging averages is wrong when the counts differ.
  • Run the workflow where the data lives. Choose Dask for control on your own cluster, openEO when a back-end holds the archive, and serverless for bursty, light tasks.

Lab: lazy graph and chunks

Try this
  1. Press Compute result in lazy mode and watch one chunk travel through read, mask, NDVI and reduce before the next chunk starts. Note the peak: one chunk held.
  2. Switch to eager and compute again. Each stage now runs for all chunks first, and the held count climbs to twelve.
  3. Check the answer: it is 0.59928 in both modes. Only the memory differs.
  4. Drag the cloud threshold down to 0.22 and watch the mask remove more pixels. Notice the answer change, and why the mean of chunk means moves differently from the correct mean.
Common traps
  • Averaging averages. The mean of chunk means is wrong whenever chunks have different numbers of valid pixels. Carry sums and counts.
  • Calling compute inside a loop. Each call runs the whole graph again. Build the graph once, then compute once.
  • Chunks that do not match the file's tiles. If a 300-pixel chunk straddles 512-pixel COG blocks, every read touches four blocks. Align chunk sizes with the internal tiling.
  • Lazy errors arrive late. A bad band name fails at compute time, perhaps after an hour of work. Validate the graph on one tile before the national run.

9.3

Publishing products as COG & STAC

The questionYou have 56,711 tiles of a national class map and its confidence map. A correction ships next year. How do users find the product, read a small part of it without downloading 2.66 GB, and cite exactly the version they used?

Intuition

Think of a published product as a library edition. It has four parts, each solving one problem. The binding and index (the Cloud-Optimised GeoTIFF, COG) let a reader open page 412 without carrying the whole book. The catalogue card (a STAC Item) says what the item is, where it lies, when it was made and where its files are. The shelf (a STAC Collection) groups the cards of one product. The edition number (a version) and the citation (a DOI and a description) let someone refer to exactly the thing they used, years later.

The point of the design is that a machine can answer "give me the 2026 class values inside this 2 km box" by reading a few kilobytes of catalogue and then a few blocks of the file, never the whole country.

Build it up

Step 1: the COG checklist. A COG is an ordinary GeoTIFF arranged so that a reader needs only the header and the blocks it wants. Four properties make that work, and each has a value to set:

PropertyValue to useWhy it matters
Internal tiling512 × 512 px blocksA window read fetches whole blocks; 512 balances request count and waste
Overviews×2, ×4, ×8, ×16, ×32A map zoomed out reads a small overview, not the full-resolution file
CompressionDEFLATE or ZSTD (lossless)Class maps must not be altered by lossy compression
Data type and no-datauint8, no-data 2550 may be a real class (water); 255 must never be a valid value
CRS and geotransformwritten inside the file (EPSG:32637 here)A file with no CRS is a file nobody can align

Tools such as GDAL and rio-cogeo can write these properties and validate them, which belongs in the publish step rather than in someone's memory.

Worked example: the overview pyramid for the 700 × 550 km mosaic

The national mosaic at 10 m is 70{,}000 \times 55{,}000 pixels. Each overview halves both sides, so the pixel count falls by four:

The extra storage is the sum of the shares, a geometric series: \frac14+\frac1{16}+\frac1{64}+\cdots=\frac{1/4}{1-1/4}=\frac13, about 33%. This is the factor used in Lesson 9.1, which turned 2.0 GB into 2.66 GB.

Step 2: the STAC Item, one per tile. A STAC Item is a GeoJSON Feature with extra rules. Its bbox and geometry are in longitude and latitude (WGS84), as GeoJSON requires, even though the pixels are in UTM. Its properties hold the time and the grid description, and its assets list the files with their media types and roles. The example below is for one 256-pixel tile in UTM zone 37N (EPSG:32637), with its upper-left corner at easting 470,000 m and northing 1,000,000 m. Read the example field by field. datetime is null because the product is a year-long composite, so the interval fields start_datetime and end_datetime carry the time instead; STAC allows this. gsd is the ground sample distance in metres. The proj: fields come from the projection extension and tell a reader how the pixel grid sits in its CRS: proj:transform is the affine transform [10, 0, 470000, 0, -10, 1000000], meaning 10 m pixels, no rotation, and the origin at the upper-left corner. The version lives in the item id and the file paths, so a v1.2.0 item and a v1.2.1 item can coexist.

Step 3: collections, versions and citation. A collection holds the items of one product and carries the product-level facts: extent, licence, providers and a description. Versions follow semantic versioning, MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH, with rules written down in advance:

Check yourself

1. A COG with full size 35,000 × 27,500 px has overviews ×2, ×4 and ×8. Give their sizes, and the total extra storage they add (as a share of the full file).
×2: 17,500 × 13,750; ×4: 8,750 × 6,875; ×8: 4,375 × 3,438 (3,437.5 rounded up). Total: 1/4 + 1/16 + 1/64 = 21/64 ≈ 32.8% extra.
2. A reviewer finds that 14 tiles of v1.2.0 were built from a bad input scene. Which version bump is right, and what happens to the other 56,697 tiles?
A PATCH bump: 1.2.0 becomes 1.2.1, because the classes and method are unchanged. Only the 14 affected tiles are re-processed and get new items and files. The other 56,697 items are untouched, and v1.2.0 stays available so earlier citations still resolve.
3. A class map uses 0 for "water" and you declare 0 as the no-data value. What goes wrong?
Every water pixel is treated as missing: masks remove them, area statistics skip them and a user's water fraction becomes wrong. The no-data value must be a value that never occurs in the data. Here that is 255, and 0 stays a valid class.

Takeaways

  • A COG (tiling, overviews, correct no-data) lets users read a window without the whole file; overviews cost about one third extra.
  • A STAC Item is a GeoJSON feature in WGS84 with the grid described by the projection extension; collections group items and carry the licence.
  • Version with rules written in advance, keep old versions reachable, and cite a fixed version and a DOI.

Common traps
  • Overwriting a published file. Users who cited a URL now get different numbers. Publish new versions to new paths and keep the old ones.
  • A DOI that points at "latest". Citations then change under the reader. A DOI should resolve to one version.
  • No overviews. A national COG without overviews looks fine on your laptop and crawls in a web map.
  • Missing licence. A product without a stated licence is legally unusable for many users, however open it looks. Name the licence, for example CC-BY-4.0, and check its attribution rules.

9.4

Monitoring GeoAI systems

The questionYour model mapped crop fields at 90% accuracy when it was validated in 2024. In 2026 there is a drought, and the satellite processing has been upgraded. How would you know the map has got worse, how many reference points would prove it, and what should trigger a new check?

Intuition

A map is a snapshot of a changing planet. Three things change under it. The world changes: a drought makes fields greener in some weeks and browner in others, and crops are planted on different dates. The sensors and processing change: a new calibration or a new surface-reflectance baseline shifts every pixel slightly. And the place changes: a model trained in one region meets fields, soils and farming practices it never saw. Each is called drift, and each needs a different check.

Geography adds two traps. First, the same field looks different in June and in September, so a check must compare like with like: this season against the same season in earlier years, at the same stage of growth. Second, neighbouring pixels are not independent, so a sample of 200 pixels from one farm tells you much less than 200 pixels spread over the country. Monitoring is a sampling problem as much as a statistics one.

Build it up

Step 1: a distribution check, by hand. Suppose the mid-season NDVI of crop pixels in 2019–2024 has mean \mu_{\text{ref}}=0.62 and standard deviation \sigma_{\text{ref}}=0.08 (illustrative). In 2026 the season mean is 0.55. Measure the shift in units of the reference spread, not in raw NDVI, because the same 0.07 means something different when the spread is 0.02 and when it is 0.20:

Worked example: the 2026 season

z=\dfrac{\mu_{2026}-\mu_{\text{ref}}}{\sigma_{\text{ref}}}=\dfrac{0.55-0.62}{0.08}=-0.875. With a rule "alarm when |z| is greater than τ = 0.5", that is an alarm. The 2025 season had mean 0.585, so z=-0.4375: below the threshold, no alarm, but a warning sign.

A check on one season only is noisy. Use the whole timeline: the reference years wobble by about ±0.004 around 0.62, so a single season that is 0.06 away is a real signal while 0.005 is noise.

Step 2: how many reference points? Monitoring also needs ground truth, and ground truth costs money. Re-validation means an analyst labels randomly chosen reference points and compares them with the map. With a measured accuracy a and n points, the 95% interval for the true accuracy is a \pm 1.96\sqrt{a(1-a)/n}.

Worked example: is 86% a real drop from 90%?

Measure a=0.86 with n=200 points. The half-width is 1.96\sqrt{0.86\cdot0.14/200}=0.048, so the interval is [0.812,\,0.908]. It contains 0.90, so the drop is not yet conclusive: the map may be exactly as good as before.

How many points would settle it? We need the upper end below 0.90: 0.86+1.96\sqrt{0.1204/n}<0.90, which gives n\ge290. At 600 points the interval is [0.832, 0.888]: a clear drop. At 5 minutes per labelled point and $45 per hour, 200 points cost 200 \times 5/60 \times 45 = \$750, and 600 points cost $2,250. The sample size is therefore a budget decision that you should make before the season, not after the alarm.

Step 3: sensor drift is small per pixel and big in aggregate. A processing change that scales near-infrared reflectance by 3% changes a single pixel only a little. Take a pixel with red 0.10 and near-infrared 0.50: NDVI goes from (0.50-0.10)/(0.50+0.10)=0.66667 to (0.515-0.10)/(0.515+0.10)=0.67480, a shift of 0.008, about a tenth of σ. Nobody sees that in one pixel. But the standard error of a mean over 10,000 pixels is 0.08/\sqrt{10{,}000}=0.0008, so the shift is about ten standard errors. Monitoring the distribution over many pixels finds what single-pixel checks cannot.

Step 4: a re-validation schedule. Put the checks on a calendar, with a size and a trigger for each. Cadence should follow the cost of being wrong, not the convenience of the team.

Where it shows up in AI
  • Operational crop monitoring and food-security systems compare each season's index distributions with reference years, then re-label samples when a change appears.
  • Sensor changes such as a new processing baseline are routinely handled by re-running the reference period, so the z-scores stay comparable.
  • Pipeline view. This is the monitor half of the Publish and monitor stage: it reads the products from Lesson 9.3 and decides when to re-validate and re-publish.

Check yourself

1. The 2026 season mean is 0.575, the reference is 0.62 with σ = 0.08, and the threshold is τ = 0.5. Is it an alarm?
z = (0.575 − 0.62)/0.08 = −0.5625. Its absolute value is above 0.5, so yes, an alarm. The 2025 mean of 0.585 gave z = −0.4375, which was below it.
2. A map measured at 86% accuracy on 200 points, against a reference of 90%. Is the drop conclusive? How many points would be needed to show it?
Not conclusive. The interval is 0.86 ± 0.048, i.e. [0.812, 0.908], which contains 0.90. Solving 0.86 + 1.96√(0.1204/n) < 0.90 gives n ≥ 290 points.
3. A processing change scales NIR by 3%. A single pixel with red 0.10 and NIR 0.50 moves from NDVI 0.66667 to 0.67480. Why is that still worth monitoring?
The shift per pixel is 0.008, about a tenth of σ, so single pixels hide it. Across 10,000 pixels the standard error of the mean is 0.0008, so the shift is about ten standard errors: the distribution-level check detects it reliably.

Takeaways

  • Drift has three sources: the world, the sensors and processing, and the place. Each needs its own check.
  • Compare like with like: the same season, at the same growth stage, measured in the reference standard deviation.
  • Accuracy needs a sample size chosen in advance; a small sample cannot tell a 4-point drop from noise.

Common traps
  • Comparing different seasons. A June mean against a September reference reports the calendar, not the drought.
  • Watching only accuracy. Accuracy needs labels, which arrive late. Distribution checks give an early warning on the cheap.
  • Testing with pixels from one farm. Spatial autocorrelation makes such a sample look far more certain than it is.
  • Retraining silently. A new model or a new baseline changes the product. Log it as a release with its own accuracy, not as a quiet update.

9.5

Privacy, ethics & responsible use

The questionA health NGO wants to publish a map of where eight households reported a disease case, and a flood-risk study wants to publish home locations. The coordinates are precise to a few centimetres. How much should they be blurred, what do they still reveal after blurring, and who else should be asked before anything is published?

Intuition

A location is almost an identifier. A home coordinate, together with a time or a pattern of visits, can single out one household as surely as a name. The map does not need to show a name to reveal a person: if only one house in a village has a particular value, the house is the person. Privacy in geospatial work is therefore a question of how much the coordinate can be narrowed down, and how many other people share the same narrowed location.

Precision is a dial. A coordinate in decimal degrees has a fixed number of decimal places, and each place removes a factor of ten from the precision. Near Addis Ababa, one degree of latitude is about 111 km, and each decimal place divides that size by ten, so four decimal places are about 11 m, a single building, while one decimal place is about 11 km, a whole neighbourhood or more. Rounding moves the point to the centre of a grid cell. The question becomes: does the cell hold enough other records to hide this one?

Build it up

Step 1: the precision ladder. At 9° N, one degree of latitude is 111.32 km and one degree of longitude is 109.94 km. Each decimal place divides these by ten. Using the cell sizes in metres:

Step 2: group and count. Take eight invented coordinates in one city (they are not real sites, and the lesson uses them only as numbers). Round each to d decimal places, count the records that share a rounded location, and call a record identifiable if fewer than k records share its cell. Here k = 3, a common teaching choice for "a person is hidden among at least three others".

Worked example: eight records, four precisions

Rounding the eight coordinates gives these group sizes (the cell counts, largest first):

Read the table carefully. At four decimal places the records are spread over six cells and all eight are identifiable. At three places, five records merge into one safe cell, but three isolated records remain alone. At two places the pair of records falls below k too, and the single record still stands by itself. Rounding to one place changes nothing for them: the one record in the north-west corner stays by itself until the grid is 111 km wide. Rounding protects the crowd and leaves the loner exposed.

So a publication rule needs more than a precision. It needs a grouping rule (every published cell has at least k records), and a treatment for the cells that fail it: suppress the record, merge it into a neighbouring cell, or publish only the aggregate count. Using k in this way is an informal version of the k-anonymity idea from the data-privacy literature: each released record must be indistinguishable from at least k − 1 others on its quasi-identifiers. For a location, the quasi-identifier is the cell.

Step 3: the questions that come before the numbers. Coordinates are only one part of responsible use. Five questions should be answered in writing before publication:

  1. Who could be harmed? Clinics, shelters, households in conflict or disaster, sites of cultural value that attract looting, and nests or dens of threatened species that attract poaching are all sensitive.
  2. Could the map be used against people? Maps of infrastructure, of vulnerable settlements or of who lives where can serve targeting as well as planning. This is dual use, and it is a question for the team, not only for the lawyers.
  3. Who has rights over the land or the data? Customary and Indigenous land claims, and data about communities, may require the consent of the communities themselves. The CARE principles for Indigenous data governance (collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, ethics) are a useful checklist.
  4. What licence applies? CC-BY-4.0 requires attribution; CC-BY-SA requires derivatives to share alike; the ODbL, used for OpenStreetMap-derived data, carries share-alike obligations on databases; CC0 puts no conditions on reuse. Imagery licences are often separate and may forbid redistribution of raw tiles.
  5. What is the release plan? Coarse grids by default, exact points only to vetted users under a data-use agreement, and a delay for sensitive species or sites are common answers.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Humanitarian and health mapping often publishes aggregated grids while keeping exact locations inside the organisation.
  • Biodiversity platforms commonly coarsen or delay the locations of sensitive species, so that people cannot use the record to find the animal.
  • Pipeline view. The privacy rules sit in the Publish and monitor stage, applied to the products of Lesson 9.3 before they reach the open archive, and they are reviewed each time the product is versioned.

Check yourself

1. At 3 decimal places, how many of the eight records are identifiable with k = 3, and which ones?
Three. The group of five shares one cell and is safe; the three singletons (each alone in its cell) are identifiable.
2. The team says that rounding to 1 decimal place (about 11 km) solves the problem. Is that right?
No. At one decimal place the groups are 5, 2 and 1, so three records remain in cells smaller than 3. The isolated record stays alone until the cell is about 111 km wide. Rounding alone cannot protect outliers; suppression or merging is needed.
3. A conservation team holds exact nest locations of an endangered bird. Give a sensible release plan.
Publish an aggregated grid at a coarse resolution (for example 1 km cells, with the k rule applied), delay the release until the breeding season has passed, share exact points only with vetted researchers under a data-use agreement, and state the licence and the reason for coarsening in the product's description.

Takeaways

  • A coordinate can identify a person; each decimal place of rounding divides the precision by ten.
  • Count the crowd: a published cell needs at least k records, and loners need suppression or merging, not just rounding.
  • Before the numbers, answer who could be harmed, how the map could be misused, whose consent is needed, under which licence, and at what resolution it is released.

Common traps
  • "We removed the names." Names are not the only identifiers. A place, a time and a rare attribute can identify a person together.
  • Rounding as the whole defence. It protects groups and leaves outliers exposed. Apply a group-size rule.
  • Forgetting the derived products. A heat map, a density surface or a model's output can reveal the same locations as the points they came from.
  • Licences as an afterthought. Share-alike and attribution terms bind every later user. Decide them before the first release.
Honest limit. A group-size rule lowers the risk of identification but does not remove it. Determined attackers can combine a published map with other datasets. Treat k as one control among several, and review each release for the sensitive cases above.
9.6

Applications & case studies

The questionFive teams come to you with five projects: a farm cooperative wants crop maps through the season, a flood agency wants extents within hours of a storm, a city wants building footprints, a climate group wants forty years of change, and a conservation charity wants weekly deforestation alerts. The same six-stage pipeline serves all five. Which parts of it must each project get right, and which can be cheap?

Intuition

Every GeoAI project is the same pipeline with different pressure points. Imagine a surgeon's kit: the same instruments serve many operations, but a heart operation stresses different tools from a cataract operation. For each application there is one stage that decides success. For a disaster it is how fast data can be fetched and processed. For a climate record it is whether the sensors agree with each other over decades. For conservation it is whether the published result keeps sensitive places safe. Choosing a project means naming that stage before you design anything.

A useful habit is to write three sentences per project: what decision the product supports, how fresh it must be, and what happens if it is wrong. Those three answers set the latency budget, the monitoring plan and the privacy rules, and they are cheap to write before any model is trained.

Step 2: a worked latency budget. Disaster teams ask one question first: how many workers do we need to finish inside the time we have? The answer is a ceiling division. Suppose a storm floods 10,000 km², which is \lceil 10{,}000/6.5536\rceil = 1{,}526 tiles of 256 pixels (no overlap, for simplicity). The budget is 10 minutes, or 600 s.

Worked example: workers for a ten-minute flood map

If an optical tile takes 1 s per worker: \lceil 1{,}526 \times 1 / 600\rceil = \lceil 2.54\rceil = 3 workers.

If a SAR tile with its speckle filtering takes 4 s (an illustrative figure): \lceil 1{,}526 \times 4 / 600\rceil = \lceil 10.17\rceil = 11 workers.

The arithmetic is small. The hours are lost elsewhere: finding the scenes, waiting for the pre-event baseline, and having an analyst check the result. A budget that counts only compute misses the stages that usually fail.

Step 3: resolution changes everything. Urban work often uses 0.5 m imagery, twenty times finer than 10 m in each direction. Count the pixels. One square kilometre at 10 m is 1{,}000/10 \times 1{,}000/10 = 10{,}000 pixels; at 0.5 m it is 2{,}000 \times 2{,}000 = 4{,}000{,}000 pixels, 400 times as many. A 256-pixel tile at 0.5 m covers 128 m, so a square kilometre holds 1{,}000/128 \times 1{,}000/128 = 61.0 such tiles, against 0.15 at 10 m. Every cost in Lesson 9.1 scales with this ratio, so a city project and a national project are not the same budget even when they cover the same area.

Takeaways

  • Every GeoAI project uses the same six stages; each application puts its weight on one or two of them.
  • Write the decision, the freshness and the cost of error before design. They set the latency budget, monitoring and privacy rules.
  • Count the workers and the pixels before you promise a deadline: resolution multiplies every cost in the chain.
