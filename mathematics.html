Level 1

Why math & the pipeline

Training a model is a loop of four mathematical moves, each from a different field: linear algebra computes predictions, probability defines the loss, calculus finds the gradient and optimization updates the weights. The pipeline below shows where each one lives.

Key takeaways

  • Predict → measure error → compute gradient → update. Repeat.
  • You never need to do this by hand, but knowing each piece tells you why training fails and how to fix it.
Level 2

Linear algebra: vectors & matrices

Data are vectors; a layer of a neural network is a matrix. Multiplying by a matrix moves every point in space: rotating, stretching, shearing or squashing it. The columns of the matrix are where the basis vectors \hat\imath,\hat\jmath land.

\mathbf y = W\mathbf x + \mathbf b \qquad \begin{pmatrix}y_1\\y_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{pmatrix}

Type your own numbers or pick a preset. The determinant is how much area scales; when it hits zero the matrix squashes the plane onto a line and information is lost. Eigenvectors (yellow) are the directions that are only stretched, never turned.

Key takeaways

  • A matrix = a linear map; its columns show where the axes go.
  • det = 0 means irreversible (rank-deficient); this is why redundant features hurt.
  • Eigenvectors power PCA and explain how repeated multiplication behaves.
Level 3

Derivatives & gradients

The derivative is the slope of the best straight-line approximation. It is the limit of secant slopes as the step shrinks:

f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \qquad \nabla f=\Big(\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\dots,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\Big)

Type any f(x) (try abs(x) to see a corner with no derivative) and press Shrink h → 0: the orange secant rotates onto the red tangent. The green dashed curve is f'(x) computed by automatic differentiation — the same machinery that trains neural networks.

Key takeaways

  • Slope tells you which way is uphill; its negative is downhill.
  • The gradient is the vector of slopes in every parameter direction.
  • Frameworks compute exact derivatives automatically; numeric differences are only for checking.
Level 4

Optimization

Gradient descent repeatedly steps against the gradient. Modern optimizers add memory of past gradients:

\text{SGD: } \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta g \qquad \text{Momentum: } v\leftarrow\beta v+g,\; \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta v
\text{Adam: } m\leftarrow\beta_1m+(1-\beta_1)g,\; s\leftarrow\beta_2s+(1-\beta_2)g^2,\; \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat s}+\epsilon}

Pick Valley: plain SGD zig-zags across the narrow canyon while Momentum and Adam glide along it. On Ripples different optimizers land in different local minima. On Saddle the gradient vanishes at the centre but it is not a minimum. Edit f(x, y) to build your own landscape, and click the map to move the start.

Key takeaways

  • Learning rate is the single most important setting: too high diverges, too low stalls.
  • Momentum smooths noise and speeds up along consistent directions.
  • Adam adapts the step per parameter, which is why it is a strong default.
Level 5

Chain rule & backpropagation

A model is a composition of simple functions, so its derivative is a product of local derivatives (the chain rule). Backpropagation evaluates that product from the output backwards, reusing every intermediate value from the forward pass.

\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\qquad\text{(one local rule per node)}

Enter any expression. The blue values flow forward; the orange gradients flow back. Check the readout: backprop agrees with finite differences (✓). When a variable appears twice, its gradients add.

Key takeaways

  • Backward cost is about the same as forward cost, regardless of how many parameters there are.
  • This is why activations must be stored during training, and why training needs more memory than inference.
Level 5

Probability & statistics

Models express uncertainty as probabilities, and losses come from probability: minimising cross-entropy is exactly maximising the likelihood of the labels.

\mathcal L=-\frac1N\sum_i\Big[y_i\log\hat p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\Big]

Averages of many independent draws look Gaussian whatever the source distribution — the central limit theorem: \bar X_n\approx\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2/n). The lab builds samples from raw uniform noise using a formula you can edit (inverse-transform sampling). Increase n and the skew and kurtosis fall toward zero. Try the Cauchy preset: it has no finite mean, so averaging never helps.

Key takeaways

  • Loss functions are negative log-likelihoods in disguise.
  • Gaussians appear everywhere because of averaging, but heavy tails break the rule.
  • Evaluation metrics are estimates: always ask how much noise is in the number.
