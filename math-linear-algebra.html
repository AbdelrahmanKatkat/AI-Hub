Module 1 · Linear algebra

Linear algebra is the study of lists of numbers (vectors), tables of numbers (matrices) and the simple geometric moves they perform. A neural network is, at heart, a long chain of these moves. By the end you will see a matrix as a machine that reshapes space, understand why A\mathbf x=\mathbf b sometimes has no solution, and know where PCA, LoRA and linear regression come from.

Vectors

The questionA music app describes every song by three numbers: how energetic it is, how acoustic it is, how happy it sounds. Song A is (0.9, 0.1, 0.8), song B is (0.8, 0.2, 0.7). Can we do arithmetic on songs — "take song A, but make it twice as energetic", or "the average of my three favourites" — and can we say which new songs are reachable by mixing a few known ones?

Intuition

A vector has two faces, and you need both.

  • The arrow face. A vector is an arrow starting at the origin: it has a direction and a length. The vector (3,2) is the arrow that goes 3 steps right and 2 steps up. Arrows are good for seeing what is going on.
  • The list face. A vector is an ordered list of numbers: (3,2), or (0.9,0.1,0.8). Lists are good for computing, and they work in 3, 100 or 12 000 dimensions where no arrow can be drawn.

These are the same thing. The tip of the arrow sits at the point whose coordinates are the list (this is exactly the "point in ℝⁿ" from Module 0). Whenever you meet a vector in 500 dimensions, quietly imagine the 2-D arrow: every rule you learn here is checked in 2-D and then simply carries on in more.

There are only two operations on vectors, and the whole of linear algebra is built from them:

  1. Adding two vectors: walk along the first arrow, then walk along the second one starting from where you ended up (tip to tail). The result is the arrow from the start to the end.
  2. Scaling a vector by a number: stretch it (number bigger than 1), shrink it (between 0 and 1), or flip it to point the other way (negative number).

Build it up

In coordinates both operations act entry by entry — there is no hidden trick. With \mathbf a=(2,1) and \mathbf b=(-1,3):

Worked example

Add: \mathbf a+\mathbf b=(2+(-1),\,1+3)=(1,4). Walk 2 right and 1 up, then 1 left and 3 up: you end 1 right and 4 up. ✓

Scale: 3\mathbf a=(6,3) (three times as long, same direction); -\mathbf b=(1,-3) (same length, opposite direction).

Combine: 2\mathbf a-\mathbf b=(4,2)-(-1,3)=(5,-1).

Now the idea that makes vectors powerful. Scale each vector by a number of your choice and add the results. This is a linear combination:

c_1\mathbf a+c_2\mathbf b

The numbers c_1,c_2 are the coefficients — think of them as knobs on a mixing desk: \mathbf a and \mathbf b are two ingredients, the knobs say how much of each to pour in. Turning the knobs makes the result slide around. The question that matters: which results can we reach? The set of every vector you can make this way is called the span of \mathbf a and \mathbf b.

What does a span look like?

  • One non-zero vector \mathbf a alone: all multiples c\mathbf a — a line through the origin.
  • Two vectors in the plane pointing in different directions: the whole plane. Any target can be reached; there is a unique pair of knob settings for it.
  • Two vectors pointing along the same line (one is a multiple of the other, e.g. (2,1) and (4,2)): still only a line. The second ingredient adds nothing new — it is redundant. We say the vectors are linearly dependent. Vectors that are not redundant in this sense are linearly independent.
Worked example — reaching a target

Can we reach (1,11) from \mathbf a=(2,1) and \mathbf b=(-1,3)? We need c_1(2,1)+c_2(-1,3)=(1,11), i.e. two equations: 2c_1-c_2=1 and c_1+3c_2=11. From the first, c_2=2c_1-1; into the second: c_1+6c_1-3=11\Rightarrow c_1=2, so c_2=3. Check: 2(2,1)+3(-1,3)=(4-3,\,2+9)=(1,11) ✓. Two independent vectors in the plane reach every target — this one needed 2 of a and 3 of b.

The formal definition

\mathbf v=\begin{pmatrix}v_1\\v_2\\\vdots\\v_n\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb R^n,\qquad \mathbf a+\mathbf b=\begin{pmatrix}a_1+b_1\\\vdots\\a_n+b_n\end{pmatrix},\qquad c\,\mathbf a=\begin{pmatrix}c\,a_1\\\vdots\\c\,a_n\end{pmatrix}
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf a_1,\dots,\mathbf a_k\}=\{\,c_1\mathbf a_1+\dots+c_k\mathbf a_k\;:\;c_1,\dots,c_k\in\mathbb R\,\}
𝐯 ∈ ℝⁿa vector with n entries (bold lower-case letter by convention); v_i is its i-th entry. Written as a column when we multiply by matrices later.
𝐚 + 𝐛add entry by entry; both vectors must have the same length
c𝐚multiply every entry by the single number c (a scalar)
c₁𝐚₁ + … + ck𝐚ka linear combination of the vectors with coefficients c_i
span{…}the set of all linear combinations — everything reachable

Independence, precisely: vectors \mathbf a_1,\dots,\mathbf a_k are linearly independent if the only way to get the zero vector, c_1\mathbf a_1+\dots+c_k\mathbf a_k=\mathbf 0, is to set every c_i=0. (If some non-zero choice worked, you could solve for one vector in terms of the others — it would be redundant.) In \mathbb R^n you can never have more than n independent vectors. A set of n independent vectors in \mathbb R^n spans everything and is called a basis; the standard one is \mathbf e_1=(1,0,\dots),\ \mathbf e_2=(0,1,\dots), so that (3,2)=3\mathbf e_1+2\mathbf e_2 — the list of numbers is just the list of coefficients in the standard recipe.

Common traps
  • A vector is not a location and an arrow at once — it is whichever helps. The point (3,2) and the arrow to it are two pictures of one object. A vector does not "live" somewhere: the arrow from (1,1) to (4,3) is the same vector as the one from the origin to (3,2).
  • You cannot add vectors of different lengths. (1,2)+(1,2,3) is undefined — in code, NumPy may "broadcast" it silently, which hides bugs.
  • "Span" is not "the vectors themselves". It is everything you can build from them. Two vectors can span a plane even if neither lies in it "obviously".
  • Two vectors can be different and still redundant ((2,1) and (4,2)). Independence is about directions, not about being unequal.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Feature vectors. One house, one patient, one customer row is a vector; a model's whole job is to map such vectors to predictions.
  • Embeddings. A word, image or user becomes a vector in ℝᵈ (d = 768…12 288). Famous demonstration: \mathbf v_{\text{king}}-\mathbf v_{\text{man}}+\mathbf v_{\text{woman}}\approx\mathbf v_{\text{queen}} — arithmetic on meaning, which only makes sense because vectors can be added and scaled.
  • Mixing features. Every hidden unit of a neural network outputs a linear combination of its inputs; the weights are the coefficients. "What can this layer express?" is a span question.
  • Redundancy. Two highly correlated features are almost linearly dependent — one reason models can become unstable (lesson 1.7).

Check yourself

1. Compute 3(1,2) − 2(0,1).
3(1,2) = (3,6); 2(0,1) = (0,2); the difference is (3,4).
2. Is (3,3) in the span of (1,2) and (2,4)? What is that span?
No. (2,4) = 2·(1,2), so the two vectors are dependent and span only the line y = 2x. The point (3,3) is not on it (3 ≠ 6).
3. What is the span of e₁ = (1,0,0) and e₂ = (0,1,0) inside ℝ³?
All vectors (c₁, c₂, 0): the flat x–y plane (third coordinate 0). Two vectors can only ever span a plane (or less) — they can never fill all of ℝ³, which needs three independent vectors.

Takeaways

  • A vector is an arrow and a list of numbers at once; both operations (add, scale) work entry by entry.
  • A linear combination c₁a + c₂b is "mix the ingredients with these amounts"; the span is everything reachable.
  • Redundant (dependent) vectors add no new directions. n independent vectors in ℝⁿ reach everything.

Dot product & angle

The questionYou have two documents, each turned into a long vector of word counts. How do you measure how alike they are with a single number? And, in a neural network, how does one neuron combine many inputs into one "score" that says how strongly the input matches what the neuron is looking for?

Intuition

Imagine pushing a shopping trolley along a straight rail. You push with some force at some angle. Only the part of your push that points along the rail makes the trolley move; a sideways push is wasted on the rail. The dot product measures exactly this: "how much do these two arrows point the same way, weighted by their lengths?"

  • Arrows pointing roughly the same way: big positive number.
  • Arrows at right angles (perpendicular): zero — neither helps the other.
  • Arrows pointing roughly opposite ways: negative.

The surprising and beautiful fact is that there are two completely different ways to compute this number — one a trivially simple multiply-and-add, the other pure geometry — and they always agree.

Build it up

Recipe 1 — the algebraic one. Multiply the vectors entry by entry and add up:

\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+\dots+a_nb_n=\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i
Worked example

\mathbf a=(2,1),\ \mathbf b=(1,3): \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=2\cdot1+1\cdot3=5. And (3,4)\cdot(4,-3)=12-12=0 — these two arrows are perpendicular (draw them!).

Notice the answer is a single number, not a vector.

Recipe 2 — the geometric one. Let \lVert\mathbf a\rVert be the length of a (Pythagoras: \sqrt{a_1^2+a_2^2+\dots}, the distance formula from Module 0) and \theta the angle between the two arrows. Then

\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf b\rVert\cos\theta

Why would these agree? Think of projecting a onto the line through b: drop a perpendicular from the tip of a onto that line. The foot of the perpendicular lies at a signed distance \lVert\mathbf a\rVert\cos\theta along b's direction (basic right-triangle trigonometry). Multiply by the length of b and you get the formula. The algebraic recipe computes the very same number — you can check it in the lab below. (A short proof: expand the law of cosines for the triangle with sides a, b, a − b.)

Worked example — angle from entries

For \mathbf a=(2,1),\mathbf b=(1,3): \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=5, \lVert\mathbf a\rVert=\sqrt5\approx2.236, \lVert\mathbf b\rVert=\sqrt{10}\approx3.162. Then \cos\theta=\dfrac{5}{\sqrt5\sqrt{10}}=\dfrac5{\sqrt{50}}\approx0.7071, so \theta=45^\circ.

Projection: the part of a that lies along b is the vector \dfrac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b}\,\mathbf b=\dfrac{5}{10}(1,3)=(0.5,\,1.5). The leftover \mathbf a-(0.5,1.5)=(1.5,-0.5) is perpendicular to b: (1.5)(1)+(-0.5)(3)=0 ✓.

Reading the sign: since lengths are positive, the sign of \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b is the sign of \cos\theta. Positive means an angle below 90°, zero means exactly 90° (orthogonal, the general word for perpendicular — and it works in any dimension), negative means above 90°.

Cosine similarity. Dividing out the lengths leaves a pure "direction agreement" score between −1 and +1:

\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf b\rVert}

Because it ignores length, a short document and a long document on the same topic can still score close to 1. That is why this, not the raw dot product, is the standard "how alike?" measure for text and embeddings.

A neuron is a dot product. A single artificial neuron takes inputs \mathbf x, owns a weight vector \mathbf w and a bias b, and computes z=\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x+b, then squashes it (for example with the sigmoid). By the geometric formula, \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x=\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\cos\theta: the neuron responds most strongly to inputs pointing in the direction of w. The weights are a template; the neuron measures how well the input matches it.

Worked example — a neuron

\mathbf w=(0.5,-1,2), \mathbf x=(4,2,1), b=0.5. Products: 0.5\cdot4=2, -1\cdot2=-2, 2\cdot1=2; sum = 2; add the bias: z=2.5. Sigmoid: \sigma(2.5)=1/(1+e^{-2.5})\approx0.924 — a confident "yes, this matches".

The formal definition

\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ib_i=\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\lVert\mathbf b\rVert\cos\theta,\qquad \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a=\lVert\mathbf a\rVert^2,\qquad \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf b}\mathbf a=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf b}\,\mathbf b
𝐚 · 𝐛the dot (inner) product: a single number. Also written \langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle or \mathbf a^{\!\top}\mathbf b (the last form comes in lesson 1.4)
‖𝐚‖the length (norm) of a, \sqrt{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a}; the dot of a vector with itself is its squared length
θthe angle between the two arrows, 0° to 180°
cos θ+1 same direction, 0 perpendicular, −1 opposite
projb athe vector shadow of a on the line through b; defined when \mathbf b\ne\mathbf 0

Orthogonal means \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=0. Properties worth knowing: \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf a; \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b+\mathbf c)=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b+\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf c; (c\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf b=c(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b). The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality |\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b|\le\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\lVert\mathbf b\rVert is just the statement that |cos θ| ≤ 1.

Common traps
  • The dot product is not another vector. It collapses two vectors into one number. (The "vector times vector → vector" product is a different thing, the cross product, which rarely matters in ML.)
  • Large dot product ≠ similar direction. (100,0)\cdot(1,0)=100 and (1,0)\cdot(1,0)=1 have the same angle (0°). Length inflates the raw dot product — normalise (cosine) if you want pure direction.
  • Zero dot product does not mean one vector is zero — it means perpendicular. (Of course the zero vector also gives 0.)
  • Angles in high dimensions. In ℝ¹⁰⁰⁰ random vectors are almost always nearly perpendicular (cos θ ≈ 0). "Nearly orthogonal" is the normal state of affairs, so a score like 0.3 can already be significant.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Neuron pre-activation: z=\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x+b — every dense layer computes many of these in parallel.
  • Attention scores in transformers: the similarity of a query and a key is \mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k/\sqrt d; a large positive dot product means "this token should attend to that one".
  • Cosine similarity for semantic search, retrieval-augmented generation, recommendation and clustering of embeddings.
  • Linear classifiers / logistic regression: the decision is the sign of \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x+b, the hyperplane from Module 0.

Check yourself

1. Compute (1,2,3)·(4,−5,6).
1·4 + 2·(−5) + 3·6 = 4 − 10 + 18 = 12.
2. Are (2,−1) and (1,2) orthogonal?
Dot product = 2·1 + (−1)·2 = 0, so yes: they are perpendicular.
3. Find the cosine similarity between (1,1) and (3,3), and between (1,0) and (−2,0). Compare with their dot products (2·3 = 6 … and −2).
(1,1)·(3,3) = 6, lengths √2 and √18, so cos θ = 6/(√2·√18) = 6/6 = 1 (same direction). (1,0)·(−2,0) = −2, lengths 1 and 2, cos θ = −2/2 = −1 (opposite). The dot product of the first pair is 6 only because (3,3) is long; the cosine ignores length.

Takeaways

  • a·b = Σaᵢbᵢ = |a||b|cos θ: multiply-and-add equals length × length × alignment.
  • Sign tells the angle: positive < 90°, zero = orthogonal, negative > 90°.
  • Cosine similarity removes length and keeps direction.
  • A neuron is a dot product with a template w; attention scores are dot products too.

Norms & distances

The questionA model's error on three test examples is the vector (3, −4, 0). Is that "big"? We need a single number for the size of a vector — but is "size" the straight-line length, the total of all the misses, or just the worst single miss? Different answers lead to different learning behaviour. Which should we choose, and why does a model penalised by one of them end up with lots of exact zeros in its weights?

Intuition

"How big is this vector?" has several sensible answers, depending on how you travel. Picture a city built on a grid, and you want to get from the origin to the point (3, −4).

  • A helicopter flies in a straight line: length \sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5. This is the L2 norm, the everyday Euclidean length.
  • A taxi must follow the grid streets: 3 blocks across plus 4 blocks down = 7. This is the L1 norm ("Manhattan" or taxicab length).
  • A delivery limit says "no leg longer than the longest single move": the biggest coordinate magnitude, 4. This is the L∞ norm ("max norm" or Chebyshev).

A norm is any rule that assigns a size to vectors and behaves sensibly: sizes are never negative, only the zero vector has size 0, scaling a vector by c scales its size by |c|, and the triangle inequality holds (a detour is never shorter than the direct route: \lVert\mathbf a+\mathbf b\rVert\le\lVert\mathbf a\rVert+\lVert\mathbf b\rVert). All three examples above obey these rules.

To see how they differ, draw the unit ball: all points whose norm is exactly 1. For L2 it is a circle. For L1 it is a diamond (a tilted square whose corners sit on the axes). For L∞ it is an axis-aligned square. The shape is the personality of the norm.

Build it up

\lVert\mathbf x\rVert_1=\sum_i|x_i|,\qquad \lVert\mathbf x\rVert_2=\sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2},\qquad \lVert\mathbf x\rVert_\infty=\max_i|x_i|

These are three members of one family, the p-norms \lVert\mathbf x\rVert_p=\left(\sum_i|x_i|^p\right)^{1/p} for p\ge1. Put p=1 or p=2 and you recover the first two; let p\to\infty and the biggest coordinate dominates everything, giving L∞. (For p<1 the triangle inequality breaks and the formula is no longer a true norm.) A good habit: always \lVert\mathbf x\rVert_\infty\le\lVert\mathbf x\rVert_2\le\lVert\mathbf x\rVert_1.

Worked example

\mathbf v=(3,-4): L1 = 3 + 4 = 7; L2 = √(9+16) = 5; L∞ = max(3, 4) = 4. Indeed 4 ≤ 5 ≤ 7. ✓

Unit vector. Divide a non-zero vector by its length to get a vector of length 1 pointing the same way: \hat{\mathbf v}=\mathbf v/\lVert\mathbf v\rVert_2=(3,-4)/5=(0.6,-0.8). Check: 0.36+0.64=1 ✓. This is "normalising" and keeps only direction.

Distance is simply the size of the difference: d(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=\lVert\mathbf a-\mathbf b\rVert. With the L2 norm it is the Euclidean distance of Module 0.

Worked example — one pair, three distances

\mathbf a=(1,2,3), \mathbf b=(4,6,3); difference (-3,-4,0). Euclidean: √(9+16+0) = 5. Manhattan: 3+4+0 = 7. Chebyshev: 4.

Similarity versus distance. They are opposite viewpoints. A distance is small when things are alike; a similarity is large. Cosine similarity compares directions, Euclidean distance compares positions. They disagree when length differs: the vectors (1,0) and (10,0) are 9 apart in Euclidean distance, yet have cosine similarity 1. Which is "right" depends on whether magnitude is meaningful (a document's length usually is not; a measured quantity usually is). If all vectors are first normalised to length 1, the two coincide: \lVert\mathbf a-\mathbf b\rVert_2^2=2-2\cos\theta.

Why the shape matters for learning. Suppose a model can reach the same prediction with weights (1,0,0) (one feature does all the work) or (\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13) (shared equally). Both give \mathbf w\cdot(1,1,1)=1. The L1 norms are 1 and 1 — a tie, so L1 does not mind concentrating the weight. The squared L2 norms are 1 and 1/3 — L2 strongly prefers spreading the weight out. Pushing weights toward the corners of the diamond (L1) makes some weights land on exactly 0, while the round L2 ball has no corners, so weights shrink but rarely vanish.

Common traps
  • "The norm" usually means L2, but always check the subscript. In a paper, \lVert\cdot\rVert with no subscript almost always means L2; \lVert\cdot\rVert_F is the Frobenius norm of a matrix (square root of the sum of squares of all entries).
  • Squared L2 is not a norm (it breaks the triangle inequality) but is used everywhere because it is smooth and easy to differentiate: \lVert\mathbf x\rVert_2^2=\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf x.
  • Unscaled features let one big-valued coordinate dominate any distance. Standardise features first (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation).
  • Normalising the zero vector divides by zero. Libraries add a tiny ε to the denominator for this reason.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Regularisation: ridge adds \lambda\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2^2 to the loss (weight decay in deep learning) and shrinks weights; lasso adds \lambda\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_1 and drives many weights to exactly zero (sparse models).
  • Losses are norms: mean squared error is a squared L2 norm of the error vector; mean absolute error is an L1 norm; L∞ appears in adversarial robustness ("change each pixel by at most ε").
  • k-nearest neighbours, k-means and retrieval all depend on the choice of distance.
  • Normalisation layers (LayerNorm, RMSNorm) rescale vectors by their L2 length; embeddings are often L2-normalised so that dot product = cosine similarity; gradient clipping caps the L2 norm of the gradient.

Check yourself

1. Compute the L1, L2 and L∞ norms of (1, −2, 2).
L1 = 1+2+2 = 5; L2 = √(1+4+4) = 3; L∞ = 2. Indeed 2 ≤ 3 ≤ 5.
2. Normalise (1, −2, 2) to a unit vector.
Divide by its L2 length 3: (1/3, −2/3, 2/3). Check: 1/9 + 4/9 + 4/9 = 1.
3. Why does the diamond shape of the L1 ball encourage sparse weights, while the circle of the L2 ball does not?
The diamond has sharp corners lying on the coordinate axes. When a loss contour first touches the L1 ball it is likely to touch at a corner, where some coordinates are exactly 0. The circle has no corners and the touching point is generically off-axis, so all weights are small but non-zero.

Takeaways

  • A norm measures size: L1 = total, L2 = straight line, L∞ = largest coordinate; always L∞ ≤ L2 ≤ L1.
  • Distance = norm of the difference; normalising divides by the length.
  • The unit ball's shape (diamond, circle, square) explains why L1 gives sparsity and L2 gives shrinkage.

Matrices

The questionYou have 4 houses, each described by 3 numbers (rooms, area, age). A price model uses one weight per feature. How do you store the whole dataset as one object, and compute the predicted price for all four houses at once instead of writing a loop? And what does it mean to "flip" the table?

Intuition

A matrix is a rectangular table of numbers. That is all it is, at first. A spreadsheet is a matrix. A grey-scale photo is a matrix (one number per pixel). Where a vector is one list, a matrix is a stack of lists — and which way you slice the stack gives you two useful viewpoints:

  • As a stack of rows: each row is one example (one house). Reading down the table, you meet example after example.
  • As a row of columns: each column is one feature (all the areas, all the ages). Reading across, you meet feature after feature.

Keeping both views in your head is the single most useful habit in linear algebra. Later we will find a third view: a matrix as a machine that moves vectors (lessons 1.5 and 1.6).

Build it up

The shape of a matrix with m rows and n columns is written m\times n ("m by n", rows first, always). The entry in row i, column j is X_{ij} (or x_{ij}). Here is our data matrix — 4 houses, 3 features, so 4\times3:

X=\begin{pmatrix}3&120&8\\2&85&15\\4&150&3\\3&100&10\end{pmatrix}\qquad\text{columns: rooms, area (m}^2\text{), age (years)}

Row 2 is house 2: (2,85,15). Column 3 is "the ages of all houses": (8,15,3,10). The entry X_{23}=15 is "the age of house 2" — row index first, column index second.

Transpose. Flip the table over its diagonal: the first row becomes the first column, and so on. The result is written X^{\top} and its entries satisfy (X^\top)_{ji}=X_{ij}. A 4\times3 matrix becomes 3\times4. Transposing twice gives back the original. Transposing a column vector gives a row vector; this is why the dot product can be written \mathbf a^{\top}\mathbf b — a one-row matrix times a one-column matrix.

Matrix–vector product, first look. Put one weight per feature in a vector \mathbf w=(10,\,1,\,-2) (toy numbers: a price contribution per room, per m², per year of age). The predicted price of house 1 is the dot product of its row with w: 3\cdot10+120\cdot1+8\cdot(-2)=134. The wonderful thing: stacking all four dot products gives X\mathbf w, a vector of four predictions.

Worked example — all four predictions at once

House 1: 30+120-16=134. House 2: 20+85-30=75. House 3: 40+150-6=184. House 4: 30+100-20=110.

So X\mathbf w=(134,\,75,\,184,\,110): a 4\times3 times a length-3 vector gives a length-4 vector. The inner sizes (3 and 3) matched and disappeared; the outer sizes survive. We will make this the main theme of the next lesson.

Special matrices worth naming. A square matrix has m = n. The identity I is square with 1s on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere — it changes nothing (I\mathbf x=\mathbf x). A diagonal matrix has non-zero entries only on the diagonal. A symmetric matrix equals its own transpose (A=A^\top; it mirrors over the diagonal) — these are very well-behaved, and will matter a lot in lessons 1.9–1.10. A matrix can be added to another of the same shape (entry by entry) and scaled by a number, just like a vector.

Common traps
  • Rows first, columns second. A 3×5 matrix has 3 rows and 5 columns, and a_{23} is row 2, column 3. Mixing this up is the most common source of shape errors.
  • Conventions differ. Math textbooks often treat a data point as a column; most ML libraries (NumPy, PyTorch, scikit-learn) store examples as rows: X.shape == (n_samples, n_features). Check which convention a paper or library uses before multiplying.

1.5

Matrix multiplication

The questionA neural network layer turns 3 input numbers into 2 output numbers using 6 weights. A second layer then turns those 2 numbers into 4. We want one rule for combining tables of numbers that handles a single example, a batch of 1000, and a chain of layers alike. What rule makes the shapes line up — and why does the order of multiplication matter?

Intuition

Matrix multiplication looks like an arbitrary recipe the first time you see it — "multiply rows by columns" — and many people memorise it without ever understanding why it is defined that way. It is defined that way because it does three jobs at once, and the recipe is the one that makes all three consistent. Learn the three views and the recipe stops being mysterious:

  1. The dot-product view — every entry of the answer is one dot product of a row of the left matrix with a column of the right one. (Good for computing by hand.)
  2. The column view — each column of the answer is a linear combination of the columns of the left matrix, with the right matrix's column telling you the amounts. (Good for understanding what the product spans.)
  3. The composition view — multiplying matrices means doing one transformation after the other: (AB)\mathbf x=A(B\mathbf x). (Good for understanding what it means; made visual in the next lesson.)

Build it up

Start small: matrix times vector. From lesson 1.4, X\mathbf w was "dot each row with w". The very same product also has a second reading: if A has columns \mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,\mathbf a_3 then

A\mathbf x=x_1\mathbf a_1+x_2\mathbf a_2+x_3\mathbf a_3

— the entries of x are the knob settings, and A\mathbf x is the mix of the columns of A. This is the linear combination from lesson 1.1, now with the ingredients packed into a matrix. Everything about "what outputs can A\mathbf x produce?" is a span question about the columns of A.

Now matrix times matrix. Treat B as several column vectors side by side; multiply A by each of them separately and place the results side by side. The shapes must agree: the number of columns of A must equal the number of rows of B (the "inner" sizes match, because each dot product pairs up a row of A with a column of B entry by entry). The outer sizes give the result.

\underbrace{A}_{m\times k}\;\underbrace{B}_{k\times n}=\underbrace{C}_{m\times n}
Worked example

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\4&5&6\end{pmatrix} (2×3), B=\begin{pmatrix}7&8\\9&10\\11&12\end{pmatrix} (3×2). Inner sizes 3 and 3 match; the result is 2×2.

Dot-product view. C_{11}=1\cdot7+2\cdot9+3\cdot11=7+18+33=58; C_{12}=1\cdot8+2\cdot10+3\cdot12=64; C_{21}=4\cdot7+5\cdot9+6\cdot11=139; C_{22}=4\cdot8+5\cdot10+6\cdot12=154. So C=\begin{pmatrix}58&64\\139&154\end{pmatrix}.

Column view. Column 1 of C = 7·(column 1 of A) + 9·(column 2 of A) + 11·(column 3 of A) = 7(1,4)+9(2,5)+11(3,6)=(7+18+33,\;28+45+66)=(58,139) ✓ — the same numbers, found a different way.

Row view. Row 1 of C = 1·(row 1 of B) + 2·(row 2 of B) + 3·(row 3 of B) = (7,8)+2(9,10)+3(11,12)=(58,64) ✓. (Rows of the answer are combinations of the rows of B.)

Order matters. In ordinary arithmetic 3·5 = 5·3. Not here. Often BA is not even defined: a 3×2 times a 2×3 is fine, but if the shapes were 2×3 and 2×3 you could not multiply them in either order. Even for square matrices, AB\ne BA in general:

Worked example — non-commutativity

P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\ Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}. Then PQ=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} but QP=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}. Different! With the composition view this is no surprise: "put on socks, then shoes" is not "put on shoes, then socks".

What does hold? Associativity (AB)C=A(BC) (so we may drop brackets), distributivity A(B+C)=AB+AC, the identity AI=IA=A, and the transpose rule (AB)^\top=B^\top A^\top (order reverses — you can check it from the formula: entry (i,j) of (AB)^\top is (AB)_{ji}=\sum_kA_{jk}B_{ki}=\sum_k(B^\top)_{ik}(A^\top)_{kj}).

Cost. Each of the m·n entries needs k multiplications and additions, so the product costs about m\cdot n\cdot k multiply–adds. A dense layer applied to a batch of 32 inputs with 4096 inputs and 4096 outputs is a (32\times4096)(4096\times4096) product: 32\cdot4096\cdot4096=536{,}870{,}912 ≈ 0.54 billion multiply–adds — for one layer, one batch. This is why GPUs, which are essentially matrix-multiplication machines, drive modern AI. Associativity also affects cost: for 1000×1000 matrices A, B and a vector x, computing (AB)\mathbf x costs about 10^9 operations while A(B\mathbf x) costs about 2\times10^6 — same answer, 500 times cheaper if you multiply the vector first.

The formal definition

C=AB,\qquad C_{ij}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}A_{il}\,B_{lj}\qquad(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times k},\;B\in\mathbb R^{k\times n},\;C\in\mathbb R^{m\times n})
Cijentry in row i, column j of the product
Σl=1..kadd over the shared (inner) index l — the index that "disappears"
Ailrow i of A, running across its k columns
Bljcolumn j of B, running down its k rows
m × k · k × ninner sizes must be equal; outer sizes give the shape of C

Equivalent statements: C_{:,j}=A\,B_{:,j} (column j of C is A times column j of B) and (AB)\mathbf x=A(B\mathbf x) for every vector x of the right length.

Common traps
  • Not commutative. AB\ne BA in general; BA may not even exist. Always write products in the order the data flows.
  • The entry-wise product is a different thing. A * B in NumPy multiplies matching entries (Hadamard product, written A\odot B); the matrix product is A @ B. Mixing them is a silent bug whenever shapes happen to allow both.
  • No cancellation. From AB=AC you cannot conclude B=C; and AB=0 does not force A=0 or B=0.
  • Check shapes first. Most dimension errors vanish if you write the shapes under every factor: (32×4096)(4096×1024) → 32×1024.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Every dense / linear layer of an MLP, every projection in a transformer (Q=XW_Q, K=XW_K, V=XW_V) and the attention score matrix QK^\top is a matrix product. Convolutions are implemented as matrix products too.
  • Batching: stacking 32 inputs as the rows of X turns 32 matrix–vector products into one matrix–matrix product — this is what GPUs are fast at; training cost is measured in "FLOPs", mostly matmul FLOPs.
  • Chains of layers W_3W_2W_1\mathbf x are compositions. Without non-linear activations in between they would collapse into the single matrix W_3W_2W_1 (lesson 1.6).
  • Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) replaces a big matrix update by a product of two thin matrices (lesson 1.10); the multiplication order determines the cost, as in our associativity example.

Check yourself

First: [[2,1],[4,3]] — the columns are swapped. Second: [[3,4],[1,2]] — the rows are swapped. The two orders give different results; the swap matrix acts on columns when it stands on the right and on rows when it stands on the left.

m·n·k = 64·256·512 = 8,388,608 (about 8.4 million).

Takeaways

  • C = AB: Cij = row i of A · column j of B; inner sizes must match, outer sizes give the shape.
  • Three views: dot products, combinations of columns, and composition (do B, then A).
  • Not commutative, but associative; the transpose reverses the order. Cost is m·n·k — the workload of every layer.
1.6

Matrices as transformations

The questionRotate a photo, stretch it, shear it like a slanted font: each of these moves every point of the plane. Is there one compact object that describes any such motion? And when a neural network layer multiplies by a weight matrix, what is it actually doing to the space the data lives in?

Intuition

So far a matrix was a table. Now watch it act. A matrix A is a machine: feed in a vector x, out comes the vector A\mathbf x. Do this for every point of the plane at once and the whole plane gets moved: rotated, stretched, flipped, sheared, squashed. Imagine the plane printed on a sheet of rubber with a square grid drawn on it. The matrix grabs the sheet and deforms it — but only in a very well-behaved way:

  • the origin stays put;
  • straight lines stay straight (no bending);
  • grid lines stay parallel and evenly spaced (the grid becomes a slanted, stretched grid of parallelograms).

Any motion with these properties is called a linear transformation. The marvellous fact: such a motion is completely determined by what it does to just two arrows — the basis vectors \hat\imath=(1,0) and \hat\jmath=(0,1). Because the grid stays a regular grid, knowing where one step right and one step up land tells you where everything lands.

Build it up

Here is the key. Any vector is a combination of the basis vectors: (x,y)=x\hat\imath+y\hat\jmath. A linear transformation respects combinations: T(x\hat\imath+y\hat\jmath)=x\,T(\hat\imath)+y\,T(\hat\jmath). So if the arrow \hat\imath lands on (a,c) and \hat\jmath lands on (b,d), then the image of (x,y) is x(a,c)+y(b,d). Put those two landing spots as the columns of a matrix and this is exactly the matrix–vector product from the last lesson:

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix},\qquad A\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=x\begin{pmatrix}a\\c\end{pmatrix}+y\begin{pmatrix}b\\d\end{pmatrix}

The columns of A are where the basis vectors land. To read a matrix, look at its columns; to design a transformation, decide where î and ĵ should go and write those as columns. A gallery of the classics (try each in the lab):

Worked example

Rotate (2,1) by 90°: \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}=2\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}+1\begin{pmatrix}-1\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\end{pmatrix}. Sanity check: the arrow (2,1) turned a quarter turn anticlockwise is (−1,2), the same length (√5) ✓.

Shear \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} on (2,1): 2(1,0)+1(1,1)=(3,1): the height stays 1 but the point is pushed right by an amount equal to its height — a slant.

Composition is the matrix product. Do transformation B first, then A. Where does î end up? First B sends it to its first column, then A acts on that. Doing this for both basis vectors builds the matrix of the combined motion — and it is exactly the product AB from lesson 1.5. That is why matrix multiplication is defined the way it is.

Worked example — composition

Rotation R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} first, then shear S=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}. Track î: R sends (1,0) to (0,1); S sends (0,1) to (1,1). Track ĵ: R sends (0,1) to (−1,0); S sends (−1,0) to (−1,0). So the combined matrix has columns (1,1) and (−1,0): SR=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} — matching the direct product. In the other order, RS=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}: a different motion. The order of moves matters.

What is not linear? Shifting every point by (1,0) moves the origin, so it is not linear — it is an affine map: \mathbf x\mapsto A\mathbf x+\mathbf b (this is exactly what a neural layer does: matrix first, then add the bias). Squaring coordinates, or taking ReLU, bends lines, so they are not linear either.

Non-square matrices. A 3×2 matrix takes a plane and embeds it as a flat sheet inside 3-D space; a 2×3 matrix flattens 3-D space onto a plane (loses a dimension). In general an m\times n matrix is a linear map from \mathbb R^n to \mathbb R^m: input dimension = number of columns, output dimension = number of rows. A neural layer "reshapes space" in exactly this sense, changing the number of dimensions while twisting and stretching the data.

The formal definition

T:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\text{ is linear}\iff T(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)=T(\mathbf u)+T(\mathbf v)\ \text{ and }\ T(c\mathbf u)=c\,T(\mathbf u)

Common traps
  • Columns, not rows, are the images of the basis vectors. Reading the rows as "where î goes" gives the transpose — a different transformation.
  • The product reads right-to-left. In AB\mathbf x, B acts first because it is closest to x.
  • Linear ≠ "a line". A function like y=2x+1 is called linear in school but is not a linear map (it moves the origin). In this course "linear" means preserving combinations; "affine" is linear plus a shift.

Where it shows up in AI
  • A dense layer \mathbf h=W\mathbf x+\mathbf b is a linear map followed by a shift; training chooses the columns of W — where each input feature lands in the output space.
  • Why non-linearities exist: composing linear maps gives a linear map, so networks insert ReLU/GELU between layers; each layer then reshapes space linearly and the activation bends it.
  • Rotations (RoPE rotary position embeddings) and orthogonal initialisations use matrices that preserve lengths; data augmentation applies rotations, scalings and shears to images.
  • Embedding spaces: aligning two languages or two models is often done by learning a linear map between their vector spaces.
  • Change of basis: describing the same data in new coordinates (as PCA does, lesson 1.10) is a matrix multiplication.

Takeaways

  • A matrix is a transformation of space; its columns say where the basis vectors land.
  • Linear = origin fixed, lines stay straight, grids stay parallel. Rotation, scaling, shear, reflection and projection are all matrices.
  • Matrix product = composition (right matrix acts first). A layer changes the shape of the data cloud; non-linearities stop layers collapsing into one.
1.7

Determinant & inverse

The questionA matrix transformed your data into new coordinates. Can you get the original back? When can a transformation be undone — and why do regression formulas blow up with nonsense when two of your features are nearly copies of each other?

Intuition

Take the unit square (the square with corners (0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1)) and push it through a matrix. It turns into a parallelogram, spanned by the two column vectors. The determinant is a single number that records by what factor the area changed:

  • determinant 2: every region doubles in area;
  • determinant 0.5: every region halves;
  • determinant 1: area is preserved (rotations, shears);
  • determinant negative: area scales by |det|, and the picture is flipped like a mirror image (orientation reversed);
  • determinant 0: the whole plane is flattened onto a line (or a point). All the area is gone.

Because every region of the plane is just a pile of tiny unit-square-like pieces, and the transformation scales them all by the same factor, the factor for the unit square is the factor for everything. In 3-D the determinant is the volume scaling factor; in n dimensions, the n-volume scaling factor.

Now the inverse. If the transformation did not squash anything flat, you can run the film backwards: every point of the output came from exactly one input, so there is an undo machine, the inverse matrix A^{-1}. But once the plane is flattened onto a line, many different points land on the same spot. Given such a point you cannot tell which one it came from — information has been destroyed, and no machine can restore it. So the inverse exists exactly when the determinant is not zero. A matrix with no inverse is called singular.

Build it up

For a 2×2 matrix with columns (a,c) and (b,d), the parallelogram's signed area is

\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad-bc

(Check with the unit square itself, I: 1\cdot1-0\cdot0=1; with scaling by 2 and 3: 2\cdot3-0=6, area of a 2×3 rectangle ✓; with the shear: 1\cdot1-1\cdot0=1, slanting a square keeps its base and height ✓.)

The 2×2 inverse is obtained by swapping the diagonal entries, negating the off-diagonal ones, and dividing by the determinant:

A^{-1}=\frac1{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}\qquad(ad-bc\ne0)

Notice the division by ad-bc: that is precisely why a zero determinant has no inverse.

Worked example

A=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\2&4\end{pmatrix}: \det A=3\cdot4-1\cdot2=10, so areas grow tenfold. A^{-1}=\frac1{10}\begin{pmatrix}4&-1\\-2&3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.4&-0.1\\-0.2&0.3\end{pmatrix}.

Check: AA^{-1}: row 1 · col 1 = 3·0.4 + 1·(−0.2) = 1; row 1 · col 2 = 3·(−0.1) + 1·0.3 = 0; row 2 · col 1 = 2·0.4 + 4·(−0.2) = 0; row 2 · col 2 = 2·(−0.1) + 4·0.3 = 1. We get I ✓.

Solving a system. To solve A\mathbf x=\mathbf b with \mathbf b=(5,6), undo A: \mathbf x=A^{-1}\mathbf b=(0.4\cdot5-0.1\cdot6,\;-0.2\cdot5+0.3\cdot6)=(1.4,\,0.8). Check: 3·1.4+0.8 = 5 ✓ and 2·1.4+4·0.8 = 6 ✓.

Singular example. \begin{pmatrix}2&4\\1&2\end{pmatrix} has determinant 2\cdot2-4\cdot1=0. Its columns (2,1) and (4,2) are multiples of each other (linearly dependent, lesson 1.1), so every output lies on the single line through (2,1): the plane has been squashed flat. No inverse.

Rules worth knowing. \det(AB)=\det A\cdot\det B (scaling factors multiply when you compose); \det(A^{-1})=1/\det A; \det(A^\top)=\det A; (AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1} (undo in reverse order: socks and shoes again); the determinant of a diagonal or triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries (e.g. \operatorname{diag}(2,3,4) has det 24: it stretches 3-D volume by 24).

Near-singular: the dangerous case. A determinant that is tiny but not zero means the matrix almost flattens space — and its inverse is enormous. Take A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1.001\end{pmatrix} (det = 0.001, A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1001&-1000\\-1000&1000\end{pmatrix}). Solve A\mathbf x=(2,2): \mathbf x=(2,0). Now nudge the right-hand side by one thousandth, \mathbf b=(2,2.001): the solution jumps to (1,1). A change of 0.001 in the data changed the answer by 1 — a thousandfold amplification. If your measurements have any noise, the "solution" is mostly noise.

This is exactly what happens with collinear features. Suppose two columns of the data matrix X are (almost) copies — say the same length measured in metres and in feet. Then X flattens one direction, and X^\top X is singular or nearly so; the normal equation of regression (lesson 1.11), (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y, requires its inverse. Example: columns (1,2,3) and (2,4,6) give X^\top X=\begin{pmatrix}14&28\\28&56\end{pmatrix} with determinant 14\cdot56-28\cdot28=0 — no inverse; infinitely many weight settings fit equally well.

The formal definition

\det A=\text{(signed) volume scaling factor of }\mathbf x\mapsto A\mathbf x,\qquad A^{-1}A=AA^{-1}=I,\qquad A^{-1}\text{ exists}\iff\det A\ne0
det Afor 2×2, ad-bc; for larger matrices, a sum over products of entries (computed by elimination in practice). Square matrices only.

Common traps
  • Only square matrices have determinants and (ordinary) inverses. A 3×2 matrix cannot be inverted; the closest thing is the pseudo-inverse (lesson 1.10–1.11).
  • A⁻¹ is not 1/A entry by entry. The inverse of \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&4\end{pmatrix} is \operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12,\tfrac14) only because it is diagonal; in general inverting entries gives nonsense.

Where it shows up in AI
  • The normal equation \mathbf w=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y for linear regression needs an invertible X^\top X; collinear features make it singular, which is why we drop duplicate features or add ridge regularisation (X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1} — adding λ to the diagonal pushes the determinant away from zero.
  • Multicollinearity in tabular data: wildly unstable, huge coefficients with opposite signs are the visible symptom of a near-singular matrix.
  • Normalising flows are generative models built from invertible transformations; they track the determinant of the Jacobian because density changes by the volume factor (change of variables).
  • Whitening and Gaussian densities use \Sigma^{-1} and \det\Sigma of covariance matrices; Gaussian processes and Kalman filters invert matrices too.

Takeaways

  • det = signed area/volume scaling; 2×2: ad − bc. Negative = mirror flip; zero = flattened.

1.8

Linear systems & rank

The questionYou have several measurements and some unknown quantities, linked by equations. Sometimes there is exactly one answer, sometimes none (the data contradict each other), sometimes infinitely many (the data are not enough). Can we tell which case we are in before solving — and how many numbers of information does a matrix really hold?

Intuition

A linear system is a set of equations in which each unknown appears only multiplied by a number and added up. For example

\begin{aligned}2x+y&=5\\x-y&=1\end{aligned}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}5\\1\end{pmatrix}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad A\mathbf x=\mathbf b

Module 0 solved this by sliding along the lines. The same system has two pictures, and switching between them is the secret skill of linear algebra:

  • Row picture. Each equation is a line (in 3-D, a plane; in n-D, a hyperplane). A solution is a point lying on all of them: where the lines meet.
  • Column picture. The left side A\mathbf x=x\,\mathbf a_1+y\,\mathbf a_2 is a linear combination of the columns of A. Solving the system means: find the recipe (x, y) that mixes the column arrows into the target arrow b. In the example, 2 of the first column and 1 of the second: 2(2,1)+1(1,-1)=(5,1) ✓.

Both pictures give the same answer, and the column picture is the more powerful one because it immediately tells you when a solution exists: if and only if b lies in the span of the columns.

Build it up

The three possibilities. Two lines in a plane can meet in one point (usually), never meet (parallel), or be the same line (meet everywhere). In the column picture: the columns are independent (they span the plane, b is reachable in exactly one way); the columns lie on one line and b is off that line (unreachable); or the columns lie on one line and b is on it (reachable in infinitely many ways, because the dependent columns can trade amounts). The numbers of solutions are only ever 0, 1, or infinitely many — never exactly 2 or 3.

Worked examples

(a) x+y=2,\ x+y=3: the same left side must equal two different numbers — contradiction, no solution (parallel lines).

(b) x+y=2,\ 2x+2y=4: the second equation is just twice the first — it carries no new information, so one equation for two unknowns: infinitely many solutions (t,\,2-t).

(c) 2x+y=5,\ x-y=1: add them: 3x=6, x = 2, y = 1 — exactly one solution.

Rank: how many genuinely different directions? The rank of a matrix is the number of linearly independent columns — equivalently (a fact worth knowing) the number of independent rows. It is the dimension of the column space, the set of all A\mathbf x. For a 2×2 matrix the rank is 2 if the columns point in different directions, 1 if they lie on one line, 0 only for the zero matrix. For a matrix with n columns:

\operatorname{rank}(A)+\dim\operatorname{null}(A)=n

where the null space is the set of vectors that A sends to zero: \{\mathbf x:A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\}. Every dimension of the input either survives into the output (counted by rank) or is crushed to zero (counted by the null space) — nothing is lost from the books. The null space is also exactly the "wiggle room" in the solutions: if \mathbf x_0 solves A\mathbf x=\mathbf b, so does \mathbf x_0+\mathbf n for any null vector n, since A(\mathbf x_0+\mathbf n)=\mathbf b+\mathbf 0.

Deciding the case (the rank test). Let [A\,|\,\mathbf b] be A with b attached as an extra column. If \operatorname{rank}[A|\mathbf b]>\operatorname{rank}A, adding b added a new direction, so it is not in the column space: no solution. Otherwise a solution exists; it is unique if rank A equals the number of unknowns n (null space = {0}), and infinite if rank A < n.

Worked example — rank and null space

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&6\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix}. Row 2 = 2 × row 1, so it adds nothing: the rank is 2, not 3 (rows 1 and 3 are independent). Rank–nullity: null space has dimension 3 − 2 = 1. Find it: x_1+2x_2+3x_3=0 and x_1+x_3=0 give x_1=-x_3, then -x_3+2x_2+3x_3=0\Rightarrow x_2=-x_3. Taking x_3=-1: the vector (1,1,-1). Check each row: 1+2-3=0, 2+4-6=0, 1+0-1=0 ✓. Notice this null vector is perpendicular to every row — the null space and the row space are orthogonal complements.

How machines solve it: Gaussian elimination. Use the allowed moves — swap equations, multiply one by a non-zero number, add a multiple of one equation to another — none of which change the solutions, to make a triangle of zeros, then work upward.

Worked example — elimination on three unknowns

x+y+z=6,\quad 2x-y+z=3,\quad x+2y-z=2.

Eliminate x: (row 2) − 2(row 1): -3y-z=-9. (row 3) − (row 1): y-2z=-4.

Eliminate y from the last: (new row 3) + (1/3)(new row 2): -\tfrac73z=-7\Rightarrow z=3.

Back-substitute: -3y-3=-9\Rightarrow y=2; then x+2+3=6\Rightarrow x=1. Solution (1,2,3). Check row 3: 1 + 4 − 3 = 2 ✓.

Wide versus tall. With fewer equations than unknowns (a wide matrix) the system is under-determined: generally infinitely many solutions. With more equations than unknowns (a tall matrix — the usual situation in machine learning: many data points, few parameters) it is over-determined: generally no exact solution, because noisy data never satisfy every equation. Then we ask for the best compromise: the least-squares solution (lesson 1.11).

The formal definition

A\mathbf x=\mathbf b,\quad A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n};\qquad \operatorname{col}(A)=\{A\mathbf x\},\quad \operatorname{null}(A)=\{\mathbf x:A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\},\quad \operatorname{rank}(A)=\dim\operatorname{col}(A)
A (m×n)m equations (rows), n unknowns (columns)
col(A)column space: the span of the columns = all reachable right-hand sides
null(A)null space (kernel): inputs that A flattens to zero; the freedom in the solutions
rank(A)number of independent columns (= number of independent rows) ≤ min(m, n)
[A | b]the augmented matrix; solvable ⇔ rank[A|b] = rank A

Solutions: if consistent, the full solution set is \mathbf x_0+\operatorname{null}(A) — one particular solution plus anything from the null space. A square n×n matrix is invertible exactly when its rank is n (full rank), tying this lesson to the last.

Common traps
  • Square does not mean solvable. A square matrix with dependent rows is singular; the system then has no solution or infinitely many.
  • "More equations than unknowns" does not guarantee a solution — it usually does the opposite — and "fewer equations" does not guarantee an infinity (the equations may contradict each other).
  • Rank is about independent directions, not size. A 1000×1000 matrix can have rank 3. In real data, exact dependence is rare but near-dependence (tiny singular values) is common.
  • Row operations preserve the solution set, not the column space. Elimination changes the columns, so you cannot read the original column space off the reduced matrix (but you can read which columns are independent).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Over-determined data: N training examples and d < N parameters give X\mathbf w=\mathbf y with no exact solution — the setting of linear regression and least squares.
  • Under-determined / over-parameterised models: modern networks have more parameters than data points, so there are infinitely many parameter settings that fit the training data exactly; the null space is huge and optimisers/regularisers decide which solution is selected.
  • Redundancy and low rank: embedding or weight matrices are often effectively low-rank, so the rank (and later the singular values) tells how much information they carry — the basis of LoRA and compression.
  • Feature redundancy: one-hot encoding all categories plus an intercept makes columns sum to a constant column — a rank deficiency known as the "dummy variable trap".
  • Solving systems at scale — Gaussian elimination, Cholesky, conjugate gradient — appears inside Gaussian processes, Newton methods and sparse solvers.

Check yourself

1. How many solutions do x + y = 1 and 2x + 2y = 3 have?
None. The second equation says x + y = 1.5, contradicting the first (parallel lines).
2. What is the rank of [[1,2],[2,4],[3,6]]?
1. Column 2 is twice column 1 (equivalently every row is a multiple of (1,2)), so there is only one independent direction. Its null space has dimension 2 − 1 = 1 (e.g. (2, −1)).
3. A is 3 × 5. What are the largest possible rank, and the smallest possible null-space dimension?
Rank ≤ min(3, 5) = 3. By rank–nullity the null space has dimension 5 − rank ≥ 5 − 3 = 2. So any 3 × 5 system A x = b that is solvable has infinitely many solutions.

Takeaways

  • Ax = b: rows = lines that must meet; columns = a recipe that must reach b. b must lie in the column space.
  • 0, 1, or ∞ solutions — never in between. Rank counts independent directions; rank + null-space dimension = number of columns.
  • Tall (over-determined) systems have no exact solution → least squares; wide (under-determined) systems have a whole family.
1.9

Eigenvalues & eigenvectors

The questionA system evolves in steps: next year's state is the matrix A times this year's state (a population in two regions, the weights of a web page's importance, the signal inside a recurrent network). What happens after 10 steps, or 1000? Does it blow up, die out, or settle into a pattern — and is there a quick way to know without multiplying 1000 times?

Intuition

When a matrix acts on a vector, it usually turns it: the output points in a different direction from the input. But for almost every matrix there are a few special directions where the matrix does not turn anything — it only stretches (or shrinks, or flips) the vector along its own line. Those directions are the matrix's eigenvectors ("own vectors", from German eigen), and the stretch factors are the eigenvalues.

Picture the rubber-sheet from lesson 1.6. After the matrix deforms the sheet, most lines through the origin have been rotated to some other line. A few lines are still lines through the origin pointing the same way — the sheet has just been stretched along them. These are the eigen-directions, the "natural axes" of the transformation. In those directions the complicated matrix behaves like plain multiplication by a number.

Why do we care? Because repeating a transformation is hard in general but easy along its eigen-directions: apply it k times to an eigenvector and you simply multiply by \lambda^k. And almost any vector can be built as a mix of eigenvectors, so the long-run behaviour is read off from the eigenvalues.

Build it up

Say it in symbols: a vector v (non-zero) is an eigenvector of A with eigenvalue λ if

A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v

How to find them. Rewrite as (A-\lambda I)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0. We want a non-zero solution v, so the matrix A-\lambda I must flatten something — it must be singular (lesson 1.7) — so its determinant must be 0. That gives the characteristic equation \det(A-\lambda I)=0. For 2×2 it is a quadratic: \lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda+\det A=0 where the trace \operatorname{tr}A=a+d is the sum of the diagonal. Solve for λ, then for each λ solve (A-\lambda I)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0.

Worked example

A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}: trace 7, determinant 10, so \lambda^2-7\lambda+10=0, i.e. (\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)=0: λ = 5 and λ = 2.

For λ = 5: (A-5I)=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}; the first row says -v_1+v_2=0, so \mathbf v=(1,1). Check: A(1,1)=(5,5)=5(1,1) ✓.

For λ = 2: (A-2I)=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix}: 2v_1+v_2=0, so \mathbf v=(1,-2). Check: A(1,-2)=(4-2,\;2-6)=(2,-4)=2(1,-2) ✓.

Handy checks: the eigenvalues add to the trace (5 + 2 = 7) and multiply to the determinant (5 · 2 = 10).

Diagonalisation: switch to the natural axes. If a matrix has n independent eigenvectors, collect them as the columns of V and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of Λ. Then AV=V\Lambda, i.e.

A=V\Lambda V^{-1},\qquad A^k=V\Lambda^kV^{-1}

In words: change to eigen-coordinates (V^{-1}), where the matrix is just a stretch by each λ, apply the stretch k times (\Lambda^k — raise numbers, not matrices, to the power), and change back (V). Computing A^{1000} now costs almost nothing.

Worked example — powers

A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix} has trace 4, determinant 3: λ = 3 and λ = 1, with eigenvectors (1,1) and (1,−1). Any start vector is a mix of these: (1,0)=\tfrac12(1,1)+\tfrac12(1,-1). Apply A k times: A^k(1,0)=\tfrac12\,3^k(1,1)+\tfrac12\,1^k(1,-1). The first part grows like 3k; the second never changes. So after a few steps the vector points almost exactly along (1,1).

Check by brute force: (1,0) → (2,1) → (5,4) → (14,13) → (41,40) → …; the ratios 2:1, 5:4, 14:13, 41:40 → 1:1. And A^5=\begin{pmatrix}122&121\\121&122\end{pmatrix} because (3^5+1)/2=122 and (3^5-1)/2=121.

That is power iteration: multiply again and again and the vector aligns with the eigenvector of the largest |λ| (the dominant one). It is the simplest algorithm for finding a leading eigenvector — and the principle behind PageRank. It works because the dominant eigen-direction grows fastest; the others become negligible in comparison.

What the eigenvalues tell you about repetition. For \mathbf x_{t+1}=A\mathbf x_t: if every eigenvalue has |λ| < 1, the system decays to zero (stable); if some eigenvalue has |λ| > 1, it generally explodes along that direction; if the largest is exactly 1, it settles to a steady state. Negative eigenvalues flip the sign every step. Some real matrices have complex eigenvalues r(\cos\theta\pm i\sin\theta): there is no real direction that survives — the matrix rotates (by θ) while scaling (by r). A pure rotation has no real eigenvectors, since every direction turns.

Symmetric matrices are the best-behaved. If A=A^\top (lesson 1.4), then (the spectral theorem) all eigenvalues are real, the eigenvectors can be chosen of length 1 and mutually perpendicular, and A=Q\Lambda Q^\top with Q^{-1}=Q^\top (a rotation/reflection). So a symmetric matrix is simply: rotate to the natural axes, stretch each axis, rotate back. Covariance matrices and Hessians are symmetric, which is why eigenvalues are so central in AI.

The formal definition

A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v\ (\mathbf v\neq\mathbf 0),\qquad \det(A-\lambda I)=0,\qquad \sum_i\lambda_i=\operatorname{tr}A,\qquad \prod_i\lambda_i=\det A
Aa square matrix (eigenvalues are only defined when input and output spaces are the same)
𝐯an eigenvector: a non-zero direction that A does not turn. Any non-zero multiple of 𝐯 is also an eigenvector — only the direction matters.
λthe eigenvalue: the stretch factor along 𝐯 (negative = flip, 0 = crushed, complex = rotation involved)
det(A − λI)the characteristic polynomial; its roots are the eigenvalues
tr Atrace: sum of the diagonal entries
V, ΛV: eigenvectors as columns; Λ: diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. A = VΛV⁻¹ when V is invertible.
Qfor symmetric A: orthogonal matrix of eigenvectors (Q^\top Q=I)

An eigenvalue 0 means A crushes a direction to zero, i.e. A is singular (det = 0 = product of eigenvalues).

Common traps
  • Eigenvectors are directions, not specific vectors. (1,1), (2,2) and (−5,−5) are the same eigenvector. Normalise to length 1 when you need a canonical choice (and note the sign is still arbitrary).
  • Not every matrix has a full set of eigenvectors. The shear \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} has λ = 1 twice but only one eigen-direction, so it cannot be diagonalised. Symmetric matrices never have this problem.
  • Real matrices can have complex eigenvalues (rotations). That is a feature (it signals rotation), not an error.
  • Eigenvalues of A + B or AB are not simply sums or products of those of A and B (unless the matrices share eigenvectors).
  • Only square matrices have eigenvalues. For rectangular matrices use singular values (lesson 1.10).
Where it shows up in AI
  • PCA: the principal components are the eigenvectors of the data's covariance matrix, with eigenvalues = variance captured (lesson 1.10).
  • Stability of dynamics and recurrent nets: a recurrent network repeatedly multiplies by a weight matrix W; if the largest |eigenvalue| > 1 signals explode, if < 1 they vanish — the same phenomenon as exploding/vanishing gradients. Careful initialisation (orthogonal matrices, |λ| ≈ 1) is chosen for this reason.
  • Hessian curvature: the eigenvalues of the Hessian (matrix of second derivatives of the loss) are the curvatures along its eigen-directions; their ratio (condition number) decides how fast gradient descent converges (Module 6). Sharp vs flat minima are statements about large vs small eigenvalues.
  • PageRank and Markov chains: a web page's rank is the dominant eigenvector of the link matrix, found by power iteration; the stationary distribution of a Markov chain is an eigenvector with λ = 1 (Module 8).
  • Spectral clustering and graph neural networks use eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian.

Takeaways

  • Av = λv: eigenvectors are directions A only stretches; λ is the stretch factor. Find λ from det(A − λI) = 0.
  • A = VΛV⁻¹ makes powers easy: Aᵏ = VΛᵏV⁻¹. Repeated multiplication aligns any vector with the dominant eigenvector (power iteration).
  • |λ| < 1 decays, |λ| > 1 explodes. Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors: the backbone of PCA and curvature analysis.
1.10

SVD & PCA

The questionA photo is a 1000×1000 matrix: a million numbers. A word-embedding table has hundreds of millions. Most of that information is redundant. Can we find the few directions that carry most of the content, throw away the rest, and know exactly how much we lose? And when each data point has 50 features, which handful of combinations of them explain most of the variation between examples?

Intuition

Eigenvectors were wonderful but limited: they need a square matrix, and may not be perpendicular. The singular value decomposition (SVD) is the version that works for every matrix, of any shape, and always has beautifully orthogonal pieces. Its message is this:

Every matrix is a rotation, then a stretch along the axes, then another rotation.

Remember the picture of the rubber sheet. Take the unit circle and push it through any matrix: you always get an ellipse (possibly flat). The directions of the ellipse's axes and how long each semi-axis is describe the matrix completely. The semi-axis lengths are the singular values \sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge0: how much the matrix stretches in its most-stretched, next-most-stretched … directions. The directions in the output are \mathbf u_1,\mathbf u_2,\dots; the input directions that get mapped to them are \mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\dots; and A\mathbf v_i=\sigma_i\mathbf u_i. A matrix with a singular value of 0 flattens one direction completely.

The punchline is compression. Write the matrix as a sum of simple "layers", each layer being a rank-1 piece (a column times a row), ordered from most important to least. Keep the first few layers and you have the best possible low-detail version of the matrix; the singular values tell how much each layer matters. And PCA is this very idea applied to a centred data table: the most important layers are the directions in which the data varies most.

Build it up

The decomposition. For any m\times n matrix A:

A=U\Sigma V^\top=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i\,\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^\top

where V (n×n) and U (m×m) have perpendicular unit-length columns (rotations/reflections), and Σ is the diagonal matrix of singular values. Read right-to-left on a vector: V^\top rotates into the "natural input axes", Σ stretches each axis by σᵢ, U rotates into the output. The sum form says: A is a sum of r rank-1 layers \sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^\top (a column times a row is a matrix whose rows are all multiples of one vector). The rank r of A is the number of non-zero singular values.

How to find them. Look at A^\top A — an n×n symmetric matrix. Its eigenvectors are the \mathbf v_i and its eigenvalues are \sigma_i^2. (Because \lVert A\mathbf v\rVert^2=\mathbf v^\top A^\top A\mathbf v: the squared stretch is a quadratic form of A^\top A.) Then \mathbf u_i=A\mathbf v_i/\sigma_i.

Worked example — the shear

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}. Then A^\top A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix} with trace 3 and determinant 1: \mu^2-3\mu+1=0\Rightarrow\mu=\frac{3\pm\sqrt5}2\approx2.618,\ 0.382. So the singular values are \sigma_1=\sqrt{2.618}\approx1.618 and \sigma_2=\sqrt{0.382}\approx0.618 (the golden ratio and its reciprocal!).

The unit circle becomes an ellipse with semi-axes 1.618 and 0.618. Check: their product 1.618 · 0.618 = 1 = |det A| — the area scaling factor, as it must be (the product of singular values is |det|). The first input direction is \mathbf v_1\approx(0.526,0.851); A\mathbf v_1=(1.376,0.851)=1.618\cdot(0.851,0.526), so \mathbf u_1\approx(0.851,0.526) ✓.

Rank-1 approximation: keep only the first layer, A_1=\sigma_1\mathbf u_1\mathbf v_1^\top\approx\begin{pmatrix}0.724&1.171\\0.447&0.724\end{pmatrix}. The error matrix A-A_1 has size exactly \sigma_2=0.618 (Frobenius norm).

Best low-rank approximation. The Eckart–Young theorem says: among all matrices of rank at most k, the truncated sum A_k=\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i^\top is the closest to A, with error \lVert A-A_k\rVert_F=\sqrt{\sigma_{k+1}^2+\sigma_{k+2}^2+\cdots}. The fraction of "energy" kept is \sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2/\sum_i\sigma_i^2. Storage drops from mn numbers to k(m+n+1) (store k columns of U, k of V and k singular values). For a 1000×1000 image with k = 50: 100 050 numbers instead of 1 000 000. Structured data (photos, text statistics) has quickly decaying singular values; pure noise does not — the lab shows both.

PCA: the same machine on data. Take your data matrix X (N examples × d features) and centre it: subtract each column's mean, so the cloud is centred at the origin. We want a direction u (unit length) such that the data projected on it — the numbers \mathbf x_i\cdot\mathbf u — are as spread out as possible (maximum variance): that direction shows the most about how the examples differ. The variance along u is \mathbf u^\top C\mathbf u where C=\tfrac1NX^\top X is the covariance matrix (symmetric!). Maximising it over unit vectors gives, by the spectral theorem, the top eigenvector of C: the first principal component; the next one is the best direction perpendicular to it, and so on. The eigenvalue λᵢ is exactly the variance captured by component i.

There is a second way to say the same thing, which is why PCA is both "maximum variance" and "minimum loss": by Pythagoras, for each point, (squared length) = (squared length of its shadow on the line) + (squared distance to the line). Since the total is fixed, maximising the shadows' variance is the same as minimising the squared distances to the line. And the SVD of the centred X gives everything at once: right singular vectors = principal directions, and \lambda_i=\sigma_i^2/N.

Worked example — PCA by hand

Four centred points: (2,1), (−2,−1), (1,2), (−1,−2). Covariance: C_{11}=(4+4+1+1)/4=2.5, C_{22}=(1+1+4+4)/4=2.5, C_{12}=(2+2+2+2)/4=2. So C=\begin{pmatrix}2.5&2\\2&2.5\end{pmatrix}: its eigenvalues are 4.5 and 0.5 (trace 5, determinant 6.25 − 4 = 2.25 = 4.5·0.5) with eigenvectors \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1) and \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1).

PC1 = (1,1)/√2 captures 4.5/(4.5+0.5)=90\% of the variance. The coordinates of the four points on PC1: (2+1)/\sqrt2=2.121, −2.121, 2.121, −2.121; variance (2.121^2\cdot4)/4=4.5 ✓. We can describe 2-D data by one number per point and lose only 10 % of the variance. (Here we divide by N; many libraries divide by N − 1, which only rescales all variances.)

Recipe for PCA: centre the data → covariance matrix (or SVD of X) → sort eigenvalues → keep the top k directions → project: Z=XV_k (N×k). Choose k from the explained-variance ratio (e.g. "keep 95 %").

Two more things the SVD gives. The condition number \sigma_1/\sigma_r says how badly the matrix amplifies errors (huge for near-singular matrices, lesson 1.7). The pseudo-inverse A^+=V\Sigma^+U^\top (invert the non-zero σ's, leave zeros alone) works for any shape and rank — used for least squares (next lesson).

The formal definition

A=U\Sigma V^\top,\quad U^\top U=I,\ V^\top V=I,\quad \Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge0);\qquad A\mathbf v_i=\sigma_i\mathbf u_i
C=\frac1N X^\top X=Q\Lambda Q^\top,\qquad \text{PC}_i=\mathbf q_i,\quad \operatorname{Var}_i=\lambda_i=\frac{\sigma_i^2}{N},\quad \text{explained ratio}_i=\frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_j\lambda_j}

Common traps
  • Forgetting to centre the data before PCA: otherwise the first component just points at the mean instead of the direction of variation.
  • Forgetting to scale features. PCA follows variance, so a feature measured in large units dominates. Standardise first unless the units are meaningful.
  • High variance ≠ important for the task. PCA is unsupervised; the direction of largest variance may be noise or irrelevant to your label.
  • Principal components are directions defined up to sign; plots may flip between runs.
  • σ vs λ: singular values of X are not the eigenvalues of C — λᵢ = σᵢ²/N. Squaring matters when you compute "explained variance".
Where it shows up in AI
  • PCA for visualisation, noise reduction, decorrelation (whitening) and as preprocessing before k-NN, clustering or regression.
  • LoRA (low-rank adaptation) fine-tunes a big weight matrix W by learning an update \Delta W=BA with a tiny inner dimension r: for a 1024×1024 matrix and r = 4, that is 2\cdot1024\cdot4=8192 parameters instead of 1 048 576 (0.78 %) — the same low-rank idea as truncated SVD.
  • Compression and recommendation: matrix factorisation (the Netflix prize) is a low-rank approximation of the user × item rating matrix; latent semantic analysis applies SVD to word × document counts; embeddings are often compressed with PCA/SVD.
  • Analysing networks: the singular values of weight matrices and embeddings reveal effective rank and training dynamics; the condition number predicts numerical trouble; the pseudo-inverse gives closed-form fits.

Takeaways

  • Every matrix = rotate · stretch · rotate (UΣVᵀ) = a sum of rank-1 layers σᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ, strongest first.
  • Truncating to k layers is the best rank-k approximation; the discarded σ's tell the error. This is compression and LoRA.
  • PCA = eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of centred data = directions of maximum variance = minimum reconstruction error; λᵢ = σᵢ²/N.
1.11

Projections & least squares

The questionThree data points do not lie on a single straight line, so X\mathbf w=\mathbf y has no exact solution (lesson 1.8). Which line is "best"? And is there a formula — with a clear geometric meaning — that gives the best weights directly, without any search?

Intuition

Stand a stick upright in the sunshine: its shadow on the ground is the projection of the stick onto the ground plane. The shadow is the point of the ground closest to the top of the stick, and the line from the top of the stick straight down to the shadow is perpendicular to the ground. That single picture is the entire lesson:

The closest point of a flat set (line, plane, subspace) to a given point is where the perpendicular from the point lands. The leftover error is perpendicular to the flat set.

Now connect it to regression. The set of all outputs a model can produce, X\mathbf w as w varies, is the column space of X — a flat sheet (a subspace) living inside the space of all possible target vectors. The real data y usually sticks out of that sheet, because of noise. The best we can do is to pick the point of the sheet closest to y — the projection of y onto the column space. Its coordinates (the combination of columns that reaches it) are the least-squares weights. "Least squares" because the distance squared is the sum of the squared residuals.

Build it up

Step 1: projecting onto a line. From lesson 1.2, the projection of b onto the line spanned by a is

\mathbf p=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a}\,\mathbf a,\qquad \mathbf e=\mathbf b-\mathbf p,\qquad \mathbf e\cdot\mathbf a=0
Worked example

\mathbf b=(2,3) onto \mathbf a=(3,1): \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=9, \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a=10, so \mathbf p=0.9\,(3,1)=(2.7,\,0.9). The error is \mathbf e=(2,3)-(2.7,0.9)=(-0.7,\,2.1), and \mathbf e\cdot\mathbf a=-2.1+2.1=0 ✓ (perpendicular). Pythagoras: \lVert\mathbf b\rVert^2=13 and \lVert\mathbf p\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf e\rVert^2=8.1+4.9=13 ✓. Any other point on the line, say 1\cdot\mathbf a=(3,1), is farther from b: distance² = 1 + 4 = 5 > 4.9.

Step 2: projecting onto a subspace spanned by several columns. We want \mathbf p=X\hat{\mathbf w} such that the residual \mathbf e=\mathbf y-X\hat{\mathbf w} is perpendicular to every column of X. "Perpendicular to every column" means each column's dot product with e is zero; written for all columns at once, that is X^\top\mathbf e=\mathbf 0. Substituting:

X^\top(\mathbf y-X\hat{\mathbf w})=\mathbf 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X^\top X\,\hat{\mathbf w}=X^\top\mathbf y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hat{\mathbf w}=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y

This is the normal equation ("normal" = perpendicular). The last form requires X^\top X to be invertible, which holds exactly when the columns of X are independent (no redundant features — lesson 1.7). The projection itself is \mathbf p=X\hat{\mathbf w}=P\mathbf y with the projection matrix P=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top. It satisfies P^2=P (projecting twice changes nothing: a shadow's shadow is itself) and P^\top=P.

Worked example — fit a line to three points

Data: (x, y) = (0,1), (1,2), (2,2). Model y\approx w_0+w_1x. Columns of X: a column of ones (for the intercept) and the x-values:

X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\ \mathbf y=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}. Then X^\top X=\begin{pmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}, X^\top\mathbf y=\begin{pmatrix}5\\6\end{pmatrix}. Determinant 15-9=6, inverse \tfrac16\begin{pmatrix}5&-3\\-3&3\end{pmatrix}.

\hat{\mathbf w}=\tfrac16(5\cdot5-3\cdot6,\;-3\cdot5+3\cdot6)=\tfrac16(7,3)=(1.167,\,0.5): the line \hat y=1.167+0.5x.

Predictions X\hat{\mathbf w}=(1.167,\,1.667,\,2.167); residuals \mathbf e=(-0.167,\,0.333,\,-0.167). Check perpendicularity: 1·e = −0.167 + 0.333 − 0.167 = 0 and x·e = 0·(−0.167) + 1·0.333 + 2·(−0.167) = 0 ✓ — the residuals sum to zero and are uncorrelated with x. Sum of squared errors = 1/36 + 1/9 + 1/36 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167. Compare another reasonable line, \hat y=1+0.5x: predictions (1, 1.5, 2), residuals (0, 0.5, 0), squared error 0.25 > 0.167. The projection wins.

Why "least squares" is the same as "perpendicular". Take any point X\mathbf w of the column space. By Pythagoras, \lVert\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf y-\mathbf p\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf p-X\mathbf w\rVert^2 (because \mathbf y-\mathbf p is perpendicular to the plane, and \mathbf p-X\mathbf w lies in it). The second term is ≥ 0 and vanishes only at X\mathbf w=\mathbf p. So the projection uniquely minimises the sum of squared residuals. (Calculus gives the same normal equation: the gradient of \lVert\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\rVert^2 is -2X^\top(\mathbf y-X\mathbf w); set it to zero — see Module 2.)

In practice. Explicitly forming (X^\top X)^{-1} is numerically fragile (the condition number is squared). Software solves the least-squares problem with QR factorisation or the SVD pseudo-inverse, \hat{\mathbf w}=X^+\mathbf y, which also handles dependent columns by returning the smallest solution. When columns are nearly dependent, ridge regression solves (X^\top X+\lambda I)\mathbf w=X^\top\mathbf y: adding λ to the diagonal makes the matrix safely invertible.

The formal definition

\hat{\mathbf w}=\arg\min_{\mathbf w}\lVert\mathbf y-X\mathbf w\rVert_2^2\ \Longleftrightarrow\ X^\top X\hat{\mathbf w}=X^\top\mathbf y,\qquad \mathbf p=X\hat{\mathbf w}=P\mathbf y,\quad P=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top

Common traps
  • Least squares minimises vertical (y-direction) squared errors, not perpendicular distance to the line in the (x, y) plane. (The perpendicular in the lesson is in the space of observations, not the scatter plot.) Errors in x are ignored.
  • Forgetting the intercept column. Without a column of ones the line is forced through the origin, and the residuals no longer sum to zero.
  • Squared error is sensitive to outliers: one wild point can drag the line a long way.
  • (XᵀX)⁻¹ needs independent columns. Redundant or near-redundant features make it singular or wildly unstable (lesson 1.7); use a solver, SVD, or ridge instead of inverting.
  • A perfect fit on training data is not the goal when d ≥ N: the projection is then y itself and the model has memorised the noise (overfitting, Module 4).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Linear regression is exactly this projection: the closed-form fit is the normal equation; gradient descent on the squared loss converges to the same ŵ.
  • Feature-based models: polynomial regression, random-feature models, and "linear probes" on frozen embeddings are all least-squares problems in a transformed design matrix.
  • Ridge regression / weight decay is the regularised projection (X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y and has a Bayesian reading (Module 4).
  • Residual structure: "residuals are orthogonal to the features" underlies diagnostic plots, and the same orthogonality appears in Gram–Schmidt / QR decompositions and in iterative solvers.
  • PCA is a projection too: the reconstruction from k components is the orthogonal projection of the data onto the top-k eigen-subspace (lesson 1.10); autoencoders learn a (nonlinear) analogue.

Takeaways

  • Projection = foot of the perpendicular: the nearest point of a subspace, with an error perpendicular to it.
  • Least squares = project y onto the column space of X: XᵀXŵ = Xᵀy, i.e. residual ⟂ every feature column. Needs independent columns; use solvers/ridge in practice.
  • Linear regression is geometry: the best-fit line is a shadow in observation space.
