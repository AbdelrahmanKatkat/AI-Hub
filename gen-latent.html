Module 7 · Latent-variable & adversarial generators

A language model writes one token at a time from a probability table. Images, audio and other continuous data call for different tools. Three families of generators were built before diffusion became dominant, and each is a different answer to one problem: how do you train a model of a distribution you cannot write down? A variational autoencoder adds a hidden code and trains a lower bound on the likelihood. A GAN trains a generator against a critic and never computes a likelihood. A normalising flow keeps the likelihood exact by using an invertible map. In the six-stage pipeline (collect and tokenise, pretrain, align, adapt and compress, generate, evaluate and guard) this module sits mainly in pretrain and generate for non-text data, and its discrete codes (lesson 7.2) are the image tokenisers that feed the multimodal models of Module 9.

7.1

Variational autoencoders & the ELBO

The questionYou want a model that generates handwritten digits, so you decide each digit comes from a hidden recipe z (a few numbers such as slant, thickness and which digit) that you never observe. The probability of a particular image is then a sum over every recipe that could have produced it. That sum is impossible to compute for real images. How can you still train the model to make the data likely?

Intuition: guess the recipe, then check how well it explains the picture

Imagine a detective who sees a finished dish (x) and has to say what recipe (z) produced it. Before tasting, the detective has a prior: which recipes are common. The kitchen has a decoder: given a recipe, how likely each dish is. The ideal answer to the detective's question is the posterior, the probability of each recipe given the dish, and Bayes' rule says it needs the sum over all recipes that we cannot compute. So we train a second, simpler model, the encoder q(z\mid x), to approximate the posterior. The trick of the variational autoencoder (VAE) is that a single number, the evidence lower bound (ELBO), rewards two things at once: the guessed recipe must explain the dish well, and it must not be stranger than the kitchen's prior. Raising the ELBO trains the decoder and the encoder together.

Build it up

Step 1: the latent-variable model. The model has a prior p(z) and a decoder p(x\mid z). Together they define the joint p(x,z)=p(z)\,p(x\mid z) and the quantity we want to be large for real data, the evidence (marginal likelihood) p(x)=\sum_z p(z)\,p(x\mid z) (an integral if z is continuous). The posterior is p(z\mid x)=p(x,z)/p(x). See Bayes' rule if that formula is new.

Worked example 1: everything exact, on two recipes

Let z\in\{0,1\} with prior p(z{=}0)=0.6,\;p(z{=}1)=0.4. The dish x takes one of three values a, b, c. We observe x=c, and the decoder gives p(c\mid z{=}0)=0.1 and p(c\mid z{=}1)=0.6.

zprior p(z)p(c | z)joint p(c, z)posterior p(z | c)
00.60.10.060.06 / 0.30 = 0.2
10.40.60.240.24 / 0.30 = 0.8

The evidence is p(c)=0.06+0.24=0.30, so \ln p(c)=-1.2040. Here the sum has two terms; for images it has astronomically many, which is why we need an approximation.

Step 2: any distribution q(z) over recipes gives a lower bound. Start from the identity \ln p(x)=\ln p(x,z)-\ln p(z\mid x), which holds for every z (because p(x,z)=p(z\mid x)\,p(x)). Average both sides over z\sim q, and add and subtract \ln q(z) inside:

\ln p(x)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\ln p(x,z)-\ln q(z)\big]}_{\text{ELBO}(q)}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\Big[\ln\frac{q(z)}{p(z\mid x)}\Big]}_{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x))}

The left side does not depend on q at all. The last term is a KL divergence, which is never negative and is zero only when q equals the posterior. Therefore \ln p(x)\ge\mathrm{ELBO}(q) always, with equality exactly when q is the true posterior. The gap is not a vague "approximation error": it is exactly the KL from q to the posterior. That is why maximising the ELBO over q makes q a better posterior, and maximising over the decoder parameters raises the likelihood.

Step 3: the form you actually compute. Split \ln p(x,z)=\ln p(x\mid z)+\ln p(z):

\mathrm{ELBO}(q)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\big[\ln p(x\mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}}-\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{keep the code close to the prior}}

The reconstruction term asks: if I draw a recipe from my guess, how well does the decoder explain the dish? The KL term charges a price for a guess that differs from the prior. In a real VAE the guess depends on the dish, q_\phi(z\mid x), produced by a neural network (the encoder, with weights \phi), and the decoder p_\theta(x\mid z) is another network with weights \theta. One encoder serves every data point ("amortised" inference), instead of solving a fresh optimisation for each dish. Training maximises the average ELBO over the dataset.

Worked example 2: checking the identity on the table

Take the encoder to be a coin q(z{=}0)=0.5,\;q(z{=}1)=0.5.

Reconstruction: 0.5\ln0.1+0.5\ln0.6=0.5(-2.3026)+0.5(-0.5108)=-1.4067.

KL to the prior: 0.5\ln\tfrac{0.5}{0.6}+0.5\ln\tfrac{0.5}{0.4}=0.0204. So \mathrm{ELBO}=-1.4067-0.0204=-1.4271, below \ln p(c)=-1.2040.

KL to the posterior (0.2,0.8): 0.5\ln\tfrac{0.5}{0.2}+0.5\ln\tfrac{0.5}{0.8}=0.2231. And -1.4271+0.2231=-1.2040, the evidence, exactly.

Now move the encoder to q(z{=}1)=0.8, the posterior: reconstruction -0.8692, KL to the prior 0.3348, ELBO -1.2040, gap 0. For q(z{=}1)=0.9 the ELBO is -1.2407 and the gap is 0.0367. Note that the best encoder pays a higher KL (0.3348) than the lazy one (0.0204): the KL term is a price, and the reconstruction gain outweighs it.

Step 4: Gaussians and a closed-form KL. In a standard VAE, p(z)=\mathcal N(0,1) per dimension and the encoder outputs a mean \mu and a standard deviation \sigma, so q=\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2). The KL between two Gaussians has a closed form, so that term needs no sampling:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0,1)\big)=\tfrac12\big(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\ln\sigma^2\big)

Check with numbers (computed by direct numerical integration of q\ln(q/p) as well): \mu=0.5,\sigma=0.8 gives \tfrac12(0.25+0.64-1-\ln0.64)=0.1681 and the integral also gives 0.1681; \mu=1,\sigma=0.5 gives 0.8181 by both. For a decoder p(x\mid z)=\mathcal N(wz,s^2) the reconstruction term is -\tfrac12\ln(2\pi s^2)-\big((x-w\mu)^2+w^2\sigma^2\big)/(2s^2): a squared error, plus a penalty for the encoder's spread.

Worked example 3: one-dimensional Gaussian VAE with a known posterior

Take w=1,\;s=0.5, observed x=2. With a Gaussian prior and a linear Gaussian decoder, the exact posterior is Gaussian: precision 1+w^2/s^2=5, mean (wx/s^2)/5=1.6, variance 0.2 (std 0.4472). Marginally x\sim\mathcal N(0,w^2+s^2)=\mathcal N(0,1.25), so \ln p(x)=-2.6305.

Encoder = prior (\mu=0,\sigma=1, KL = 0): reconstruction -10.2258, ELBO -10.2258. The gap to \ln p(x) is 7.5953.

Encoder = exact posterior (\mu=1.6,\sigma=0.4472): reconstruction -0.9458, KL 1.6847, ELBO -0.9458-1.6847=-2.6305, equal to \ln p(x). The lab below does exactly this, and also estimates the ELBO by Monte Carlo with 2000 samples to confirm the closed forms.

The reparameterisation trick

To train by gradient descent we need the gradient of \mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\phi}[f(z)] with respect to the encoder's parameters \mu,\sigma, where f(z)=\ln p_\theta(x\mid z). The difficulty: the random draw z sits between the parameters and the loss, and "drawing a random number" has no gradient. The fix is to move the randomness out of the way. Write the sample as z=\mu+\sigma\varepsilon with \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1) drawn independently of \mu,\sigma. Now z is an ordinary differentiable function of \mu,\sigma for a fixed \varepsilon, and the chain rule gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\,\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\big[f(\mu+\sigma\varepsilon)\big]=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\big[f'(z)\big],\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\,\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\big[f(\mu+\sigma\varepsilon)\big]=\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\big[f'(z)\,\varepsilon\big]

You estimate each expectation by averaging over a few draws of \varepsilon; in practice one draw per data point is common. (An alternative, the score-function or REINFORCE estimator, needs no differentiable f and also works for discrete z, but it typically has much higher variance. That is why the VAE uses the reparameterisation for continuous codes.)

Worked example 4: a numeric gradient check

Let f(z)=(z-3)^2, \mu=1,\sigma=0.5. The exact answer is available: \mathbb{E}[f]=(\mu-3)^2+\sigma^2=4.25, so \partial_\mu=2(\mu-3)=-4 and \partial_\sigma=2\sigma=1.

Take four fixed draws \varepsilon=(0.5,-1,1.5,0). Then z=(1.25,0.5,1.75,1), f(z)=(3.0625,6.25,1.5625,4) with mean 3.71875, and f'(z)=2(z-3)=(-3.5,-5,-2.5,-4). The pathwise estimates are \partial_\mu\approx\text{mean}(f')=-3.75 and \partial_\sigma\approx\text{mean}(f'\varepsilon)=(-1.75+5-3.75+0)/4=-0.125.

A finite-difference check on the same four draws (nudge \mu by 10^{-5}, recompute the mean of f) gives -3.7500 and -0.1250: identical, because the chain rule is exact for fixed draws. The difference from the true values -4 and 1 is Monte Carlo noise from using only four draws, and the lab shows it shrinking as draws accumulate.

The formal definition

\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x)=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\ln p_\theta(x\mid z)\big]-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)\;\le\;\ln p_\theta(x),\qquad \ln p_\theta(x)-\mathcal L=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big)
xan observed data point (an image, a clip).
zthe latent code: the unobserved recipe, usually a vector of 16 to a few hundred numbers.
p(z)the prior over codes, typically standard normal.
p_\theta(x\mid z)the decoder, a neural network with weights \theta that outputs a distribution over x (for a Gaussian decoder, a mean image and a fixed or learned spread).
q_\phi(z\mid x)the encoder with weights \phi: a distribution over codes for this x, an approximation to the intractable p_\theta(z\mid x).
\mathbb{E}_{q}[\cdot]average over codes drawn from the encoder (estimated by sampling with the reparameterisation).
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(a\,\|\,b)KL divergence from a to b: \mathbb{E}_a[\ln(a/b)]\ge0, zero iff a=b. It is not symmetric.
\mathcal Lthe ELBO, "evidence lower bound": training maximises it (equivalently minimises -\mathcal L as the loss).

Cost and memory: one forward pass through the encoder and one through the decoder per example, plus sampling noise of the code's dimension. Memory is that of two networks of ordinary size. Generating new data afterwards needs only the decoder: draw z\sim p(z), decode. The related image models are in the vision module.

Common traps
  • "The ELBO is the likelihood." It is a lower bound, off by exactly the KL from encoder to true posterior. A rising ELBO can come from a better decoder, a better encoder, or both; the gap is unknown unless you can compute \ln p(x).
  • The KL direction. The ELBO's gap is D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x)) with q first. Reversing it changes which mistakes are punished (lesson 7.2 shows the consequence for blur).
  • Reparameterisation is for continuous codes. You cannot write a discrete draw as a smooth function of its parameters; discrete latents need other estimators or relaxations (see VQ-VAE in 7.2).
  • A good ELBO is not the same as good samples. The bound measures likelihood, and likelihood and visual quality can disagree.
Where it shows up in AI
  • VAEs learn a compact latent space in which nearby codes decode to similar data, used for interpolation, denoising and anomaly scoring.
  • Autoencoders inside other generators. Many latent-diffusion style systems first compress data with a VAE-type autoencoder and model the latent space (Module 8, latent diffusion); the KL term keeps that space well-behaved.
  • The ELBO idea is everywhere: diffusion training objectives (Module 8) are derived as ELBOs of a latent-variable model with many latent layers, and the same bound appears in variational Bayes.

Check yourself

1. In worked example 1, what is the ELBO and the gap when the encoder is q(z=1) = 0.9?
Reconstruction = 0.1 ln 0.1 + 0.9 ln 0.6 = −0.2303 − 0.4597 = −0.6900. KL to the prior = 0.1 ln(0.1/0.6) + 0.9 ln(0.9/0.4) = 0.5507. ELBO = −0.6900 − 0.5507 = −1.2407. The gap is KL(q ‖ posterior) = 0.1 ln(0.1/0.2) + 0.9 ln(0.9/0.8) = 0.0367, and −1.2407 + 0.0367 = −1.2040 = ln p(c).
2. What is the KL between q = N(1, 0.5²) and the prior N(0, 1)?
½(μ² + σ² − 1 − ln σ²) = ½(1 + 0.25 − 1 − ln 0.25) = ½(0.25 + 1.3863) = 0.8181.
3. Why can the ELBO never exceed ln p(x), whatever q you choose?
Because ln p(x) = ELBO(q) + KL(q ‖ posterior) for every q, and a KL divergence is never negative. The ELBO is ln p(x) minus a non-negative number, so it equals ln p(x) only when q is exactly the posterior.

Takeaways

  • ln p(x) = ELBO + KL(q ‖ p(z | x)): the ELBO is a lower bound, tight exactly when the encoder equals the true posterior (checked: −1.2040 both sides).
  • ELBO = reconstruction − KL(q ‖ prior). For Gaussians the KL is ½(μ² + σ² − 1 − ln σ²), verified against numerical integration.
  • The reparameterisation z = μ + σε moves the randomness out of the way so gradients flow through the sample; the pathwise gradient matches a finite-difference check on the same draws.
  • One amortised encoder serves all data points; generation needs only the prior and decoder.
7.2

VAE pitfalls & discrete codes

The questionYou trained a VAE and the ELBO improved steadily, yet its samples look like washed-out blurs, and when you inspect the latent code you find the decoder barely uses it. Is the model broken, or is the objective doing exactly what it was told? And if a continuous code is awkward, can a discrete code (a list of tokens, like text) work better?

Intuition: the code is a budget, and the loss is an average

Think of the latent code as a message you are allowed to send the decoder, and of the KL term as the price per bit of that message. If bits are expensive, you send fewer of them, and with fewer bits the decoder can only say "something near the average". If bits are free, the code carries everything and the reconstruction is sharp but the latent space need not look like the prior (so sampling from the prior misses). That trade-off is a rate (bits sent) against distortion (error after decoding), and the VAE objective picks one point on the curve by a fixed exchange rate. Two failures follow from extreme prices. A decoder powerful enough to model the data without any code makes sending bits pointless: posterior collapse. And a squared-error decoder that is unsure which of two plausible outputs is right, answers with their average: blur.

Build it up

The beta-VAE: make the exchange rate explicit. Multiply the KL term by a number \beta:

\mathcal L_\beta=\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\ln p(x\mid z)\big]-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)

With \beta=1 this is the ELBO. Larger \beta makes bits more expensive; smaller makes them cheaper. (For \beta\ne1 the objective is no longer a bound on \ln p(x); it is a deliberate re-weighting.) In the one-dimensional Gaussian model of lesson 7.1 (decoder x\sim\mathcal N(wz,s^2)), the best encoder for a given \beta is found by setting the derivatives to zero:

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{w^{2}}{\beta s^{2}}},\qquad \mu=\frac{w\,x/s^{2}}{\beta+w^{2}/s^{2}}

(Derivation in one line each: the derivative in \mu is w(x-w\mu)/s^2-\beta\mu=0; the derivative in \sigma^2 is -w^2/(2s^2)-\tfrac\beta2(1-1/\sigma^2)=0.) At \beta=1 this is exactly the posterior of lesson 7.1. As \beta\to\infty, \sigma^2\to1 and \mu\to0: the encoder becomes the prior and the code carries no information about x. That is collapse, reached here by turning a knob.

Worked example 1: sweeping beta for x = 2

Take w=1,\;s=0.5,\;x=2 (each row was also confirmed by a brute-force search over a grid of \mu,\sigma).

Read across a row: as \beta grows, the KL (the information the code carries) falls from 2.72 to 0.09 nats, and the decoded value slides from 1.88 (almost the true 2) to 0.40, drifting toward 0, the average of a world in which the data sit near both +2 and −2. The decoder gives back the average of what the code leaves undetermined.

Blur has the same cause. With a Gaussian decoder of fixed spread, the best decoded output for a given code is the average of all data points consistent with that code. Suppose half your images have a bright eye on the left and half on the right and the code cannot tell them apart: the optimum is a faint eye in both places. Real VAE blur is this effect at scale. It comes from the pixel-wise squared-error (Gaussian) likelihood plus the limited bits in the code, not from a flaw in the ELBO derivation. Remedies include a richer decoder likelihood, a learned or perceptual loss, and a cheaper code (lower \beta).

Posterior collapse in practice. Take a decoder that is itself a strong sequence model of x (for example autoregressive). It can predict x well without ever looking at z. Then the cheapest way to raise the ELBO is to set q(z\mid x)=p(z) for every x: the KL is then exactly 0, the code is ignored, and the model is only a plain decoder. Common mitigations are to start with \beta\approx0 and anneal it up during training (KL warm-up), to guarantee a minimum number of nats of KL per dimension ("free bits"), or to weaken the decoder.

Discrete codes: the VQ-VAE

Text has a natural discrete vocabulary; images do not. A vector-quantised VAE (VQ-VAE) builds one. The encoder outputs a vector z_e; there is a learned codebook of K vectors e_1,\dots,e_K; the vector is snapped to the nearest code, k=\arg\min_j\|z_e-e_j\|^2, and the decoder receives z_q=e_k. An image becomes a grid of integer indices, a token sequence, exactly the kind of object a transformer can model (the route to the image tokenisers used by multimodal models). Each position costs at most \log_2K bits: for a grid of 256 positions and K=512, 256\times9=2304 bits in all (a hypothetical size for the arithmetic).

Snapping has no gradient (the argmin is a step function), so the VQ-VAE uses the straight-through estimator: in the backward pass, pretend z_q=z_e and copy the gradient of the loss at z_q to z_e. The full loss has three terms:

\mathcal L=\underbrace{-\ln p(x\mid z_q)}_{\text{reconstruction}}+\underbrace{\big\|\operatorname{sg}[z_e]-e_k\big\|^2}_{\text{move the code to the vector}}+\gamma\underbrace{\big\|z_e-\operatorname{sg}[e_k]\big\|^2}_{\text{commitment: keep the vector near its code}}

Here \operatorname{sg}[\cdot] is "stop gradient": treat the value as a constant. No KL term is needed because the prior over the (uniform, deterministic) code indices is fixed; a separate autoregressive or transformer model is trained afterwards on the index sequences to generate new ones. Discreteness also sidesteps the posterior-collapse failure of continuous codes in many setups, though VQ-VAEs have their own failure: codebook collapse, where only a few codes are ever chosen and the rest "die" (stay unused). Remedies include re-initialising dead codes from data and updating the codebook by an exponential moving average of the vectors assigned to it.

Worked example 2: quantising six numbers

One-dimensional encoder outputs 1.0,\,1.2,\,0.8,\,4.9,\,5.2,\,5.1 and a codebook of two codes e_1=0,\,e_2=3.

Assign. 1.0, 1.2, 0.8 are nearer to 0 (distances 1.0, 1.2, 0.8 against 2.0, 1.8, 2.2); 4.9, 5.2, 5.1 are nearer to 3. Quantisation error: (1+1.44+0.64+3.61+4.84+4.41)/6=15.94/6=2.6567.

Move. e_1\to(1.0+1.2+0.8)/3=1.0000, e_2\to(4.9+5.2+5.1)/3=5.0667. New error 0.0211. A second assignment changes nothing: converged. This is the codebook update of the lab (a moving average of the assigned vectors plays this role in practice).

Straight-through. Let the decoder be d(z)=2z and the target x=3. A vector 1.3 snapped to code 1.0 gives loss (2\cdot1.0-3)^2=1 and gradient with respect to z_q of 2(2-3)\cdot2=-4. The estimator hands -4 straight to the encoder output 1.3, telling it to move up toward 3/2, even though the real function (the argmin) has zero gradient.

The formal summary

\betaweight on the KL term. \beta=1 is the ELBO; larger values lower the information in the code (rate) and raise reconstruction error (distortion).
posterior collapseq(z\mid x)\approx p(z) for all x: zero KL, code ignored.
K,\;e_kcodebook size and the k-th code vector (learned).
z_e,\;z_qencoder output before snapping and the code vector after snapping.
\gammaweight of the commitment loss (small, often a few tenths).
\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]stop-gradient: the value is used but no gradient flows through it.
Common traps
  • Blurry does not mean the ELBO derivation is wrong. It is a property of the likelihood and the limited code; the sharpest likelihood-trained model still averages over what it cannot tell apart.
  • "Lower KL is always better." A KL of zero means the code is useless. You want the information the code carries to be the useful part, which is a judgement the objective does not make for you.
  • Larger β gives "disentanglement" for free. Claims that β-VAE separates factors of variation are empirical and depend on the data and the settings; the closed form above shows only the rate-distortion effect.
  • A big codebook is not automatically better. Unused codes waste capacity, and the effective number of codes is what counts.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Image and audio tokenisers. Quantised autoencoders turn pictures or sound into integer sequences so that a transformer can model and generate them like text (Module 9 builds on this).
  • Compression. The rate (bits per code) against distortion trade-off is the same one that governs learned image and audio codecs.
  • Latent spaces for diffusion. Autoencoders trained with a small KL or a quantiser provide the compressed space in which latent diffusion works.

Check yourself

1. For w = 1, s = 0.5, x = 2 and β = 4, what are the best σ² and the decoded value?
σ² = 1 / (1 + 1/(4 × 0.25)) = 1 / (1 + 1) = 0.5. μ = (1 × 2 / 0.25) / (4 + 1/0.25) = 8 / 8 = 1.0, so the decoded value is w·μ = 1.0 (squared error 1.0, KL 0.5966).
2. With codes 1.0 and 5.0667, which code does the vector 2.2 pick, and what is its squared quantisation error?
Distance to 1.0 is 1.2, to 5.0667 is 2.8667, so it picks 1.0. Squared error 1.2² = 1.44.
3. Why does a very powerful decoder tend to cause posterior collapse?
If the decoder can model x well without z, setting q(z | x) equal to the prior makes the KL term exactly zero while losing nothing in reconstruction, so the ELBO is highest when the code is ignored. The remedies (KL warm-up, free bits, a weaker decoder) all make using z worthwhile.

Takeaways

  • The KL term prices the information in the code: β sets the exchange rate between rate and distortion (best σ² = 1/(1 + w²/(β s²))).
  • Blur is an average over what the limited code cannot distinguish; collapse is the code being ignored (KL = 0).
  • A VQ-VAE snaps each vector to the nearest of K codes, trains with a straight-through gradient, and yields integer tokens that a transformer can model.
  • Watch the effective number of codes: unused codes are wasted.
7.3

The GAN objective

The questionA VAE needs a likelihood. Suppose your generator is a black box: you feed it random noise and it outputs an image, and you have no formula for the probability it assigns to any image. How could you possibly measure how close its output distribution is to the real data, and use that to improve it?

Intuition: a forger and a detective

A forger paints fake banknotes. A detective is shown a mixed pile of real and forged notes and must say which is which. Each learns from the other. When the detective catches a fake, the forger learns what gave it away and improves. When the forger improves, the detective must look harder. The game ends, in theory, when the forger's notes are indistinguishable from real ones and the detective can only guess (a coin flip). The forger never needs a formula for "how real" a note is; the detective's verdicts are the only measuring stick. This is a generative adversarial network (GAN): a generator G and a discriminator D trained against each other.

Build it up

The two players. The generator maps noise z (say standard normal) to a sample G(z); the distribution of those samples is called p_g. The discriminator takes a sample x and outputs D(x)\in(0,1), its estimate of the probability that x is real data (from p_{\text{data}}) rather than from the generator. A natural score for the discriminator is the log-probability it gives to the right answer: \ln D(x) on real data, \ln(1-D(x)) on fakes. Average and you get the value

V(D,G)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\ln D(x)\big]+\mathbb{E}_{z}\big[\ln\big(1-D(G(z))\big)\big],\qquad \min_G\max_D V(D,G)

The discriminator tries to make V large (classify correctly); the generator tries to make it small (fool it). This is the minimax game.

Step 1: the best discriminator for a fixed generator. Write both expectations as sums or integrals over x: V=\int\big[p_{\text{data}}(x)\ln D(x)+p_g(x)\ln(1-D(x))\big]dx. The discriminator chooses D(x) separately at each x, so maximise a\ln D+b\ln(1-D) with a=p_{\text{data}}(x), b=p_g(x). Setting the derivative a/D-b/(1-D)=0 gives

D^*(x)=\frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x)+p_g(x)}

This is just Bayes' rule for "real or fake?" with equal mix of real and fake samples: at each point, the fraction of real among all samples there. D^*=1 where only data live, D^*=\tfrac12 where the two densities agree, D^*=0 where only the generator lives.

Step 2: what the generator then faces. Substitute D^* into V. Let m=\tfrac12(p_{\text{data}}+p_g) be the 50/50 mixture. Then D^*=p_{\text{data}}/(2m) and 1-D^*=p_g/(2m), so

V(D^*,G)=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\!\Big[\ln\frac{p_{\text{data}}}{m}\Big]+\mathbb{E}_{p_g}\!\Big[\ln\frac{p_g}{m}\Big]-2\ln2=-\ln4+2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g),\qquad \mathrm{JS}(P\|Q)=\tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|m)+\tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|m)

The Jensen-Shannon divergence is a symmetric, smoothed KL; it is zero only when the distributions are equal and never exceeds \ln2. So if the discriminator were always optimal, the generator would be minimising a JS divergence between its distribution and the data, and the global optimum is p_g=p_{\text{data}}, with value -\ln4=-1.3863 and D^*=\tfrac12 everywhere.

Worked example: four values, everything computed

Let x\in\{1,2,3,4\}, p_{\text{data}}=(0.1,0.4,0.4,0.1), and a poor generator p_g=(0.4,0.1,0.1,0.4).

Optimal discriminator. D^*=(0.1/0.5,\,0.4/0.5,\,0.4/0.5,\,0.1/0.5)=(0.2,0.8,0.8,0.2).

Value. V(D^*,G)=\sum p_{\text{data}}\ln D^*+p_g\ln(1-D^*)=-1.0008.

JS check. The mixture is m=(0.25,0.25,0.25,0.25). D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}}\|m)=0.2\ln0.4+0.8\ln1.6=0.1927, and by symmetry the same for p_g, so \mathrm{JS}=0.1927. Then -\ln4+2\,\mathrm{JS}=-1.3863+0.3855=-1.0008, matching the direct value.

Other discriminators are worse. D=\tfrac12 everywhere gives -1.3863; D=(0.3,0.7,0.7,0.3) gives -1.0523; D=(0.1,0.9,0.9,0.1) gives -1.0896. All below -1.0008, the maximum. A perfect generator (p_g=p_{\text{data}}) would give \mathrm{JS}=0 and the best the discriminator could reach is -1.3863.

The loss you really train: non-saturating

Early in training the generator is poor, so the discriminator is confident: D(G(z)) is near 0. Look at the gradient of the generator's loss \ln(1-D) there. Write D=\sigma(a) with a the discriminator's logit. Then \partial_a\ln(1-\sigma(a))=-\sigma(a)=-D, which is nearly zero when D is nearly zero: the loss is flat exactly when the generator most needs a signal. It saturates. The standard fix is for the generator to minimise -\ln D(G(z)) instead. It has the same fixed point (it wants D(G(z)) to be large) but its gradient is \partial_a(-\ln\sigma(a))=-(1-D), which is near -1 when D is small. Numerically at a=-4 (D=0.018): the minimax slope is -0.018, the non-saturating slope -0.982. The non-saturating objective is no longer exactly the JS divergence, a theoretical price accepted for training signal.

The formal summary

G,\;p_gthe generator network and the distribution of its outputs G(z), z\sim p(z). We can sample from p_g but cannot evaluate it.
D(x)the discriminator's probability that x is real, in (0,1); D=\sigma(a) for a logit a.
V(D,G)the value: expected log-probability D assigns to the correct label.
D^*the maximising discriminator for fixed G: p_{\text{data}}/(p_{\text{data}}+p_g).
mthe equal mixture (p_{\text{data}}+p_g)/2.
\mathrm{JS}Jensen-Shannon divergence, 0\le\mathrm{JS}\le\ln2; V(D^*,G)=-\ln4+2\,\mathrm{JS}.

Cost: each training step needs a forward and backward pass through both networks, and the generator's gradient flows through the discriminator. There is no likelihood to evaluate, which is both the strength (any sampler works) and the weakness (no number says how good the model is; see 7.4). The vision-side picture is in GANs for images.

Common traps
  • The theory assumes an optimal discriminator and unlimited generator capacity. Real training alternates a few gradient steps with finite networks, so the JS picture is an idealisation, not a guarantee.
  • Confusing D with a probability of quality. D reports how real a sample looks to this discriminator, not how good the image is.
  • Believing the minimax loss is what people train. The non-saturating loss is the usual generator loss.
  • JS is not KL. JS is bounded by ln 2 even when the two distributions share no support; that boundedness will turn out to be a weakness (lesson 7.4).
Where it shows up in AI
  • GANs for image synthesis, super-resolution, image-to-image translation and data augmentation were the leading image generators for several years before diffusion.
  • Adversarial losses as components. Even in systems that are not GANs, a small discriminator loss is often added to a reconstruction loss (for instance in the autoencoders that compress images for latent diffusion) to sharpen the output.
  • Learned discriminators as detectors and critics turn up in domain-adaptation methods that make features indistinguishable between two domains.

Check yourself

1. At a point where p_data(x) = 0.3 and p_g(x) = 0.1, what is the optimal discriminator's output?
D*(x) = 0.3 / (0.3 + 0.1) = 0.75. Three quarters of the samples that land at x are real.
2. If the generator matches the data exactly, what is V(D*, G)?
JS = 0, so V = −ln 4 = −1.3863, and D* = ½ everywhere: the discriminator can do no better than guessing.
3. What are JS and V(D*, G) if the data and generator distributions have completely disjoint supports?
The mixture m is p_data/2 on the data's support and p_g/2 on the generator's, so each KL to m is ln 2, and JS = ln 2 = 0.6931. Then V = −ln 4 + 2 ln 2 = 0. The discriminator separates them perfectly (D* = 1 on data, 0 on fakes, so ln 1 + ln 1 = 0).

Takeaways

  • A GAN needs only samples: V(D,G) rewards D for classifying real against fake and G for fooling it.
  • For a fixed G the best discriminator is D* = p_data / (p_data + p_g) (checked: 0.2, 0.8, 0.8, 0.2).
  • Then V(D*, G) = −ln 4 + 2·JS (checked: −1.0008 both ways), minimised when p_g = p_data.
  • The saturating generator loss gives almost no gradient when D is confident; the non-saturating −ln D fixes the signal.
7.4

Training GANs

The questionThe theory of lesson 7.3 said the game has a clean optimum, yet practitioners describe GAN training as fragile: losses that oscillate instead of settling, generators that produce only a few kinds of output, and no single number that says whether the model is getting better. What goes wrong, which fixes exist, and why did diffusion models largely replace GANs for image synthesis?

Intuition: two people walking uphill and downhill on the same hill

Ordinary training has one person walking downhill on a fixed landscape: eventually they stop at a valley. A GAN has two people on a landscape that each one's step reshapes: one walks downhill, the other uphill on the same surface. They can chase each other around in circles forever, like two people dancing around a pole. Three separate troubles follow: the dynamics can circle or blow up, the generator can find a safe answer and cover only part of the data (mode collapse), and the distance the discriminator measures can go flat so that it tells the generator nothing about which way to move. Fixes exist for each, none is complete, and there is no loss curve to judge progress by.

Build it up

1. Instability: the simplest game that goes wrong. Take the smallest adversarial game: one player picks x to minimise xy, the other picks y to maximise it. The only equilibrium is (0,0). Gradient steps with step size \eta, taken simultaneously, are x\leftarrow x-\eta y and y\leftarrow y+\eta x. The squared distance from the equilibrium gets multiplied each step:

x'^2+y'^2=(x-\eta y)^2+(y+\eta x)^2=(1+\eta^2)\,(x^2+y^2)

(the cross terms cancel). So the point spirals outward, with radius growing as (1+\eta^2)^{t/2}, however small \eta is. That is not the GAN's exact dynamics, but it shows that "both players follow their gradient" can fail to converge even when a clean equilibrium exists. Two fixes in this toy: alternating updates (the second player sees the first's new value) keep the radius bounded but the orbit never shrinks; optimistic updates use x\leftarrow x-2\eta y_t+\eta y_{t-1} (a correction for where the opponent is heading) and spiral inward.

2. Mode collapse. Suppose the data have several clumps ("modes"). The generator is rewarded for outputs the discriminator calls real. If the discriminator currently likes one clump the most, the generator's best move is to put all its output there. Then the discriminator learns that clump is over-supplied and now prefers another; the generator jumps there. The generator hops between modes and never covers them all. A tiny abstraction shows this: three modes with data probabilities (0.5,0.3,0.2), a discriminator that is optimal at every step, and a generator that moves a fraction \alpha of its mass onto the mode with the highest D^*.

Worked example 2: the greedy generator

Start with p_g=(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13). Then D^*=(0.5,0.3,0.2)/(0.5,0.3,0.2+\tfrac13)=(0.600,0.474,0.375): mode 1 is the most under-supplied, so the generator moves there. With \alpha=0.9 the new p_g=(0.9333,0.0333,0.0333). Now D^*=(0.349,0.900,0.857): mode 1 is flooded, so the generator jumps to mode 2, giving p_g\approx(0.09,0.90,0.00), and the cycle continues 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3 and never matches (0.5,0.3,0.2). With \alpha=0.02 the same rule drifts gently toward the data mixture. This is an illustration of the mechanism; it is not a measurement of any real network.

3. The Wasserstein idea: a distance that does not go flat. In 7.3 the discriminator measures JS. Take data uniform on [0,1) and a generator uniform on [\theta,\theta+1). For \theta\ge1 the supports do not overlap, JS equals \ln2, and it stays \ln2 however close the generator gets. Its gradient with respect to \theta is zero: no direction to improve. The Wasserstein-1 (earth mover's) distance asks how far the probability mass must be carried to turn one distribution into the other; here that is simply \theta, a clean slope. In one dimension W_1=\int|F_P(x)-F_Q(x)|\,dx (the area between the two cumulative distribution functions), and in general, by the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality,

W_1(P,Q)=\sup_{\|f\|_{L}\le1}\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}[f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}[f(x)]

where the supremum is over 1-Lipschitz functions (functions whose slope never exceeds 1 in size). A Wasserstein GAN replaces the discriminator by such a critic f without a final sigmoid, trained to widen the gap in average score between real and generated data, while the generator tries to close it. The Lipschitz limit has to be enforced: by clipping the weights (the original, crude choice) or by adding a gradient penalty \lambda\,(\|\nabla f(\hat x)\|-1)^2 at points between real and fake samples. These help stability in many settings; they are not a cure-all.

4. Evaluating a GAN without a likelihood. A GAN offers no p_g(x), so held-out likelihood is unavailable. Common practice is to compare sample statistics. The Fréchet distance between feature distributions (the idea behind widely used image scores) passes real and generated samples through a fixed feature extractor, fits a Gaussian to each set of features, and computes

d^2=\|\mu_1-\mu_2\|^2+\operatorname{Tr}\!\big(\Sigma_1+\Sigma_2-2(\Sigma_1\Sigma_2)^{1/2}\big)

In one dimension this reduces to (\mu_1-\mu_2)^2+(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2; for \mathcal N(0,1) against \mathcal N(1,2^2) it is 1+1=2. Such scores depend on the feature extractor, on the number of samples (small sets bias them), and on the Gaussian assumption; they can miss mode dropping inside a class. Separate precision (do samples look real?) and recall (is the whole data distribution covered?) measures are used because a single score hides which one failed.

Why diffusion models overtook GANs

Many practitioners moved to diffusion models for image generation for reasons that are widely discussed rather than proven by one experiment: diffusion is trained with a simple regression loss that behaves like ordinary supervised learning, so training is far more stable; the loss decreases and is interpretable; it tends to cover all modes of a dataset; and it scales smoothly with data and compute and conditions naturally on text. GANs still sample in a single forward pass, which is much cheaper than iterative denoising, and distilled diffusion models, and GAN-style losses used to sharpen them, are partly motivated by that speed. Treat the ranking as a statement about current practice, which can change. Module 8 starts with the forward noising process.

Common traps
  • Reading the loss curves. The generator and discriminator losses move against each other; a low discriminator loss may mean a strong discriminator or a weak generator. They do not track sample quality the way a supervised loss tracks accuracy.
  • A good score from a single number. Fréchet-style scores can hide mode dropping and depend on the feature network and sample count.
  • "Wasserstein fixes everything". It gives a more informative distance, but the Lipschitz constraint has to be enforced approximately and training still needs care.
  • Assuming GANs are obsolete. They remain useful where one-step sampling matters, and adversarial losses are used inside other systems.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Image synthesis history: an era of GAN-based generators (and their training tricks: spectral normalisation, gradient penalties, progressive growing, two-time-scale updates) preceded the diffusion era.
  • Adversarial and optimistic updates appear wherever two objectives are optimised against each other, including multi-agent and game-theoretic learning.
  • Wasserstein and optimal-transport distances are used to compare distributions in domain adaptation and in some flow-matching variants (Module 8).

Takeaways

  • Two players following their own gradients can circle or spiral out (radius × √(1+η²) per step in the toy game); alternating and optimistic updates change that.
  • A generator that chases the current best discriminator tends to hop between modes instead of covering them all.
  • JS goes flat (ln 2) when supports do not overlap; Wasserstein keeps a slope, at the price of enforcing a Lipschitz critic.
  • With no likelihood, evaluation relies on feature-space scores whose limits you must know; diffusion largely replaced GANs through stable training and coverage, while GANs keep one-step speed.
7.5

Normalising flows

The questionVAEs give only a bound on the likelihood and GANs give none. Is there a generator whose likelihood is exact, so you can train it by plain maximum likelihood, evaluate the density of any data point, and still sample by pushing simple noise through a network?

Intuition: stretch a rubber sheet, and the ink must conserve its total

Spread a layer of ink evenly (or in a bell-shaped blob) on a rubber sheet. Now stretch the sheet unevenly. Where the sheet stretches, the ink thins out; where it is squeezed, the ink piles up. The total amount of ink never changes. A normalising flow does exactly this to a probability density: start with simple noise (a Gaussian), pass it through an invertible map that stretches and squeezes space, and read off the new density from how much the map stretched at each point. Because the map can be undone, you can also take any data point, push it back to the noise space and ask how likely it was there, then correct for the stretching. That correction is the change-of-variables formula, and it makes the likelihood exact.

Build it up

One dimension. Let z have density p_z and let x=f(z) with f strictly increasing (so it has an inverse). The probability that z lies in a tiny interval of width dz equals the probability that x lies in the image interval of width dx=f'(z)\,dz:

p_x(x)\,|dx|=p_z(z)\,|dz|\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_x(x)=\frac{p_z(z)}{|f'(z)|},\qquad z=f^{-1}(x)

If f'>1 the map stretches, so the density is divided by that factor and thins out; if f'<1 it squeezes and the density rises. (Equivalently p_x(x)=p_z(f^{-1}(x))\,|(f^{-1})'(x)|.)

Worked example 1: three maps of the standard normal

The standard normal density is \varphi(z)=e^{-z^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}, with \varphi(1)=0.2420.

Affine: x=2z+1, f'=2. At x=3 (z=1): p_x(3)=\varphi(1)/2=0.1210. The sheet is stretched twice, so the density is halved.

Exponential: x=e^z, f'=e^z=x, z=\ln x. Then p_x(x)=\varphi(\ln x)/x (the lognormal density). At x=2: \varphi(\ln2)=0.3137, so p_x(2)=0.3137/2=0.1569. Integrating p_x over x\in(0,\infty) numerically gives 0.999999: total probability still 1.

A non-linear increasing map: f(z)=z+1.5\tanh z, f'(z)=1+1.5(1-\tanh^2z). At z=1: x=f(1)=2.1424, f'(1)=1.6300, so p_x(2.1424)=0.2420/1.6300=0.1485. Inverting f numerically on a grid of x values from −40 to 40 and adding p_x\,dx gives 1.000000. This is the default of the lab below.

Several dimensions. The stretching factor of a map f:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d at a point is the absolute value of the determinant of its Jacobian matrix J_f (the matrix of all partial derivatives \partial f_i/\partial z_j): the factor by which small volumes are scaled. In logarithms, which is what you train with:

\ln p_x(x)=\ln p_z(z)-\ln\big|\det J_f(z)\big|,\qquad z=f^{-1}(x)

A flow chains several simple invertible maps, x=f_K\circ\cdots\circ f_1(z). The log-determinants simply add, \ln|\det J|=\sum_k\ln|\det J_{f_k}|, so each layer can be simple. The difficulty is design: for a general d\times d Jacobian the determinant costs about d^3 operations, far too slow for images with thousands of dimensions. Flow layers are built so the Jacobian is cheap.

Coupling layers. Split the coordinates into two parts. Keep the first part, and transform the second by a scale and shift that depend only on the first:

y_1=x_1,\qquad y_2=x_2\,e^{s(x_1)}+t(x_1)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad x_2=\big(y_2-t(y_1)\big)\,e^{-s(y_1)}

The inverse is just as cheap, and s and t can be arbitrary neural networks, because we never invert them. The Jacobian is triangular: the top-left block is 1, the top-right is 0, so its determinant is the product of the diagonal, e^{s(x_1)} (a product of exponentials in more dimensions). Hence \ln|\det J|=\sum s(x_1), essentially free. Stacking such layers while alternating which half is kept gives an expressive flow with an exact likelihood.

Worked example 2: a coupling layer on one point

Take x=(x_1,x_2)=(1,\,0.5) with s(u)=0.5u, t(u)=0.3u^2. Then s=0.5, t=0.3, and y_2=0.5\cdot e^{0.5}+0.3=0.5\times1.6487+0.3=1.1244, so y=(1,1.1244). The log-determinant is s(x_1)=0.5 (determinant e^{0.5}=1.6487): the layer stretches area by 1.65 there.

Suppose x comes from a 2-D standard normal: p_x(x)=\varphi(1)\varphi(0.5)=0.0852. The density at the new point is p_y(y)=p_x(x)/e^{0.5}=0.0852/1.6487=0.0517: area was stretched so density dropped. Computing p_y instead from the inverse map, \varphi(y_1)\,\varphi\big((y_2-t)/e^{s}\big)/e^{s}, gives the same 0.0517; the lab prints both, and checks the determinant against a numerical Jacobian.

Training and what flows can and cannot do

For a dataset \{x_i\} you maximise \sum_i\big[\ln p_z(f^{-1}(x_i))-\ln|\det J_f|\big] directly: it is an exact likelihood, with no lower bound (VAE) and no adversary (GAN). Sampling needs the forward direction (draw z, apply f) and density evaluation needs the inverse, so a flow has to be cheap in both. The price: the map must be invertible and keep the dimension the same (no bottleneck code), and the architecture constrains what it can express. Two cautions: for discrete data such as 8-bit pixels, a continuous density is only meaningful after adding small noise ("dequantisation"); and a high likelihood does not guarantee good samples, and flows have been reported to give high likelihood to data unlike their training set, so likelihood alone is a weak out-of-distribution detector.

Link forward. A flow of many small steps can be written as an ordinary differential equation dx/dt=v(x,t) (a "continuous flow"), whose log-density changes by minus the divergence of v. Flow matching (Module 8) trains such a velocity field by simple regression along paths between noise and data, avoiding the cost of exact likelihood training, and the diffusion samplers of fast samplers are the same kind of object.

The formal definition

zthe noise variable with a simple density p_z (standard normal).
x=f(z)the data-space variable produced by the invertible map f; f^{-1} maps data back to noise.
f'(z)the slope of f: how much a short interval is stretched.
J_f(z)the Jacobian matrix of f at z; |\det J_f| is the factor by which small volumes are scaled.
s(x_1),\,t(x_1)the log-scale and shift of a coupling layer, arbitrary functions of the kept coordinates.
\ln|\det J|log of the volume-scaling factor; sums over layers; equals \sum s(x_1) for a coupling layer.

Cost: each coupling layer costs one pass of the networks s,t, and its log-determinant is a sum of d/2 numbers, so training needs about the cost of a forward pass of the stack plus an inverse pass, with memory like an ordinary network of that depth.

Common traps
  • Forgetting the stretching factor. Substituting x = f(z) into p_z without dividing by |f′| gives a density that does not integrate to 1.
  • Non-invertible maps. If f is not one-to-one (z², ReLU), the formula fails; with several preimages you sum the contributions, and where f′ = 0 the density is infinite.
  • Using the wrong direction. The determinant in ln p_x is of the map from noise to data evaluated at z = f⁻¹(x); using its inverse flips the sign of the correction.
  • Equating likelihood with quality. Exact likelihood is a property of the model class; it does not by itself mean the samples look good.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Density estimation and exact likelihood models for tabular, scientific and low-dimensional data, where an exact density matters (anomaly scoring, simulation-based inference).
  • Flexible posteriors. A flow can be used as the encoder distribution of a VAE (replacing the Gaussian) to shrink the ELBO gap of lesson 7.1.
  • Continuous flows and flow matching. The change-of-variables idea reappears as the instantaneous change of variables in continuous-time generators (Module 8).

Check yourself

1. For x = e^z with z standard normal, what is p_x(2)?
p_x(x) = φ(ln x)/x. φ(ln 2) = φ(0.6931) = 0.3137, so p_x(2) = 0.3137 / 2 = 0.1569.
2. For x = 3z with z standard normal, what is p_x(3)?
z = 1, f′ = 3, so p_x(3) = φ(1)/3 = 0.2420/3 = 0.0807.
3. A coupling layer has s(x₁) = −0.4 at some point. What is its log-determinant, and by what factor does it scale area there?
The log-determinant is s(x₁) = −0.4, so the area factor is e^(−0.4) = 0.6703: area shrinks by about a third and the density there rises by 1/0.6703 = 1.49.

Takeaways

  • For an increasing map x = f(z): p_x(x) = p_z(z)/|f′(z)|. Checked: the tanh-map density integrates to 1.000000.
  • In d dimensions ln p_x(x) = ln p_z(z) − ln|det J_f(z)|, and log-determinants add across layers.
  • Coupling layers (y₂ = x₂ e^s(x₁) + t(x₁)) have triangular Jacobians, so the determinant is e^s and the inverse is cheap.
  • Flows give exact likelihood and one-pass sampling, at the price of invertibility and equal dimension; continuous flows lead to flow matching.
