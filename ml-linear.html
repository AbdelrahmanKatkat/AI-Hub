2.1

Linear regression

The questionFive flats: size (in 10 m² units) and rent (in 100 €): (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 4), (5, 6). A new flat of size 6 appears. What rent do we predict, and — more importantly — what does "the best straight line through these points" even mean, so that two people always draw the same line?

Intuition

Imagine a stiff ruler laid over the dots. Wherever the ruler is not touching a dot there is a vertical gap — the residual. A good line has small gaps overall. But "overall" needs a rule, because gaps above and below the line would cancel if we simply added them. The standard rule: square each gap and add them up. Squaring makes every gap positive and punishes one big miss more than several small ones. The line with the smallest total squared gap is the least-squares line.

There are two ways to find it. You can solve for it directly with algebra (the normal equation), or you can walk to it: start with any line, measure which way a small change in slope and intercept lowers the total squared gap, and step that way (gradient descent). On this tiny problem both reach the same line — the lab below shows them side by side.

Build it up

The model is \hat y=w x+b: slope w, intercept b. With several features it becomes \hat y=w_1x_1+\dots+w_dx_d+b=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+b. A trick removes b: append a constant feature 1 to every example, so \hat y=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x with \mathbf w=(b,w_1,\dots). Stack the examples as rows of a matrix X (n rows, one per example) and the predictions of all examples are \hat{\mathbf y}=X\mathbf w.

Loss. The mean squared error is L(\mathbf w)=\frac1n\sum_i(\hat y_i-y_i)^2=\frac1n\lVert X\mathbf w-\mathbf y\rVert^2.

Gradient (derivation). Differentiate one term with the chain rule (partial derivatives and the gradient): \frac{\partial}{\partial w_j}(\hat y_i-y_i)^2=2(\hat y_i-y_i)\,x_{ij}. Summing over examples and collecting all j into one vector:

\nabla L=\frac2n X^\top(X\mathbf w-\mathbf y)

Read it as: "for every feature, take the correlation between that feature and the current errors". For the line, \partial L/\partial w=\frac2n\sum_i e_ix_i and \partial L/\partial b=\frac2n\sum_i e_i with e_i=\hat y_i-y_i.

Normal equation. At the minimum the gradient is zero: X^\top(X\mathbf w-\mathbf y)=0\Rightarrow X^\top X\,\mathbf w=X^\top\mathbf y, so, if X^\top X is invertible (no feature is a copy or combination of the others, and n ≥ d+1):

\mathbf w^\star=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y

Because L is a bowl (convex), this single stationary point is the global minimum.

Worked example · normal equation on 5 points

Rows of X are (1, x): (1,1),(1,2),(1,3),(1,4),(1,5); y = (2,3,5,4,6).

X^\top X=\begin{pmatrix}5&15\\15&55\end{pmatrix} (5 = n, 15 = Σx, 55 = Σx²), X^\top\mathbf y=\begin{pmatrix}20\\69\end{pmatrix} (20 = Σy, 69 = Σxy = 2+6+15+16+30).

Determinant = 5·55 − 15·15 = 50, so (X^\top X)^{-1}=\frac1{50}\begin{pmatrix}55&-15\\-15&5\end{pmatrix}.

b=\frac{55\cdot20-15\cdot69}{50}=\frac{65}{50}=1.3,\qquad w=\frac{-15\cdot20+5\cdot69}{50}=\frac{45}{50}=0.9. The line is ŷ = 1.3 + 0.9x; for size 6 the prediction is 1.3 + 5.4 = 6.7.

Residuals y − ŷ: (−0.2, −0.1, 1.0, −0.9, 0.2); they sum to 0 (always, when there is an intercept). SSE = 0.04+0.01+1+0.81+0.04 = 1.9, MSE = 0.38. Total variation SST = Σ(y−ȳ)² = 10 (ȳ = 4). R^2=1-\mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}=0.81: the line explains 81 % of the variation in rent.

Worked example · one gradient-descent step from (w, b) = (0, 0)

All predictions are 0, so MSE₀ = (4+9+25+16+36)/5 = 18. \partial L/\partial w=\frac25\sum(-y_ix_i)=-0.4\cdot69=-27.6, \partial L/\partial b=-0.4\cdot20=-8. With η = 0.02: w ← 0 + 0.552, b ← 0 + 0.16, and the MSE drops from 18 to 5.39. Repeating this walks the pair toward (0.9, 1.3). The two knobs live in a 2-D "loss map"; the lab draws it.

The formal definition

\hat{\mathbf y}=X\mathbf w,\quad L=\tfrac1n\lVert X\mathbf w-\mathbf y\rVert^2,\quad \nabla L=\tfrac2nX^\top(X\mathbf w-\mathbf y),\quad \mathbf w^\star=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}
Xn × (d+1) design matrix: one row per example, a leading column of 1s for the intercept
wweights; each w_j is the change in prediction when feature j rises by 1 and the others stay fixed
Lmean squared error, the number gradient descent lowers
∇Lgradient: the direction of steepest increase; we move the opposite way
R²fraction of the variance of y explained; 1 = perfect, 0 = no better than predicting ȳ, negative = worse than ȳ

Why squares? If the errors are Gaussian noise, minimising squared error is exactly maximum likelihood (MLE). Assumptions behind the interpretation: the relation is roughly linear in the features, residuals are independent with similar spread, no feature is a near-copy of another (multicollinearity makes individual w unstable), and no wild outliers (one far point drags the line, because its squared gap is huge).

Common traps
  • High R² does not mean a correct model. Always plot the residuals; curvature in them means the line is wrong. Adding features always raises training R².
  • Extrapolation. A line fitted on sizes 1–5 says nothing reliable about size 50.
  • Reading w as causal. A weight describes association in this dataset, holding other features fixed.
  • Singular XᵀX. Two identical or perfectly dependent columns make the inverse not exist; use regularisation (2.2) or lstsq/SVD, never an explicit inverse in production.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Baseline and calibration layers: price, demand and risk models start here; scikit-learn LinearRegression, Ridge, SGD solvers.
  • Every neural network's last layer for regression is exactly \mathbf w^\top\mathbf h+b with the squared loss; "linear probes" test what features a frozen network contains.
  • Gradient descent here is the same loop that trains deep networks, only the model is simpler (gradient descent).

Check yourself

1. For the fitted line ŷ = 1.3 + 0.9x, predict the rent for size 6 and give the residual if the true rent is 7.
ŷ = 1.3 + 0.9·6 = 6.7; residual y − ŷ = 7 − 6.7 = 0.3.
2. Why is the sum of residuals exactly 0 for a least-squares line with an intercept?
The b-component of the normal equations, ∂L/∂b = 0, says Σ(ŷᵢ − yᵢ) = 0. In our example −0.2−0.1+1−0.9+0.2 = 0.
3. What goes wrong with the normal equation if all five flats had the same size?
The columns (1, x) become proportional, XᵀX has determinant 0 and is not invertible: infinitely many lines fit equally well (the slope is undetermined). Regularisation or a pseudo-inverse picks one.

Takeaways

  • Linear regression minimises the mean squared residual; the gradient is (2/n)Xᵀ(Xw − y).
  • If XᵀX is invertible, w = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy; otherwise or at scale, use gradient descent or SVD-based solvers.
  • R² measures explained variance; check residuals, not only R².
  • A model linear in its weights can still fit curves by adding features.
2.2

Polynomials, overfitting & regularisation

The questionA straight line cannot follow a curved relation, so we give the model extra features (x², x³, …). With 14 data points and a degree-13 polynomial the curve passes through every point exactly — training error zero. Yet it predicts new points terribly. How do we get the flexibility of a rich model without letting it memorise noise?

Intuition

Model flexibility is a dial. Too low and the model underfits (high bias: it cannot bend to the truth). Too high and it overfits (high variance: it also bends to every random wiggle in this sample, so a different sample would give a very different curve). A flexible curve forced through noise needs huge, opposing weights to make sharp turns — big coefficients are the symptom. Regularisation puts a price on large weights: the model may keep its flexibility but must pay to use it, so it uses only what the data really supports.

Build it up

Polynomial features. Replace x by (x, x², …, x^d). The model \hat y=w_0+w_1x+\dots+w_dx^d is still linear in w, so everything from lesson 2.1 applies with new columns in X. Degree is a flexibility dial.

Ridge (L2). Add the squared length of the weights to the loss:

L_{\text{ridge}}=\tfrac12\lVert X\mathbf w-\mathbf y\rVert^2+\tfrac\lambda2\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2^2

Setting the gradient to zero: X^\top(X\mathbf w-\mathbf y)+\lambda\mathbf w=0\Rightarrow(X^\top X+\lambda I)\mathbf w=X^\top\mathbf y:

\mathbf w_{\text{ridge}}=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y

Adding λ to the diagonal makes the matrix always invertible (for λ > 0) and shrinks every weight. In the SVD view (SVD), a direction with singular value s is shrunk by the factor s^2/(s^2+\lambda): weak, noisy directions (small s) are damped most. From the Bayesian side, ridge is MAP estimation with a Gaussian prior on w (MAP).

Worked example · shrinkage on the five flats

Centre the data: x − 3 = (−2,−1,0,1,2), y − 4 = (−2,−1,1,0,2). Then Sxx = Σ(x−x̄)² = 10 and Sxy = Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ) = 9, so the one-weight ridge solution is w=\dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}+\lambda} (the intercept is not penalised):

The slope shrinks smoothly toward 0 but never reaches it. It is a trade: a little bias (the slope is too small) for lower variance.

Lasso (L1) and why it gives exact zeros. Penalise the sum of absolute values instead: L_{\text{lasso}}=\tfrac12\lVert X\mathbf w-\mathbf y\rVert^2+\lambda\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_1. Geometry. Minimising the loss subject to a penalty budget is the same as finding where the loss's elliptical contours first touch the budget region. The L2 region is a round disc: the ellipse touches it at a generic point, rarely on an axis. The L1 region is a diamond with corners on the axes; the expanding ellipse most often meets a corner first, and at a corner a coordinate equals zero exactly. In many dimensions the L1 ball has many such corners and edges, so many weights are zero at once. Algebra, one weight. Minimise \tfrac12(w-z)^2+\lambda|w|, where z is the unpenalised solution. For w > 0 the derivative is w − z + λ = 0, giving w = z − λ (valid only if that is positive). For w < 0 it is w = z + λ. If |z| ≤ λ neither side has a stationary point, and the minimum sits at the kink w = 0:

w^\star=S_\lambda(z)=\operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z|-\lambda,\,0)\qquad\text{(soft threshold)}

Compare ridge, \tfrac12(w-z)^2+\tfrac\lambda2w^2\Rightarrow w=z/(1+\lambda): it divides, so it can shrink but never reach zero.

Worked example · soft threshold

With λ = 1: z = 3 gives lasso w = 2, ridge w = 1.5. z = 0.8 gives lasso w = 0 (|0.8| ≤ 1), ridge w = 0.4. On the five flats, w=S_\lambda(S_{xy})/S_{xx}=\max(9-\lambda,0)/10: λ = 5 → 0.4, λ = 9 → 0 (the feature is dropped).

Elastic net mixes the two penalties, \lambda(\alpha\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_1+\frac{1-\alpha}2\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2^2): it keeps sparsity but handles groups of correlated features better (lasso tends to pick one of a correlated group arbitrarily; ridge spreads weight among them).

Choosing λ. Never by training error (it always prefers λ = 0). Hold out validation data or cross-validate (Module 5) and pick the λ with the lowest validation error — the "U" in the lab's error curve. Scale features first: the penalty treats all weights alike, so a feature measured in millimetres would be penalised differently from the same feature in metres.

Common traps
  • Penalising the intercept or forgetting to standardise: the penalty then depends on units.
  • Selecting λ on the test set. Use validation or cross-validation; keep the test set for one final check.
  • Lasso is not a causal feature selector: among correlated features it picks one arbitrarily and the choice changes with the sample.
  • Polynomials of high degree on raw x explode at the edges; prefer splines or scaled features.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Weight decay in neural networks is L2 regularisation (AdamW applies it to the weights each step).
  • Sparse linear models (lasso, L1 logistic regression) for genomics and text, where features outnumber examples (p ≫ n).
  • Elastic net in glmnet/scikit-learn; the L2 penalty also stabilises ill-conditioned problems (2.5).

Check yourself

1. With the five flats (centred Sxx = 10, Sxy = 9), what slope does ridge give for λ = 5? Lasso?
Ridge: 9/(10+5) = 0.6. Lasso: (9 − 5)/10 = 0.4 (using the ½-loss convention above).
2. Why can ridge never set a weight exactly to zero but lasso can?
Ridge's solution z/(1+λ) is a nonzero scaling of z. The L1 penalty has a kink at 0; if |z| ≤ λ the loss slope cannot beat the penalty slope on either side, so 0 is the minimum. Geometrically, the L1 ball has corners on the axes.
3. Training error keeps falling as you add polynomial degree. What should you watch instead, and what shape does it have?
The validation (or cross-validated) error: it falls, reaches a minimum, then rises again as the model overfits (a U shape).

Takeaways

  • Polynomial features keep the model linear in w; degree controls flexibility.
  • Ridge: w = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy, shrinks all weights, always solvable. Lasso: soft-threshold, exact zeros, sparse models.
  • Choose λ on validation data after scaling features.
  • Large weights are the signature of overfitting.
2.3

Logistic regression

The questionWe want the probability that an email is spam, a loan defaults, a tumour is malignant. A line ŷ = w·x + b can output −3 or 7, which are not probabilities. How can we keep the idea "a weighted sum of the evidence" yet end up with a number between 0 and 1 that we can trust?

Intuition

Think of the weighted sum as evidence: every feature pushes toward "yes" (positive weight) or "no" (negative weight), and the total can be any real number. We need a smooth gate that turns "total evidence" into a probability: very negative → near 0, zero → exactly ½, very positive → near 1. The S-shaped sigmoid is that gate. Why this one? Because it is what you get if you let the evidence be the log-odds.

Odds and log-odds. If p = 0.8, the odds are 0.8 : 0.2 = 4 to 1. Odds run from 0 to ∞; log-odds z=\ln\frac p{1-p} run over the entire real line, with z = 0 meaning p = ½ and symmetry (z and −z give p and 1−p). A quantity that can be any real number is exactly what a linear function produces. So: assume the log-odds are linear in the features.

Try this
  1. Drag z: p follows the sigmoid. At z = 0, p = 0.5; at z = ±2, p ≈ 0.88 / 0.12; the curve flattens, so extra evidence matters less when you are already confident.
  2. Set w = 0.7 and raise x by 1: the odds are multiplied by e^0.7 ≈ 2.01 wherever you start, while the change in p depends on the starting point.

Build it up

The model. \ln\frac{p}{1-p}=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+b=z. Solving for p: \frac p{1-p}=e^z\Rightarrow p=\frac{e^z}{1+e^z}=\sigma(z)=\frac1{1+e^{-z}}.

Decision boundary. Predict class 1 when p > ½, i.e. z > 0. The boundary \mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+b=0 is a straight line (plane in higher dimensions); w points toward the "yes" side, and its length controls how sharp the transition is.

Likelihood → cross-entropy (derivation). Each label is a coin with probability p: P(y\mid\mathbf x)=p^{y}(1-p)^{1-y} (for y = 1 this is p, for y = 0 it is 1−p). Assuming independent examples, the likelihood of the whole dataset is the product, and the best weights maximise it (maximum likelihood). Products are awkward, so take the log and flip the sign to get something to minimise:

L(\mathbf w,b)=-\frac1n\sum_i\Big[y_i\ln p_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\Big]

This is the binary cross-entropy (cross-entropy). It charges −ln p for a confident-wrong or unsure "yes" and is convex in w, so gradient descent finds the global optimum.

Gradient (derivation). Use \sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))=p(1-p). For one example, \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z}=-\frac y p\,p(1-p)+\frac{1-y}{1-p}\,p(1-p)=-y(1-p)+(1-y)p=p-y. Since \partial z/\partial w_j=x_j:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j}=\frac1n\sum_i(p_i-y_i)\,x_{ij},\qquad\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=\frac1n\sum_i(p_i-y_i)

Remarkably the same shape as in linear regression — "error times input" — with the error being predicted probability minus label.

Worked example · one gradient step on 3 points

One feature: x = 0.5 (y = 0), x = 1.5 (y = 1), x = 3 (y = 1). Start at w = 0, b = 0, so z = 0 and every p = 0.5. Loss = −ln 0.5 = 0.6931.

Errors p − y: (0.5, −0.5, −0.5). \partial L/\partial w=\frac13(0.5\cdot0.5-0.5\cdot1.5-0.5\cdot3)=\frac{-2}{3}=-0.667. \partial L/\partial b=\frac13(0.5-0.5-0.5)=-0.1667.

With η = 1: w = 0.6667, b = 0.1667. New probabilities σ(0.6667·x + 0.1667) = 0.622, 0.763, 0.897 (for y = 0, 1, 1). The loss falls to 0.4512. The model already leans in the right direction; its boundary −b/w is at x = −0.25 and will move as training continues.

Reading coefficients as odds ratios. Raising feature j by 1 adds w_j to the log-odds, so it multiplies the odds by e^{w_j}: w = 0.7 → odds ×2.01; w = −0.5 → ×0.61; w = 0 → ×1. Probabilities do not change by a fixed amount, but odds ratios do. Compare coefficients across features only after scaling the features.

Regularisation. If the classes are perfectly separable, the loss keeps decreasing as the weights grow without bound (the sigmoid is pushed toward 0/1), so training never stops. Adding an L2 (or L1) penalty, exactly as in lesson 2.2, keeps weights finite; scikit-learn's default LogisticRegression already does this (parameter C = 1/λ). Non-linear boundaries come from adding polynomial features, as the lab's "Features" menu shows.

Common traps
  • Logistic regression is a classifier despite its name; the "regression" is on the log-odds.
  • Interpreting e^w as a probability change. It is a change in odds.
  • Using accuracy on imbalanced data: choose the threshold from costs, not always ½ (Module 5).
  • Forgetting the separable case: infinite weights without regularisation.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Credit scoring, medical risk scores, click-through-rate prediction (the production baseline in ad systems).
  • The final layer of every binary neural classifier is a sigmoid on a linear layer, trained with this same cross-entropy.
  • Linear probes and text classifiers over TF-IDF features.

Check yourself

1. What probability corresponds to log-odds z = 1.2? What are the odds?
p = σ(1.2) = 0.7685; the odds are e^1.2 = 3.32 to 1.
2. A coefficient is w = 0.7. By what factor do the odds change when the feature rises by 1? By 2?
e^0.7 = 2.01; for 2 units e^1.4 = 4.06 (effects multiply).
3. In the worked example, why is the gradient for w negative at the start, and what does a negative gradient imply?
The positive examples (y = 1) have larger x and p − y = −0.5 for them, outweighing the one y = 0 example; ∂L/∂w = −0.667 < 0 means raising w lowers the loss, so descent increases w.

Takeaways

  • Assume log-odds are linear; the sigmoid turns them into probabilities.
  • Maximum likelihood gives binary cross-entropy; the gradient is (p − y)x averaged.
  • e^w is the odds ratio per unit of a feature; the decision boundary is linear.
  • Regularise, especially with separable data or many features.
2.4

Multi-class classification

The questionA handwritten digit is one of 10 classes; a news article is one of 20 topics. Logistic regression separates two classes. How do we get 10 probabilities that are all positive, sum to 1, and are trained by the same simple gradient?

Intuition

Give every class its own weighted sum ("score" or logit): z_k=\mathbf w_k^\top\mathbf x+b_k. Higher score = more evidence for that class. Now turn the list of scores into probabilities: exponentiate each (making it positive and amplifying differences) and divide by the total (making it sum to 1). That is softmax. The two-class case collapses back to the sigmoid, so softmax regression is logistic regression generalised.

Two ways to go multi-class. One-vs-rest trains K separate binary classifiers ("is it class k or anything else?") and picks the most confident — simple, works with any binary model, but the K probabilities do not sum to one and each classifier sees an imbalanced problem. Multinomial (softmax) trains all K sets of weights jointly with one loss, giving coherent probabilities. For linear models, multinomial is usually the better default and is what neural networks use.

Build it up

p_k=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf z)_k=\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}

Loss. With one-hot label \mathbf y (1 at the true class c), the likelihood of an example is p_c, so the loss is \ell=-\ln p_c=-\sum_k y_k\ln p_k (categorical cross-entropy).

Gradient (derivation). \ell=-z_c+\ln\sum_je^{z_j}. Then \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z_k}=-\mathbb 1[k=c]+\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_je^{z_j}}=p_k-y_k. Since z_k=\mathbf w_k^\top\mathbf x+b_k:

\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf w_k}=(p_k-y_k)\,\mathbf x,\qquad\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial b_k}=p_k-y_k

Same "error × input" shape again, one row of weights per class: weights of the true class move toward x, weights of the others move away in proportion to how much probability they stole.

Worked example · one example, three classes

Scores z = (2, 1, 0.1); the true class is the second one, y = (0, 1, 0). Exponentials: (7.389, 2.718, 1.105), sum 11.2125. Probabilities p = (0.659, 0.242, 0.099) (sum 1). Loss = −ln 0.242 = 1.417.

Gradient with respect to the scores: p − y = (0.659, −0.758, 0.099). For the input x = (1, 2) the weight gradients are (p_k − y_k)·x: class 1: (0.659, 1.318), class 2: (−0.758, −1.516), class 3: (0.099, 0.197). A descent step lowers the weights of class 1 (it wrongly dominates), raises those of class 2, and slightly lowers class 3.

Numerical stability. e^{1000} overflows. Because softmax is unchanged when the same constant is subtracted from every score, compute with z_k-\max_j z_j (see stable softmax). The same shift makes the weights non-unique (adding a constant vector to all \mathbf w_k changes nothing), which regularisation or fixing one class to 0 resolves. Decision regions are where one score is largest; the boundary between two classes is the line (\mathbf w_a-\mathbf w_b)^\top\mathbf x+(b_a-b_b)=0, so regions are convex polygons.

Common traps
  • Softmax outputs are not reliable uncertainty by default; neural networks are often over-confident (calibration, Module 5).
  • Applying softmax then log separately: use a fused log-softmax / cross-entropy for stability.
  • Using softmax when classes are not mutually exclusive (a photo with both "dog" and "park"): use independent sigmoids (multi-label).
Where it shows up in AI
  • The output layer of nearly every classification network and of language models: next-token prediction is a softmax over the vocabulary (tens of thousands of classes).
  • Attention weights are a softmax over scores; the p − y gradient is why logits train so cleanly under cross-entropy.

Check yourself

1. Scores (1, 1, 1): what are the probabilities? What if you add 5 to every score?
(1/3, 1/3, 1/3) in both cases: softmax is unchanged by adding a constant to all scores.

Takeaways

  • Softmax turns K scores into probabilities; with cross-entropy its gradient w.r.t. the scores is p − y.
  • Multinomial training is joint and coherent; one-vs-rest is simpler but its outputs do not sum to 1.
  • Regions are convex polygons bounded by straight lines.
2.5

Training linear models in practice

The questionYou train a linear model on flats with two features: area in m² (50 to 150) and number of rooms (1 to 4). With a learning rate that works perfectly on a toy problem, the loss crawls for hundreds of thousands of steps — or explodes. Why, and what is the one-line fix?

Intuition

Picture the loss as a bowl over the weight space. If both features have the same scale the bowl is round, and the steepest-descent direction points straight at the bottom. If one feature is measured in much bigger numbers, a small change in its weight changes the predictions a lot, so the bowl becomes a long narrow valley. Steepest descent then points mostly across the valley wall, not along it: it bounces from side to side and creeps toward the minimum. And the learning rate is limited by the steep direction (go too far and you leap over the valley and diverge) while progress along the flat direction is slow. The ratio of steepness to flatness is the condition number.

Build it up

Batch, stochastic and mini-batch. Batch gradient descent uses all n examples per step: exact gradient, cost O(nd) per step, smooth. Stochastic (SGD) uses one random example: a very noisy but unbiased gradient costing O(d); you take n times more steps per pass over the data and make rapid early progress, but the path jitters and a decaying learning rate is needed to settle. Mini-batch uses B examples (32–1024): noise falls as 1/√B, hardware vectorises well — the default everywhere. One pass over the data is an epoch.

Quadratic analysis. Near its minimum, the MSE loss is exactly a quadratic \tfrac12\mathbf e^\top H\mathbf e, where e = w − w* and the Hessian is H=\frac2nX^\top X (\frac1nX^\top X if the loss carries a factor ½, as in the example below; κ is the same). Gradient descent updates \mathbf e\leftarrow(I-\eta H)\mathbf e. In the eigen-direction with eigenvalue \lambda_i the error is multiplied each step by 1-\eta\lambda_i. That requires |1-\eta\lambda_i|<1 for all i:

0<\eta<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}},\qquad\text{best }\eta=\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}},\ \ \text{error shrinks by }\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\text{ per step},\ \ \kappa=\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}}

So the number of steps to reduce the error by a factor 10⁴ is about \frac{\kappa}{2}\ln 10^4\approx4.6\,\kappa.

Worked example · the effect of scaling

Five flats, area (50, 80, 100, 120, 150) m² and rooms (1, 2, 3, 3, 4). After centring, the matrix \frac1nX^\top X has variance 1160 for area, 1.04 for rooms and covariance 34. Its eigenvalues are 1161.0 and 0.0434, so κ ≈ 26 744. The stable learning rate for the loss \frac12\cdot\frac1n\lVert X\mathbf w-\mathbf y\rVert^2 is below 2/1161 ≈ 0.0017, and about 4.6·26 744 ≈ 123 000 steps are needed for a 10⁴-fold error reduction.

Standardise each feature (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation: area std 34.06, rooms std 1.02). The matrix becomes the correlation matrix with off-diagonal r = 0.979, eigenvalues 1 ± r, so \kappa=\frac{1+r}{1-r}\approx93.7 and about 430 steps — a 280-fold speed-up from one line of preprocessing. The remaining κ comes from the strong correlation between area and rooms; ridge regularisation (adding λ to every eigenvalue) reduces it further: κ becomes (1160.99 + λ)/(0.0434 + λ) on the raw data.

Convergence checks. Plot the training loss against the step: it should fall monotonically for batch descent (a rise means η is too big). Stop when (a) the relative change in loss over several steps is below a tolerance, (b) the gradient norm is near 0, or (c) the validation loss stops improving (early stopping — which is itself a regulariser). In SGD, decay η over time (e.g. η₀/(1 + t/τ)) or use momentum/Adam (optimisers).

Common traps
  • Fitting the scaler on all data (including validation/test) leaks information; fit on the training set only.
  • Not scaling before any penalty or gradient method; the normal equation alone is scale-free but regularised solutions are not.
  • Tuning η on the test set, or judging convergence on only a few steps of a noisy SGD loss.
  • Believing a smaller η is always safer: it is safe but may need far more steps than your budget.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Feature standardisation is the first step of nearly every scikit-learn pipeline; its deep-learning cousins are batch/layer normalisation, which keep layer curvature well-conditioned.
  • Mini-batch SGD with momentum or Adam, learning-rate schedules and early stopping train every neural network; the linear case is the clearest place to see why they are needed.
  • L-BFGS, liblinear and SAGA are the solvers behind scikit-learn's logistic regression.

Check yourself

1. The curvature eigenvalues are 100 and 1. What is the largest stable learning rate, and what is κ?
η < 2/100 = 0.02; κ = 100/1 = 100. The best fixed η = 2/101 ≈ 0.0198 shrinks the error by 99/101 ≈ 0.98 per step.
2. Why is the loss of SGD noisy even near the optimum, and what fixes it?
Each step uses the gradient of a random sample, which fluctuates around the true gradient. Decay the learning rate, enlarge the mini-batch, or average the iterates.
3. You standardise with the mean and std of the whole dataset before splitting. What is wrong?
The validation/test rows influenced the scaling parameters: a small information leak that makes validation scores optimistic. Fit μ and σ on the training split only and reuse them.

Takeaways

  • Gradient descent on a quadratic converges iff η < 2/λmax, at a speed set by κ = λmax/λmin.
  • Standardise features: it often cuts κ by orders of magnitude, and a regulariser reduces it more.
  • Mini-batches trade gradient noise for speed; monitor the loss, use early stopping.
  • Pick the solver by problem size and penalty: closed form, L-BFGS, coordinate descent or SGD.
