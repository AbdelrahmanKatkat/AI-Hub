9.1

Data, labelling & augmentation

The questionYou have 1,000 photos of production-line parts, and only 50 show a defect. You cannot afford 10,000 more. How do you make a model that finds defects reliably — and how do you know your labels are even right?

Intuition

A model learns whatever regularities its data contains, including accidental ones. If every defect photo was taken in the afternoon, the model may learn "afternoon light = defect". So the first job is the dataset design: it must look like the world the model will meet, be split so that test images are truly unseen (split by part or session, not by random frame, or near-duplicates leak across the split), and be labelled consistently.

Labels come in several types, each costing more per image: a class tag (seconds), a bounding box (≈10–30 s), a polygon / pixel mask (minutes), keypoints. Pick the cheapest label that answers the product question.

Augmentation is a cheap trick with a precise idea: apply a change that you know does not alter the label (a flipped cat is still a cat, a slightly darker part is still defective) and train on the changed image. The model sees a new picture every epoch, which teaches it the invariances you chose.

Build it up

Augmentation policies. A policy is a list of random operations with ranges: flip with probability 0.5; rotate by a random angle in ±15°; random crop keeping at least 70% of the side; brightness ×(1±0.3); Gaussian noise σ. Each epoch every image gets fresh random draws. Crucially, a policy must respect the task: flipping left/right is wrong for reading text or telling a left shoe from a right one; heavy rotation is wrong for "which way is up" tasks.

Worked example — how many different images does a policy make?

Take a 224×224 image, a crop with side ≥ 70% (≥ 157 px), flip on, rotation ±15° in whole degrees. Square crop windows of side s can sit in (225-s)^2 positions, so the count is \sum_{s=157}^{224}(225-s)^2=\sum_{k=1}^{68}k^2=\frac{68\cdot69\cdot137}{6}=107{,}134. Multiply by 2 (flip) and by 31 (rotations −15…15): 6,642,308 geometric variants of one photo, before brightness and noise (continuous). 1,000 images → billions of possible inputs. They are strongly correlated (neighbours look alike), so this is not "billions of independent images", but it is why augmentation fights overfitting so well.

Class imbalance. With 950 normal and 50 defective images, a model that says "normal" always scores 95% accuracy and is useless. Three fixes, usually combined: (1) resample (show defects more often, augmented differently each time); (2) weight the loss; (3) change the loss itself (focal loss).

Weighted loss. Give class c the weight w_c=\dfrac{N}{K\,n_c} (N images, K classes, n_c images in class c).

Worked example — weights

N=1000, K=2: w_{\text{normal}}=\frac{1000}{2\cdot950}=0.526, w_{\text{defect}}=\frac{1000}{2\cdot50}=10. One missed defect now costs 19× as much as one false alarm, so total "importance" of both classes is equal: 950·0.526 = 50·10 = 500. A softer alternative uses the effective number of samples E_n=\frac{1-\beta^n}{1-\beta}: with β=0.999, n=50 gives 48.8 (almost all 50 count) while n=950 gives 613 (redundancy is discounted).

Focal loss. Cross-entropy for the true class with predicted probability p_t is -\ln p_t. In a dataset dominated by easy examples, thousands of easy cases each contribute a tiny loss but together swamp the few hard ones. Focal loss multiplies by (1-p_t)^\gamma to switch easy examples off.

Worked example — focal loss, γ = 2

Easy example, p_t=0.9: CE = −ln 0.9 = 0.1054; factor (0.1)² = 0.01 → FL = 0.00105 (100× smaller). Medium, p_t=0.5: CE = 0.6931, factor 0.25 → 0.1733 (4× smaller). Hard, p_t=0.1: CE = 2.3026, factor 0.81 → 1.8651 (barely changed). Now a thousand easy examples contribute 1.05 in total — comparable to ONE hard example (1.87) instead of 105 vs 2.3. With the usual α_t = 0.25 everything is scaled again (hard case: 0.466).

Label noise. Real labels are wrong some of the time (tired annotators, ambiguous cases). If a fraction η of labels are flipped at random, a model with true accuracy a on a 2-class problem appears to have accuracy a(1-\eta)+(1-a)\eta. With a=0.95 and η=0.10 you will measure only 0.86. So a noisy test set puts a ceiling on what you can see. Defences: measure inter-annotator agreement, relabel the examples where the model confidently disagrees with the label, and use label smoothing or robust losses.

Common traps
  • Leakage. Augmenting before splitting, or splitting frames of one video into train and test, makes test accuracy fiction. Split first, augment only the training set.
  • Augmenting the test set. Evaluate on clean images (or a fixed set of test-time transforms), never on random ones.
  • Label-breaking transforms. Flipping digits, cropping away the only defect, or colour-jitter on a task where colour is the label.
  • Fixing imbalance by oversampling exact duplicates invites memorisation; oversample with augmentation.
Where it shows up in AI
  • RandAugment, AutoAugment, MixUp, CutMix, Mosaic are learned or randomised policies; Mosaic (YOLO) pastes four images into one to expose small objects at many scales.
  • RetinaNet's focal loss (γ=2, α=0.25) is what made one-stage detectors accurate: a detector sees ~100,000 background boxes for a handful of objects.
  • Self-supervised vision (SimCLR, DINO) treats augmentation as the definition of similarity: two crops of one image must map to similar embeddings.

Check yourself

1. Compute the class weights for 9,000 normal and 1,000 defective images (K=2).
N=10,000. w_normal = 10000/(2·9000)=0.556; w_defect = 10000/(2·1000)=5. A defect is worth 9× a normal image.
2. For p_t=0.95 and γ=2, by what factor does focal loss shrink the cross-entropy?
(1−0.95)² = 0.0025, i.e. 400× smaller. CE = 0.0513, FL = 0.000128.
3. Your annotators flip 5% of labels at random and your model is truly 90% accurate (2 classes). What accuracy will you measure?
0.90·0.95 + 0.10·0.05 = 0.855 + 0.005 = 0.86.

Takeaways

  • Design the dataset and the split before the model; leakage ruins evaluation.
  • Augmentation applies label-preserving random transforms; choose ones that match the task's invariances.
  • Imbalance: resample, weight the loss (w = N/(K·nc)), or use focal loss to silence easy examples.
  • Label noise caps measurable accuracy; audit labels.
9.2

Transfer learning & fine-tuning

The questionYou have 1,500 labelled photos of 10 kinds of leaf disease. Training ResNet-50 (25.6 million parameters) from random weights would memorise them. How can a network learn from so few images — and which parts of it should you change?

Intuition

A network trained on ImageNet's 1.28 million images has already learned a hierarchy: early layers detect edges and colours, middle layers textures and parts, late layers object-specific shapes. Edges and textures are useful for leaves too. Transfer learning keeps that knowledge: start from the pretrained weights and adapt them. Imagine hiring an experienced photographer for a new job: you do not teach them what a lens is; you show them the new subject.

Two regimes. Feature extraction: freeze the whole backbone, replace the final classifier with a new one and train only that. Fine-tuning: also unfreeze the last blocks (or everything) with a small learning rate, so the useful features bend toward the new task without being destroyed. The less data and the more similar the domain, the more you should freeze.

Build it up

Look at what you would be training. The ResNet-50 blueprint (computed with the architecture engine, so exactly the published 25,557,032 parameters):

The parameters sit mostly in the last stages (wide channels), while early stages are cheap in parameters but expensive in compute (large feature maps).

Worked example — trainable parameters for 10 leaf diseases

Throw away the 1000-class FC and add a new one: 2048·10 + 10 = 20,490 parameters. The backbone has 25,557,032 − 2,049,000 = 23,508,032, so the new network has 23,528,522 parameters.

Head only: 20,490 trainable = 0.087% of the network. Head + layer4: 14,964,736 + 20,490 = 14,985,226 = 63.7%. Everything: 100%. Adam keeps 8 bytes of state per trainable parameter, so head-only needs 164 KB of optimizer state while full fine-tuning needs 188 MB.

Layer-wise (discriminative) learning rates. Early layers hold general features, so change them gently; later layers and the new head can move faster. With head rate \eta and decay \gamma per block group, group at distance j from the head uses \eta\gamma^{j}. Example: η=0.01, γ=0.5: layer4 0.005, layer3 0.0025, layer2 0.00125, layer1 0.000625, stem 0.0003125.

LoRA and adapters. For huge models even storing a full fine-tuned copy is wasteful. LoRA freezes a weight matrix W\in\mathbb R^{d\times d} and learns a low-rank update \Delta W=BA, with B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}, A\in\mathbb R^{r\times d}, r\ll d. Parameters: 2dr instead of d^2. For d=2048, r=8: 32,768 vs 4,194,304 = 0.78% (the ratio is 2r/d). For a non-square matrix, r(din+dout): a 512→2048 layer at r=8 needs 8·2560 = 20,480 instead of 1,048,576. B is initialised to zero, so training starts exactly at the pretrained model. Adapters are a related idea: tiny bottleneck layers inserted between frozen blocks. Both are standard for Vision Transformers.

Common traps
  • Learning rate too high on pretrained layers wipes out what they know ("catastrophic forgetting"). Fine-tune with 10–100× smaller rates than from-scratch training.
  • Different preprocessing. Pretrained models expect their own input size and mean/std normalisation; changing it silently hurts.
  • BatchNorm with tiny batches. Statistics from a batch of 2 are noisy; freeze BN layers (use running statistics) when fine-tuning with small batches.
  • The chart of loss above is an illustration of typical behaviour; always validate on your own data.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Almost every detector and segmenter (Module 6–7) starts from an ImageNet or self-supervised backbone.
  • CLIP / DINOv2 / SAM are used as frozen feature extractors with a small head ("linear probe").
  • LoRA adapts large ViTs and diffusion models with a few megabytes per task.

Check yourself

1. How many parameters does a new 5-class head on ResNet-50 have?
2048·5 + 5 = 10,245.
2. LoRA with r=16 on a 1024×1024 matrix: parameters and fraction?
2·1024·16 = 32,768 vs 1,048,576 = 3.125% (2r/d = 32/1024).
3. You have 200 images of a domain very different from ImageNet (satellite). Freeze everything, or fine-tune? Why?
Neither extreme: a frozen backbone cannot adapt to the shift, but full fine-tuning on 200 images overfits. Unfreeze the last stage(s) with small, layer-wise learning rates, or use LoRA, and watch validation loss.

Takeaways

  • Reuse a pretrained backbone; replace the head; unfreeze progressively.
  • ResNet-50 holds 58.6% of its weights in layer4 and 8% in the FC head.
  • Use smaller learning rates for earlier layers; LoRA trains 2dr instead of d² parameters.
9.3

Losses & training recipes

The questionYour training run needs a 24 GB GPU you do not have. Halving the batch size fixes memory — but does it change the right learning rate? And how much does mixed precision really save?

Intuition

A loss turns "how wrong is this prediction" into one number that gradient descent can reduce. Every vision task has its natural loss: classification — cross-entropy -\ln p_{\text{true}}; box regression — smooth-L1 or IoU-based losses (1-\text{IoU}, and GIoU/CIoU which still give a gradient when boxes do not overlap); segmentation — per-pixel cross-entropy plus Dice loss 1-\frac{2|P\cap G|}{|P|+|G|} which copes with tiny foreground regions; detection — a sum of classification, box and "objectness" terms; generation — adversarial or noise-prediction losses (Module 8).

A training recipe is everything around the loss that decides whether training works: the optimiser (SGD with momentum 0.9 for CNNs, AdamW for transformers), the learning-rate schedule (warm-up then cosine decay), weight decay, batch size, augmentation, number of epochs, and numerical precision.

Build it up

Batch size and learning rate. A bigger batch averages more examples, so the gradient is less noisy and you can take a bigger step. The linear scaling rule (Goyal et al., ResNet-50 on ImageNet): \eta=0.1\cdot B/256. B=32 → 0.0125, B=256 → 0.1, B=1024 → 0.4. Large rates are unstable at the start, so ramp up over the first ~5 epochs (warm-up) and then decay (cosine or step). One epoch of ImageNet (1,281,167 images) is ⌈1,281,167/B⌉ steps: 40,037 at B=32, 5,005 at B=256, 1,252 at B=1024.

Mixed precision. Store and compute activations in 16-bit floats (half the memory, and tensor cores run 8–16× faster than FP32 units on modern GPUs) while keeping a 32-bit "master" copy of the weights and scaling the loss so tiny gradients do not underflow to zero.

Worked example — memory of training ResNet-50 (P = 25,557,032)

Plain fp32 + Adam: weights 4 B + gradients 4 B + Adam moments 8 B = 16 B per parameter → 409 MB.

Classic mixed precision + Adam: fp32 master 4 B + fp16 weights 2 B + fp16 gradients 2 B + Adam 8 B = 16 B → also 409 MB. So mixed precision does not shrink the model state; it shrinks the activations.

Activations: the sum of block outputs in ResNet-50 is 6,676,456 values per image (the engine's figure). Backprop must keep several tensors per block (conv, norm and ReLU outputs); assume k = 4 such tensors per block output → 26.7 M values ≈ 107 MB per image in fp32, 53 MB in fp16. At batch 32: 3.4 GB fp32 → 1.7 GB fp16. (k is an approximation; real frameworks vary, so measure.)

Compute: forward = 4.09 GMACs = 8.18 GFLOPs per image; training ≈ 3× (forward + two backward products) = 24.5 GFLOPs. An A100 peaks at 312 TFLOPs fp16 and 19.5 TFLOPs fp32; a reported ≈2,600 img/s with mixed precision = 64 TFLOPs, about 20% of peak — memory traffic and small layers keep real utilisation far below the datasheet.

The formal definition

\mathcal L=\lambda_{\text{cls}}\mathcal L_{\text{cls}}+\lambda_{\text{box}}\mathcal L_{\text{box}}+\lambda_{\text{obj}}\mathcal L_{\text{obj}},\qquad \eta(t)=\eta_0\,\min\!\Big(\tfrac{t}{t_w},1\Big)\,\tfrac12\Big(1+\cos\tfrac{\pi t}{T}\Big)
λ·weights balancing the loss terms
η0, tw, Tpeak learning rate, warm-up length, total training length (epochs or steps)
IoUintersection area / union area of predicted and true box

Common traps
  • Changing the batch size without touching the learning rate (or the reverse).
  • Weight decay applied to BatchNorm and bias parameters; usually exclude them.
  • Comparing training loss across different loss weightings; compare validation metrics instead.
  • fp16 without loss scaling: gradients underflow to 0 and the model silently stops learning.
Where it shows up in AI
  • YOLO training mixes box (CIoU), objectness and class losses with fixed λ's.
  • U-Net segmentation of medical scans uses cross-entropy + Dice because lesions are a few pixels.
  • Foundation-model vision training uses AdamW, warm-up + cosine, bf16, and batch sizes in the thousands.

Check yourself

1. The linear scaling rule gives what learning rate for batch 512, starting from 0.1 at 256?
0.1·512/256 = 0.2.
2. Memory for weights + gradients + Adam moments (fp32) of a 10 M-parameter model?
10 M · (4+4+8) B = 160 MB.
3. Why is Dice loss better than per-pixel accuracy for a tumour covering 0.5% of the pixels?
Predicting "no tumour" everywhere gives 99.5% pixel accuracy but Dice = 0; Dice measures overlap of the small region itself.

Takeaways

  • Choose the loss that matches the task; combine terms with weights.
  • Scale learning rate with batch size (η=0.1·B/256), warm up, then decay.
  • Mixed precision saves activation memory and time, not model-state memory.
9.4

The end-to-end vision pipeline

The questionA camera sends 100 frames per second. Your detector takes 8 ms per frame. Is that fast enough — and what actually limits the system: the model, or everything around it?

Intuition

A deployed vision system is a factory line. A frame enters, passes through stations, and leaves as a result. Each station takes time. Two numbers matter and they are different: latency (how long one frame takes from camera to answer) and throughput (how many frames per second the line finishes). A line of six 5-minute stations has a 30-minute latency but, if every station works at once on different items, outputs one every 5 minutes. The slowest station — the bottleneck — sets the throughput.

Worked example — the latency budget

Per frame, one at a time: decode 4 ms + resize 1 + normalise 0.5 + inference 8 + NMS 1 + store 1 = 15.5 ms. If a single thread does all stations in turn: 1000/15.5 = 64.5 frames/s. The camera sends 100/s, so this design drops frames.

Pipeline it: each station is its own worker, so a new frame can be decoded while the previous one is in inference. Now the throughput is limited by the slowest stage only: 1000/8 = 125 frames/s ≥ 100. The latency of each frame is still ≈ 15.5 ms (plus queueing) — parallelism raised throughput, not speed.

Batching. A GPU is inefficient on one small image; running B images together amortises fixed costs. Model: inference time t(B)=t_0+t_1B with t₀=6 ms fixed, t₁=0.5 ms per image.

Throughput climbs, but a frame at the head of the batch waits (B-1)/\lambda for the batch to fill. Real servers cap this with a maximum wait (e.g. 10 ms): "batch up to 8 but never hold a frame longer than 10 ms".

Little's law. In any stable system, the average number of items inside equals the arrival rate times the average time each spends there: L=\lambda W. At 100 frames/s and W = 30 ms: L = 100·0.030 = 3 frames in flight. It sizes your buffers (and tells you a queue that keeps growing means λ exceeds capacity).

Common traps
  • Benchmarking only the model: decode and resize on the CPU often take as long as the network.
  • Quoting throughput as if it were latency (and vice versa). A video-analytics product needs throughput; a robot needs latency.
  • Mismatched preprocessing between training and serving (BGR vs RGB, different resize interpolation) — a classic silent accuracy loss.
  • Unbounded queues: they hide overload until memory runs out. Bound them and drop or degrade gracefully.
Where it shows up in AI
  • NVIDIA DeepStream / Triton implement exactly this: GPU decode, dynamic batching with a max-wait, TensorRT inference, GPU NMS.
  • Autonomous driving runs several camera pipelines and fuses results under a hard end-to-end deadline (tens of milliseconds).
  • Mobile apps run the same stages on one chip, so decode, resize and NPU inference compete for memory bandwidth.

Check yourself

1. Stages take 3, 7, 2 ms and run as a parallel pipeline. Throughput and latency?
Throughput = 1000/7 = 143 frames/s (slowest stage). Latency ≈ 3+7+2 = 12 ms when nothing waits.
2. A service handles 250 requests/s and each stays 40 ms in the system on average. How many are in flight?

3. With t(B)=6+0.5B ms, what is the throughput at B=20?

Takeaways

  • Latency is the sum along the line; throughput is set by the slowest stage.
  • Batching trades latency for throughput; cap the wait.
  • L = λW sizes queues; a growing queue means overload.
  • Profile the whole pipeline, not only the network.
9.5

Making models small & fast

The questionYour ResNet-50 is 102 MB and needs 8 GFLOPs per image. The target phone has little memory and no big GPU. Can you shrink it 4× or more and lose almost no accuracy?

Intuition

Trained networks are over-built: many weights are tiny, many are stored with far more precision than needed, and a big model's knowledge can be copied into a small one. Three standard tools: quantization (store numbers with fewer bits), pruning (delete weights or neurons that matter little), distillation (train a small student to imitate a big teacher). Architecture choice (MobileNet, EfficientNet) is a fourth, covered in Module 4.

Build it up: quantization

Float32 uses 32 bits per weight. Int8 uses 8: only 256 possible values, so we choose a linear grid over the weights' range. For weights between w_{\min} and w_{\max}, the scale is the grid spacing s=\frac{w_{\max}-w_{\min}}{255} and the zero point z=\operatorname{round}(-w_{\min}/s) is the integer that represents the real number 0 (so zero is exact). Quantize: q=\operatorname{clip}(\operatorname{round}(w/s)+z,\,0,\,255). Dequantize: \hat w=(q-z)\,s.

Worked example — int8 on a few weights

Range [−1.0, 1.5]: s = 2.5/255 = 0.009804, z = round(1.0/0.009804) = 102.

Rounding never moves a weight by more than half a grid step: |error| ≤ s/2 = 0.0049. Size: ResNet-50 25,557,032 weights × 4 B = 102.2 MB → × 1 B = 25.6 MB, exactly 4× smaller (plus a few scales). Int8 arithmetic is also faster and uses less energy.

Two ways to get there: post-training quantization (calibrate ranges on a few hundred images, no retraining; usually loses 0.1–1% on ResNet-50) and quantization-aware training (simulate rounding during fine-tuning, recovers most loss, needed for 4-bit). Important detail: the range is set by the largest weight, so one outlier wastes the whole grid; use per-channel scales and clipping.

Build it up: pruning

Magnitude pruning: set the smallest-|w| weights to zero, then fine-tune to recover. Unstructured pruning removes individual weights (high sparsity possible, but the matrix keeps its shape, so speed-ups need sparse hardware: Ampere GPUs support 2:4 — two zeros in every four weights — for 2×). Structured pruning removes whole filters/channels, giving a genuinely smaller dense network that is faster everywhere but costs more accuracy per removed parameter.

Worked example

Weights w = (0.8, −0.05, 0.3, −0.6, 0.02, 0.45, −0.1, 0.9), threshold 0.25. Kept: 0.8, 0.3, −0.6, 0.45, 0.9 (5 of 8); pruned 3 → sparsity 37.5%. For input x = all ones: dense output 1.72; pruned output 0.8+0.3−0.6+0.45+0.9 = 1.85. The error 0.13 is exactly the sum of the removed weights (−0.05+0.02−0.10 = −0.13) — which is why removing many tiny weights of both signs hurts so little.

Build it up: distillation

A trained teacher's probabilities contain more than the label: for a picture of a cat it may say cat 0.95, dog 0.047, fox 0.002 — a dog is more cat-like than a fox. The student is trained on these soft targets as well as the hard label. A temperature T>1 softens the distribution so the small probabilities become visible: p_i^{(T)}=\frac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}}.

Worked example — 3 classes (cat, dog, fox)

Teacher logits (5, 2, −1); student logits (3, 2.5, −0.5).

T=1: teacher (0.950, 0.047, 0.002), student (0.611, 0.371, 0.018). KL(teacher‖student) = 0.3176.

T=4: teacher (0.590, 0.279, 0.132), student (0.435, 0.384, 0.181). KL = 0.0483, and the factor T² = 16 restores its size: 0.7725. (Gradients of soft terms shrink like 1/T², hence the T².)

Hard cross-entropy of the student on the label "cat" (T=1): −ln 0.611 = 0.4927. With α = 0.5: L=0.5\cdot0.7725+0.5\cdot0.4927=0.6326.

The formal definition

s=\frac{w_{\max}-w_{\min}}{2^b-1},\quad q=\operatorname{clip}\!\big(\operatorname{round}(w/s)+z\big),\quad \hat w=(q-z)s\qquad \mathcal L_{KD}=\alpha\,T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p^{(T)}_{\text{teacher}}\,\|\,p^{(T)}_{\text{student}}\big)+(1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{CE}(y,p_{\text{student}})
bbit width (8 → 256 levels)
s, zscale (grid spacing) and zero point (integer for real 0)
Ttemperature; T=1 is the ordinary softmax
αmix between imitating the teacher and fitting the hard label
KL\sum_i p_i\ln(p_i/q_i), zero only when the two distributions match

Common traps
  • Judging compression by parameter count alone: unstructured sparsity may not speed anything up; measure latency on the target device.
  • Calibrating quantization on unrepresentative images; ranges then clip real data.
  • Pruning without fine-tuning; accuracy drops far more than needed.
  • Forgetting that the speed/accuracy trade-off is a curve: pick the point your product needs, not the smallest model.
Where it shows up in AI
  • TensorFlow Lite / Core ML / TensorRT int8 run quantized MobileNets and detectors on phones and cameras.
  • DistilBERT-style distillation also applies to vision: DeiT distils a CNN teacher into a ViT with a "distillation token".
  • NVIDIA 2:4 sparsity and channel pruning (network slimming) are used in production edge models.

Check yourself

1. Range [−2, 2], int8 asymmetric: scale, zero point, and the largest rounding error?
s = 4/255 = 0.01569; z = round(2/0.01569) = 127.5 → 128 (round half up; either 127 or 128 is a valid convention); max error s/2 = 0.0078.
2. MobileNet-v2 has 3.50 M parameters. Its int8 weight size?
3.50 MB (1 byte each), versus 14.0 MB in float32.
3. Why multiply the soft loss by T²?
Soft-target gradients scale as 1/T²; multiplying by T² keeps the soft and hard terms comparable when T changes.

Takeaways

  • Int8 quantization: s=(max−min)/255, zero point z, error ≤ s/2, 4× smaller.
  • Pruning removes small weights; only structured pruning (or 2:4 sparsity) gives real speed-ups.
  • Distillation transfers a teacher's soft probabilities; temperature exposes them.
9.6

Deployment & hardware

The questionThe model works in PyTorch on your workstation. How do you get it onto a server GPU, a phone, and a 5 W camera, and how do you know it will fit and run fast enough?

Intuition

Training frameworks are flexible but heavy. Deployment wants the opposite: a fixed, optimised program for specific hardware. The workflow: export the trained network to a neutral graph format (ONNX — a list of operators and tensors, readable by many runtimes), then let a hardware-specific compiler optimise it (TensorRT for NVIDIA GPUs, OpenVINO for Intel CPUs, Core ML for Apple, TFLite for Android/NPUs).

What compilers do: fuse layers (a conv, BatchNorm and ReLU become one kernel, saving memory trips), choose the fastest kernel per layer, use lower precision, and plan memory so tensors reuse buffers.

Build it up

Worked example — folding BatchNorm into a convolution

At inference BN is a fixed affine map y=\gamma\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}+\beta, so it can be merged into the previous conv: w'=w\,\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}, b'=\beta+(b-\mu)\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}. Take w=0.5, b=0, γ=2, β=0.1, μ=0.2, σ²+ε=0.25. Factor = 2/0.5 = 4, so w' = 2.0 and b' = 0.1 + (0−0.2)·4 = −0.7. Check with input 1: conv → 0.5, BN → 4(0.5−0.2)+0.1 = 1.3; fused: 2·1 − 0.7 = 1.3. Identical, one operation instead of two.

Hardware. A CPU has few powerful cores, flexible, good for small models and pre/post-processing, slow for big convs. A GPU has thousands of simple cores and very high memory bandwidth: ideal for big batched convs and transformers. An NPU (neural processing unit, in phones, cameras, Edge-TPUs) is fixed-function silicon for int8 matrix/conv work: far better performance per watt, but only supports certain operators and precisions. Example peaks (datasheet-style, assumed for this arithmetic): CPU 200 GFLOPs, a 19.5 TFLOP fp32 GPU, an NPU 4 TOPs int8 at 2 W. ResNet-50 needs 8.18 GFLOPs/image: ideal time CPU 41 ms, GPU 0.42 ms, NPU 2 ms. Real numbers are 2–5× worse (memory bound, partial utilisation).

Throughput versus latency (again). The GPU reaches full speed only at larger batches; at batch 1 it is under-used. For a robot choose batch 1 and latency; for a server farm choose big batches and images/second/dollar.

Worked example — memory for ResNet-50, batch 32, inference

Weights: 25,557,032 × 4 B = 102.2 MB (fp32), 51.1 MB (fp16), 25.6 MB (int8).

Activations: at inference a tensor can be freed once its consumer has run, so you keep only the live ones. The largest tensor in ResNet-50 is 802,816 values (64×112×112 after the stem, also 256×56×56). Two live buffers (input and output of a layer): 2 × 802,816 × 4 B = 6.4 MB per image; batch 32 → 205.5 MB. (The input batch itself: 32·3·224·224·4 = 19.3 MB.)

Workspace: scratch for algorithms such as FFT/Winograd convolutions, typically a few hundred MB (assume 256 MB; the runtime lets you cap it).

Total ≈ 102 + 206 + 256 = 564 MB in fp32; in int8 ≈ 26 + 51 + 256 ≈ 333 MB. It fits an 8 GB edge GPU with room for several models. A training run of the same batch would need gigabytes (Lesson 9.3): inference is cheap because nothing is saved for backprop.

Serving. A service wraps the model with an HTTP/gRPC endpoint, a request queue, dynamic batching and autoscaling; the edge version is a loop reading a camera. Always measure on the target: end-to-end p50/p95 latency, throughput and power.

Common traps
  • Unsupported operators: the NPU compiler silently falls back to the CPU for one layer, destroying speed. Check the op list.
  • Comparing numbers from different batch sizes or precisions.
  • Ignoring thermal throttling: a phone sustains a fraction of its peak after a minute.
  • Exporting with dynamic shapes you never tested.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Phone cameras run segmentation and face detection on the NPU every frame.
  • Cloud vision APIs run TensorRT-optimised models with dynamic batching on GPU fleets.
  • Drones and smart cameras run int8 MobileNet/YOLO variants within a few watts.

138.36 M × 2 B = 276.7 MB.
2. Fold BN with γ=1, β=0, μ=0.5, σ²+ε=1 into w=2, b=1. New w' and b'?

3. A GPU is idle 70% of the time at batch 1. Two ways to improve utilisation?

Takeaways

  • Export to ONNX, then compile for the target (TensorRT, OpenVINO, Core ML, TFLite).
  • Memory = weights + live activations + workspace; inference is far cheaper than training.
  • CPU = flexible, GPU = throughput, NPU = efficiency; always benchmark on the real device.
9.7

Monitoring, bias & failure modes

The questionYour model scored 94% in the lab and has been live for three months. How would you know if it silently got worse, whether it works equally well for everyone, and whether someone could fool it on purpose?

Intuition

A model is a snapshot of the world at training time. The world moves: seasons, new camera firmware, a new product design. That is drift. Ground-truth labels in production arrive late or never, so you watch the inputs and the outputs for change instead. Separately, a model can be accurate on average and unfair to a subgroup, can leak private data, and can be fooled by inputs crafted to exploit it. Responsible deployment means measuring all of these.

Build it up: drift

Covariate drift: the inputs change (images get darker) though the right answers for a given image do not. Concept drift: the meaning changes (a new defect type). Detect drift by comparing a feature's live distribution with its training distribution. The Population Stability Index splits the feature into bins (usually training deciles, so each holds 10% in training) and sums \sum_i(a_i-e_i)\ln(a_i/e_i), where e_i is the expected (training) share and a_i the live share. Rule of thumb: <0.1 stable, 0.1–0.25 investigate, >0.25 act. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic is the largest gap between the two cumulative distributions.

Worked example — PSI with 3 bins

Training shares (0.25, 0.50, 0.25); live shares (0.10, 0.45, 0.45).

(0.10−0.25)·ln(0.10/0.25) = (−0.15)(−0.9163) = 0.1374; (0.45−0.50)·ln(0.9) = (−0.05)(−0.1054) = 0.0053; (0.45−0.25)·ln(1.8) = 0.2·0.5878 = 0.1176. PSI = 0.260 > 0.25: alert.

Build it up: bias and fairness

Overall accuracy hides group differences. Compute the confusion matrix per group and compare: demographic parity (equal rate of positive predictions), equal opportunity (equal true-positive rate), equalized odds (equal TPR and FPR). Bias usually comes from the data: under-represented groups, labels reflecting human prejudice, or a proxy (background, camera, lighting) that correlates with the group.

Worked example

Group A (200 people): TP 80, FN 20, FP 10, TN 90. Group B (200): TP 45, FN 30, FP 5, TN 120.

Accuracy A = 170/200 = 0.85; B = 165/200 = 0.825 — nearly equal. But TPR A = 80/100 = 0.80, TPR B = 45/75 = 0.60: equal-opportunity gap 0.20. Positive rate A = 90/200 = 0.45, B = 50/200 = 0.25: demographic-parity gap 0.20. A single accuracy number hid that group B's true cases are missed twice as often (FN rate 40% vs 20%).

Privacy. Images contain faces, plates, documents. Good practice: collect only what you need, blur or discard faces on the device, store features not frames, set retention limits, and get consent. Models can memorise: membership-inference attacks guess whether a given photo was in the training set, and generative models can regurgitate training images. Differential privacy (clipping and noising gradients) bounds this, at some accuracy cost; federated learning trains without centralising images.

Build it up: adversarial examples

A tiny, deliberately chosen perturbation can flip a prediction. The Fast Gradient Sign Method moves every input value by ε in the direction that increases the loss: x'=x+\varepsilon\,\operatorname{sign}(\nabla_x\mathcal L). For a linear classifier with score s=w\cdot x+b, the gradient of the score is just w, so the attack on a positive example is x'=x-\varepsilon\,\operatorname{sign}(w) and the score changes by exactly -\varepsilon\lVert w\rVert_1.

Worked example — FGSM by hand

w = (2, −1, 3), b = −1, x = (0.5, 0.2, 0.4). Score s = 1.0 − 0.2 + 1.2 − 1 = 1.0, probability σ(1) = 0.731: confidently positive.

‖w‖₁ = 2+1+3 = 6. Attack direction: −sign(w) = (−1, +1, −1). With ε = 0.1: x' = (0.4, 0.3, 0.3), s' = 0.8 − 0.3 + 0.9 − 1 = 0.4 (= 1 − 0.1·6), probability 0.599. With ε = 0.2: x' = (0.3, 0.4, 0.2), s' = 0.6 − 0.4 + 0.6 − 1 = −0.2: decision flipped, probability of the true class 0.450. The smallest flipping budget is ε* = |s|/‖w‖₁ = 1/6 = 0.167.

Why images are fragile: the shift is ε‖w‖₁ — it grows with the number of inputs. With d = 150,528 pixels and typical |wᵢ| = 0.01, even ε = 1/255 (one grey level, invisible) shifts the score by (1/255)·150,528·0.01 = 5.9. Many tiny nudges add up.

Defences: adversarial training (include attacked examples in training), input preprocessing, ensembles, certified bounds — none is complete. Physical attacks (stickers on stop signs) and out-of-distribution inputs also cause failures. Safe deployment: a human fallback for low-confidence outputs, uncertainty estimates, monitoring dashboards, staged roll-out (shadow mode, then a small share of traffic), a rollback plan, and documentation (model cards, datasheets) stating intended use and known failure modes.

The formal definition

\mathrm{PSI}=\sum_{i}(a_i-e_i)\ln\frac{a_i}{e_i}\qquad \Delta_{\mathrm{EO}}=\big|\mathrm{TPR}_A-\mathrm{TPR}_B\big|\qquad x'=x+\varepsilon\,\operatorname{sign}\big(\nabla_x\mathcal L(x,y)\big)

TPRgtrue-positive rate of group g: TP/(TP+FN)

∇xℒgradient of the loss with respect to the input pixels

Common traps
  • Alerting on every statistically significant shift: with large windows tiny, harmless drifts are "significant". Use effect-size thresholds like PSI.
  • Reporting only overall accuracy; always slice by group, lighting, camera and region.
  • "Fairness through unawareness": dropping the sensitive attribute does not remove proxies.
  • Believing adversarial robustness is solved by obscurity; assume the attacker may know the model.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Gender Shades (2018) found commercial face analysis error rates of up to ~35% for darker-skinned women versus under 1% for lighter-skinned men: the canonical bias audit.
  • Evidently, WhyLabs, Arize and similar tools compute PSI/KS dashboards for production ML.
  • Adversarial patches and stop-sign stickers drove research on robust perception for autonomous driving.

Check yourself

1. FGSM on w=(1,−2), b=0, x=(1,0), positive class. Score, ε*, and x' at ε=0.4?
s = 1. ‖w‖₁ = 3, ε* = 1/3. x' = x − ε·sign(w) = (1−0.4, 0+0.4) = (0.6, 0.4); s' = 0.6 − 0.8 = −0.2: flipped.
2. Group A has TPR 0.9, group B 0.7, equal overall accuracy. Which fairness criterion fails?
Equal opportunity (TPR gap 0.20); equalized odds also fails unless FPRs compensate.
3. Why watch input drift rather than accuracy in production?
Labels arrive late or never; input and prediction statistics are available immediately and warn before the damage shows up in metrics.

Takeaways

  • Monitor input distributions (PSI/KS) and outputs; alert and retrain before accuracy collapses.
  • Evaluate per group; equal accuracy can hide unequal error types.
  • FGSM shifts the score by ε‖w‖₁, which grows with dimension: images are fragile.
  • Deploy with fallbacks, staged roll-out and documentation.
