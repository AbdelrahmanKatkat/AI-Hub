Module 4 · Perception for robots

A robot does not see pixels; it needs geometry: where an object is, how it is turned, which surfaces are flat and where to put the fingers. This module turns raw 3-D points and camera images into poses and grasps. By the end you will align two scans by hand and with ICP, budget the latency of a detector, solve a six-number pose from keypoints, check a grasp against friction, and close a camera loop on image error.

6 lessons≈ 2.5 hoursNeeds Module 1 (cameras, lidar) and Module 3 (estimation)

Where this sits. Perception is mostly stage 2 of the robot pipeline (estimate): it turns sensor data into a model of the scene. Visual servoing (Lesson 4.6) is the exception: it closes the control loop directly on image features, so it lives in stage 4 (control). Each lesson says which stage it is in and how fast its loop must run.

Notation. Poses are written as a rotation R and a translation t: a point p in one frame becomes Rp + t in another. Angles are in degrees unless a formula uses radians; lengths are in metres unless a pixel is stated; frequencies are in hertz (Hz).

4.1

Point clouds & 3-D geometry

The questionA depth camera returns about 300 000 points for every frame. Your planner needs to know where the box is, which of its surfaces are flat and whether the gripper can reach it, all within about 50 ms. How do you store the scene so that it is small enough to process and still keeps the geometry?

Intuition

Imagine a scanner that sprays a fine cloud of paint dots onto everything in front of it. Each dot records one position (x, y, z) in the scanner's frame. The result is a point cloud: a list of 3-D points with no connections, no surfaces and no colour required. It is the most direct picture of geometry a sensor can give, because it is what the sensor measured.

Two questions follow at once. First, a cloud is heavy, so how do we make it lighter without losing the shape? Second, a flat table and a curved bowl look the same as points, so how do we recover which way a surface faces? The answers are voxel downsampling and surface normals. Both use only the points near each other.

Build it up

Step 1: moving a cloud. A cloud is an array of N rows of (x, y, z). When the robot moves, every point moves with the same rigid motion, p' = Rp + t. The order matters: rotate first, then translate.

Worked example: one point, one pose

Take R as a 90° turn about the z axis, and t = (1, 0, 0) m. Then R = \begin{bmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}. For p = (0.3, 0.2, 0.4) m:

Rp = (0\cdot0.3 - 1\cdot0.2,\; 1\cdot0.3 + 0\cdot0.2,\; 0.4) = (-0.2,\; 0.3,\; 0.4)
p' = Rp + t = (0.8,\; 0.3,\; 0.4)\text{ m}

Step 2: voxel downsampling. Cut space into cubes of side v (a voxel, like a 3-D pixel). Give every point the index of the cube it falls in, \lfloor x/v\rfloor, \lfloor y/v\rfloor, \lfloor z/v\rfloor, and replace all points that share an index by their mean. A cloud of a million points might become a few thousand, and the shape survives because each cube keeps its centre of mass.

Worked example: six points, voxel size 0.5 m

Points (in metres): A (0.10, 0.10, 0.10), B (0.30, 0.20, 0.40), C (0.45, 0.35, 0.20), D (0.70, 0.10, 0.10), E (0.80, 0.40, 0.30), F (1.20, 1.10, 0.90). Their voxel indices are:

  • A, B, C all give (0,0,0) (for example \lfloor 0.45/0.5\rfloor = 0).
  • D and E give (1,0,0), because \lfloor 0.7/0.5\rfloor = \lfloor 0.8/0.5\rfloor = 1.
  • F gives (2,2,1) alone.

The centroids are: voxel (0,0,0) gives \tfrac{1}{3}(0.85, 0.65, 0.70) = (0.283, 0.217, 0.233); voxel (1,0,0) gives (0.750, 0.250, 0.200); F stays at (1.20, 1.10, 0.90). Six points became three.

Step 3: normals from neighbours. A flat patch is one where the neighbouring points barely spread in one direction. Take the k nearest points, compute their mean and their 3 × 3 covariance matrix, and find the eigenvector with the smallest eigenvalue. That direction is the normal: the neighbourhood has almost no extent along it.

Worked example: the normal of a line of points (the 2-D version)

Points (0,0), (2,1), (4,2), (6,3) lie on the line y = x/2. Their mean is (3, 1.5). The centred deviations are (-3,-1.5), (-1,-0.5), (1,0.5), (3,1.5), so the covariance sums are S_{xx}=20,\; S_{xy}=10,\; S_{yy}=5:

C = \begin{bmatrix}20 & 10\\ 10 & 5\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{eigenvalues } 25 \text{ and } 0

The eigenvector for 0 is (-1, 2)/\sqrt5 = (-0.447, 0.894). It is perpendicular to the line direction (2,1), so it is the normal. In 3-D the same recipe uses a 3 × 3 matrix; a flat patch gives one near-zero eigenvalue, and its eigenvector is the surface normal.

The formal definition

P = \{\mathbf p_i\}_{i=1}^{N},\qquad \mathbf p_i\in\mathbb R^3 \qquad\qquad \mathbf p' = R\,\mathbf p + \mathbf t
k_i=\left(\lfloor x_i/v\rfloor,\lfloor y_i/v\rfloor,\lfloor z_i/v\rfloor\right),\qquad \bar{\mathbf c}_k=\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i:\,k_i=k}\mathbf p_i
C=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)}(\mathbf p_j-\bar{\mathbf p})(\mathbf p_j-\bar{\mathbf p})^{\top},\qquad C\,\mathbf n=\lambda_{\min}\mathbf n

Lab

Try this
  1. Let the box turn once. Notice that the cloud is 900 points, all on the six faces, not filling the volume: a depth sensor sees surfaces, not interiors.
  2. Set the voxel size to 5 cm. Count the orange centroids. Compare with the readout: roughly how many raw points did each centroid replace?
  3. Raise the voxel size to 10 cm and watch the faces thin into a few dots. This is the trade-off: smaller clouds are faster, but a 10 cm voxel cannot represent a wire 1 cm thick.
  4. Set the voxel size back to off. Keep the cloud turning and think about what a normal computed from a 5-point neighbourhood would tell you on a face and on an edge.
Common traps
  • "More points is always better." Points beyond the sensor's resolution add cost and noise, not detail. Downsample to the resolution your task needs.
  • Voxels that are too large erase small objects. A grasp needs detail at the scale of the fingers, not of the room.
  • Normals have no inherent sign. n and -n are both valid. Libraries orient normals toward the sensor by convention; if you forget this, lighting and inside/outside tests flip.
  • Frames and units. A cloud is only meaningful with its frame (camera, lidar or robot base). Transforms are applied in the right order, and a scanner in millimetres fed to a planner in metres is off by a factor of 1000.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Grasp and scene-understanding pipelines take a cloud as input and downsample it first. Open3D and the Point Cloud Library (PCL) are the usual open-source tools for these steps.
  • The ROS 2 message sensor_msgs/PointCloud2 carries a cloud between sensor and perception nodes, so the voxel size you choose sets the bandwidth of the whole stack.
  • Learned point-cloud networks (PointNet-style models) consume the same list of points, which is why downsampling choices also affect what a learned model can learn.
Pipeline positionStage 1 (sense) produces the raw cloud; stage 2 (estimate) downsamples it and computes normals. Typical lidar and depth sensors deliver 10–30 frames per second, so the whole step must fit in tens of milliseconds. The stored product is a downsampled cloud in one named frame.

Check yourself

1. A point is p=(0,1,0). Apply R = 90° about z (as in the worked example) and t = (1, 0, 0). Where is it?
Rp = (−1, 0, 0) because R(0,1,0) = (−1, 0, 0). Adding t gives (0, 0, 0), the origin.
2. Voxel size 0.2 m. Points at x = 0.39 and x = 0.41 (same y and z). How many voxels do they fall in?
Two. \lfloor 0.39/0.2\rfloor = 1 and \lfloor 0.41/0.2\rfloor = 2. A voxel boundary lies between them, even though they are only 2 cm apart.
3. Why does the smallest eigenvector of the neighbourhood covariance give the normal?
Its eigenvalue is the variance of the neighbours along that direction. On a flat patch the variance across the surface is large and the variance perpendicular to it is near zero, so the smallest eigenvector points out of the surface.

Takeaways

  • A point cloud is a list of 3-D points in one frame; moving it means p' = Rp + t, rotation first.
  • Voxel downsampling replaces each occupied cube by its centroid: fewer points, same shape, a cost set by the voxel size.
  • Normals come from the eigenvector with the smallest eigenvalue of a local covariance matrix; their sign is a convention.
4.2

Registration & ICP

The questionThe robot holds a map scan of a shelf taken earlier, and a new scan taken now from an unknown position. Both scans show the same shelf, but in different frames. What rigid motion (rotation and translation) lines the new scan up with the map?

Intuition

Picture two transparencies, each with the same drawing, placed at different angles on a desk. You slide one until the lines overlay. If you could say which dot on one sheet matches which dot on the other, lining them up would be a single calculation. If you cannot, you guess the matches, line up, look again at which dots now sit closest, and repeat. That loop is the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm, and the calculation at its heart is registration: finding (R, t) that best aligns two clouds.

Build it up

Step 1: aligning when the matches are known. Suppose each point p_i of the scan has a known partner q_i in the map, with q_i \approx R p_i + t. First remove the translation by centring both sets on their means, \bar p and \bar q. The centred points a_i = p_i - \bar p and b_i = q_i - \bar q then only need a rotation. The best rotation maximises the agreement \sum_i (R a_i)\cdot b_i, which has a closed-form answer through the singular value decomposition (SVD) of H = \sum_i a_i b_i^{\top}. The translation then follows as t = \bar q - R\bar p. In two dimensions the rotation is even simpler: \theta = \operatorname{atan2}\left(\sum a\times b,\ \sum a\cdot b\right).

Worked example: recover a known motion exactly

Let the scan be p_1=(1,0), p_2=(0,1), p_3=(-1,-1), and let the map be the same points turned by 90° and shifted by (2,1): q_1=(2,2), q_2=(1,1), q_3=(3,0).

  • Means: \bar p = (0,0), \bar q = (2,1). The centred points are the same as the raw points for the scan, and b = (0,1), (-1,0), (1,-1) for the map.
  • \sum a\cdot b = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0 and \sum a\times b = 1 + 1 + 2 = 4.
  • \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(4, 0) = 90^\circ. Then t = \bar q - R\bar p = (2,1).

The motion is recovered exactly, which is what closed-form registration promises when the matches are right.

Step 2: guessing the matches. When the partners are unknown, a reasonable first guess is the nearest point in the other cloud. Match, fit, move, and repeat. Each round can only make the fit at least as good for the current matches, so the sum of distances falls until nothing changes.

Worked example: one matching round from the identity

Keep the same clouds, but start from no motion (identity). The nearest map point for each scan point is:

  • p_1=(1,0): distances to (2,2), (1,1), (3,0) are 2.236, 1.000, 2.000, so the match is (1,1).
  • p_2=(0,1): nearest is (1,1) at distance 1.000.
  • p_3=(-1,-1): nearest is (1,1) at distance 2.828.

All three scan points choose the same map point. The matches are many-to-one, the fit is therefore wrong, and the next round starts from a bad place. This is the typical failure of a poor first guess, and it is why the lab asks you to try different starting headings.

The formal definition

E(R,\mathbf t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert R\,\mathbf p_i+\mathbf t-\mathbf q_{\pi(i)}\right\rVert^{2},\qquad \pi(i)=\arg\min_j\lVert R\,\mathbf p_i+\mathbf t-\mathbf q_j\rVert
H=\sum_i a_i b_i^{\top}=U\Sigma V^{\top},\qquad R=V\,\mathrm{diag}\!\left(1,\,\det(VU^{\top})\right)U^{\top},\qquad \mathbf t=\bar{\mathbf q}-R\bar{\mathbf p}

Lab

Try this
  1. Leave the initial heading error at 20° and press play. Watch the blue scan move toward the orange map and find the grey matching lines of each step. Read the RMS readout and the plot.
  2. Reset and set the error to 10°. The scan now converges to RMS near zero. Explain to yourself why the same algorithm succeeded here and stalled before.
  3. Try 30° and 40°. Each stalls at a similar RMS, a different local minimum where the shapes look roughly aligned.
  4. Slow the speed to 0.25×. Notice that the matches (grey lines) are computed first, and the points only glide to their new positions after the fit.
Common traps
  • Trusting the first fit. ICP reaches a local minimum; it has no way to know that a better alignment exists elsewhere. A good initial guess (from odometry or a previous pose) is part of the algorithm.
  • Symmetric shapes. A cylinder can slide along its axis and a flat wall can slide along itself: the error barely changes, so the answer is not determined. This is a degeneracy, not a bug.
  • Outliers pull the fit. A person walking through the scan adds points that have no partner. Reject matches farther than a gate distance before fitting.
  • Forgetting the reflection check. The SVD can return a mirror image; the determinant correction in the formula removes it.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Lidar odometry and mapping methods (LOAM and later KISS-ICP-style systems) estimate the robot's motion by registering each new scan to recent ones.
  • Generalised ICP replaces point-to-point distances with distances to local surface planes, which makes flat walls and floors constrain the fit better.
  • Learned registration networks predict a good initial guess, so that classical refinement (ICP) only needs a few steps.
Pipeline positionStage 2 (estimate). Lidar scan matching typically runs at the sensor rate (about 10 Hz), so one ICP run has roughly 100 ms. It stores the robot pose in the map frame and a growing map cloud.

Check yourself

1. Why is the rotation in the SVD solution forced to have determinant +1, and what happens if you skip the correction?
A reflection (determinant −1) would match the points better in some cases but is not a physical motion. Without the correction the SVD can return a mirror image of the cloud. The factor \det(VU^{\top}) flips the sign of the last singular direction when needed, so the result stays a rotation.
2. A robot drives down a long, featureless corridor and scan-matches each frame. What can go wrong, and why?
The fit can slide along the corridor. Moving the scan forward or back leaves almost every point's distance unchanged, so the error E has nearly no gradient along that direction. The estimate drifts, which is a degeneracy in the geometry, not a numerical error. Fuse the scan match with wheel or inertial odometry.
3. In the worked example all three scan points matched (1,1). What would a one-to-one (mutual) rule do?
It would keep only the pair where both points are each other's nearest neighbour. Here (1,1) is the nearest map point for all three, but only one scan point can be its partner, so most matches are rejected. The fit is then based on fewer, more trustworthy pairs.

Takeaways

  • With known matches, rigid alignment has a closed form: centre both clouds, take an SVD of the cross-covariance, fix the determinant, and recover the translation from the means.
  • ICP alternates nearest-neighbour matching and that closed-form fit; each round reduces the error for its matches.
  • ICP needs a good starting pose. Symmetry and featureless scenes make the answer ambiguous; fuse with odometry.
4.3

2-D and 3-D object detection for robots

The questionIn a bin of mixed parts, a 2-D detector finds a box in the image and draws a rectangle around it. The robot needs something different: a box in space, with a centre, a size and a heading, and it needs it within the time the arm takes to move. What changes when the detector must output 3-D boxes, and how fast can it run?

Intuition

A 2-D box is a window: it says where the object is in the picture. A 3-D box is a real box in space: centre (x,y,z), size (\ell, w, h) and a heading angle \theta around the vertical axis. Robots have two ways to get it. A camera gives a 2-D box and a depth value; a lidar gives points that are already 3-D. Many 3-D detectors reuse the ideas of 2-D detectors (features, anchors, a score per box) and simply add a depth dimension.

Detection also has a clock. A sensor that delivers 10 frames per second gives a new picture every 100 ms. If the detector needs 130 ms for each picture, it cannot keep up, and how it handles the frames it cannot process decides whether the robot acts on fresh or stale information.

Build it up

Step 1: lifting a pixel to a point with depth. In a pinhole camera with focal length f (in pixels) and principal point (c_x, c_y), a pixel (u,v) with depth Z corresponds to the 3-D point X = (u-c_x)Z/f, Y = (v-c_y)Z/f. A stereo pair gets its depth from disparity, Z = fB/d (see Module 1, cameras and depth).

Worked example: lift one pixel

With f = 800 px, c_x = 320 px and depth Z = 1.5 m, a pixel at u = 360 px has

X = \frac{(360-320)\cdot 1.5}{800} = 0.075\ \text{m}

so the object sits 7.5 cm to the right of the optical axis. A detector that outputs a 2-D box plus a depth can be lifted this way to a 3-D centre.

Step 2: measuring overlap in 3-D. Detections are scored against ground truth with intersection over union (IoU): the overlap volume divided by the combined volume. In a bird's-eye view (BEV, looking down from above) the boxes are flat rectangles, which is much simpler to compute.

Worked example: two boxes, BEV and 3-D

Box A covers x\in[0,4], y\in[0,2], z\in[0,1.5] m. Box B covers x\in[2,6], y\in[1,3], z\in[0.5,2] m.

  • BEV overlap: (4-2)\times(2-1) = 2\ \text{m}^2. Areas: 8 and 8. Union 8+8-2 = 14\ \text{m}^2. BEV IoU = 2/14 = 0.143.
  • Height overlap: \min(1.5,2)-\max(0,0.5) = 1.0 m. Volumes: A = 12 m³, B = 4 × 2 × 1.5 = 12 m³. Overlap 2 \times 1 \times 1 = 2\ \text{m}^3.
  • 3-D IoU = 2 / (12 + 12 - 2) = 2/22 = 0.091.

The 3-D score is lower than the BEV score because the boxes also miss each other in height. Many benchmarks accept a match at IoU ≥ 0.5 or 0.7; the exact threshold is part of the evaluation rule, not a physical constant.

Step 3: the latency budget. Let the sensor deliver a frame every T seconds, and let the detector take L seconds per frame. If L \le T, every frame can be processed. If L > T and every frame is queued, the backlog grows without bound. If only the newest frame is processed each time the detector is free, the output rate becomes 1/L and the results are not growing older.

Worked example: 10 Hz sensor, 130 ms detector

The frame period is T = 100 ms and L = 130 ms. Queueing every frame, frame k starts when frame k−1 finishes, so its age at output grows by L - T = 30 ms per frame: 130, 160, 190, 220, 250 ms, and so on.

Processing only the newest frame, the detector is busy for 130 ms each time. The steady output is 1/0.130 = 7.7 results per second, with ages between about 140 and 220 ms (mean about 170 ms). The rate is lower than the sensor's 10 Hz, but the answer never gets older than roughly one detector period plus one frame.

The formal definition

\mathrm{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|},\qquad \mathrm{IoU}_{3D}=\frac{V(A\cap B)}{V_A+V_B-V(A\cap B)}
\text{age at output}=\text{capture-to-finish time}=\text{wait}+L,\qquad \text{output rate}=\min\!\left(\tfrac1T,\tfrac1L\right)\ \text{(newest-frame policy)}

Lab

  2. Make box B taller (raise its top) with the same footprint. The BEV IoU does not change, but the 3-D IoU does. This is why 3-D evaluation is stricter than BEV evaluation.
  3. In the latency lab, set the sensor to 10 Hz and the detector to 130 ms, then switch between latest frame only and queue every frame. Read the age of the newest result in each case.

Common traps
  • Comparing a BEV score with a 3-D score. They are different numbers with different thresholds. Always state which one you report.
  • Axis-aligned boxes for rotated objects. The overlap formulas above assume boxes whose sides are parallel to the axes; a rotated box needs a polygon intersection.
  • Forgetting frames. A detection is in the camera frame. Transform it to the robot base before planning, or the arm reaches for the wrong place.
  • Latency hides in the pipeline. A fast model that waits for a slow stage is still slow. Measure the age of the result, not only the model's time.
Where it shows up in AI
  • PointPillars and CenterPoint are well-known 3-D detectors for lidar point clouds; both are evaluated on driving datasets such as KITTI and nuScenes with BEV and 3-D IoU metrics.
  • Bin-picking and warehouse systems pair a 2-D or 3-D detector with a tracker, so that a detection from an earlier frame can be carried forward to the moment of grasping.
  • Latency budgets are a design input: a 30 Hz camera with a 200 ms model needs frame skipping, a smaller model or a faster accelerator, before any accuracy is considered.
Pipeline positionStage 2 (estimate, the object layer), feeding stage 3 (plan). The loop rate is set by the sensor (often 10–30 Hz) and the detector latency together; the stored product is a list of boxes with class, pose, size and timestamp.

Check yourself

1. A pixel is at u = 400 px, depth Z = 2 m, with f = 800 px and cx = 320 px. What is its lateral position X?
X = (400 − 320) × 2 / 800 = 0.2 m to the right of the optical axis.
2. Two BEV boxes C = [0,2] × [0,2] and D = [1,3] × [0,2] (metres). What is their IoU?
Overlap = 1 × 2 = 2 m²; areas 4 and 4; union = 4 + 4 − 2 = 6 m². IoU = 2/6 = 0.333.
3. A 20 Hz sensor (50 ms period) feeds a detector that takes 80 ms per frame, and every frame is queued. What happens to the age of results over time, and what is the fix?
Each frame waits 30 ms longer than the one before (80 − 50 = 30 ms), so the age grows without bound. Fix it by processing only the newest frame. The output then runs at 1 / 0.080 = 12.5 results per second, with the age staying bounded.

Takeaways

  • A depth pixel lifts to a 3-D point with X = (u-c_x)Z/f; a 3-D box adds size and heading.
  • IoU is overlap over union. Compare BEV and 3-D scores only with their own thresholds.
  • The sensor rate and the detector latency together set how fresh a detection is; the newest-frame policy keeps the lag bounded.
4.4

6-DoF object pose estimation

The questionA tray holds a bolt whose CAD model the robot knows. From one camera image, where exactly is the bolt, and how is it turned? The gripper needs six numbers, three for position and three for orientation, and it needs to know how far to trust them.

Intuition

A pose has six degrees of freedom (6-DoF): three to say where the object is and three to say which way it faces. A camera throws away depth when it makes an image, so one picture cannot give the six numbers by itself. What it does give is a set of keypoints: points on the object whose positions on the model are known and whose pixels are visible. Each keypoint says "this model point appears at this pixel". With enough such matches, the pose is the one that makes the model's projection land on the observed pixels. This is the Perspective-n-Point (PnP) problem.

Build it up

Step 1: a rotation has three numbers, in one of several forms. A rotation matrix R has nine entries but only three degrees of freedom: its columns are unit vectors that are perpendicular, and its determinant is +1. Euler angles (yaw, pitch, roll) are three numbers that are easy to read, but they have a gimbal-lock problem at pitch = ±90°, and their meaning depends on the order of the turns. A quaternion q = (w, x, y, z) with w^2+x^2+y^2+z^2=1 is the form most solvers use. For a turn by angle \theta about a unit axis \mathbf u: q = (\cos\tfrac\theta2,\ \sin\tfrac\theta2\,\mathbf u).

Worked example: a 90° turn about z as a quaternion

\theta = 90^\circ, \mathbf u = (0,0,1): q = (\cos 45^\circ, 0, 0, \sin 45^\circ) = (0.7071, 0, 0, 0.7071). Its matrix is \begin{bmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}, the same rotation as in Lesson 4.1. The quaternion -q gives the same rotation, so a solver must treat the two signs as one answer.

Step 2: projecting a keypoint. Let the object's keypoint be X in the object frame. The camera sees it at X_c = RX + t, and the pinhole model projects it to u = f\,X_c/Z_c + c_x, v = f\,Y_c/Z_c + c_y. Pose estimation runs this in reverse: the observed (u,v) and the known X are given, and the solver searches for (R,t).

Worked example: project three keypoints

Camera: f = 800 px, (c_x, c_y) = (320, 240). Pose: the 90° turn about z from above, and t = (0,0,2) m. Recall that R(x,y,z) = (-y, x, z) for this turn.

  • X = (0.10, 0, 0): X_c = (0, 0.10, 2), so u = 800\cdot 0/2 + 320 = 320, v = 800\cdot 0.10/2 + 240 = 280. Pixel (320, 280).
  • X = (0, 0.05, 0): X_c = (-0.05, 0, 2), so u = 800\cdot(-0.05)/2 + 320 = 300, v = 240. Pixel (300, 240).
  • X = (0.10, 0.05, 0): X_c = (-0.05, 0.10, 2), pixel (300, 280).

Moving the object farther away (t_z = 3 m) shrinks every pixel offset to two-thirds of its size: a 0.10 m keypoint offset that spans 40 px at 2 m spans about 27 px at 3 m. Depth is the hardest of the six numbers to pin down.

Step 3: solving for the pose. Each keypoint gives two equations (its u and v). Six unknowns need at least three keypoints in principle, and the exact three-point problem (P3P) can have up to four solutions. With four or more well-spread keypoints the solution is usually unique, and a robust solver (such as EPnP followed by refinement) starts from a closed-form guess. The refinement minimises the reprojection error, the sum of squared pixel differences, with a Levenberg–Marquardt iteration: a damped Gauss–Newton step that moves the six numbers toward lower error.

Step 4: how sure are we? Near the solution, the residual Jacobian J tells how each pixel changes with each of the six numbers. The approximate pose covariance is \sigma^2(J^{\top}J)^{-1}. For a small object far away, the depth entries are large, so the depth is the least certain number. A grasp planner must add margin for that uncertainty.

The formal definition

\pi(X_c)=\begin{pmatrix} f\,X_c/Z_c + c_x\\ f\,Y_c/Z_c + c_y\end{pmatrix},\qquad X_c = RX+t
\min_{R,\,t}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert \pi\!\left(R X_i + t\right)-u_i\right\rVert^{2}

  2. The residual of about 0.5 px is the image noise, not a mistake: the hidden true pose is known only to within the pixel noise of the observations.

  4. Move a slider by hand while the solver is stopped: the error readout updates at once. Find a pose that looks roughly right but has a visibly larger error.
Common traps
  • Symmetric objects. A bolt, a washer or a bowl can look identical after a turn. The pose is then ambiguous, and the error does not show it. Use a symmetry-aware metric and choose a grasp that works for every equivalent pose.
  • Euler-angle order. Yaw-pitch-roll and roll-pitch-yaw are different rotations. Always write the convention next to the numbers.
  • Inverting a pose. T_{co} (object in camera) and T_{oc} (camera in object) are inverses, not equal. Name the frames.
  • Reporting a pose without uncertainty. A pose with no covariance is an answer without a margin. Grasp planners need one.
Where it shows up in AI
  • The BOP benchmark (a standard evaluation suite for 6-DoF object pose) scores methods with symmetry-aware errors, so results from different papers are comparable.
  • Learned keypoint and dense-correspondence networks predict the 2-D pixels, and a classical PnP solver turns them into a pose. This split lets a neural network handle appearance while geometry stays exact.
  • Pose estimates feed directly into grasp planners and into the frame tree of the robot (Module 0), so a wrong frame here becomes a crash later.
Pipeline positionStage 2 (estimate): the object pose is part of the world model, and it is produced for each frame that contains the object (typically 5–30 Hz). The stored product is a named object pose, with its frame, timestamp and covariance.

Check yourself

1. A 0.10 m wide object is 1 m from a camera with f = 800 px. How wide is it in the image, and how wide at 2 m?
At 1 m: 800 × 0.10 / 1 = 80 px. At 2 m: 800 × 0.10 / 2 = 40 px. Doubling the depth halves the image size.
2. The quaternions (0.7071, 0, 0, 0.7071) and (−0.7071, 0, 0, −0.7071) are both listed for one object. Are they two poses?
No. q and -q encode the same rotation: every entry of the rotation matrix is a product of two quaternion components, so flipping all four signs changes nothing. A solver should compare rotations (matrices or angles), not raw quaternion components.
3. Why does the solver use at least four well-spread keypoints when three are enough in principle?
Three keypoints can give up to four valid poses (P3P), so the answer is ambiguous. A fourth point usually selects one solution, and extra points average out pixel noise, so the estimate is more stable.

Takeaways

  • A 6-DoF pose is a rotation and a translation; a camera gives keypoint-to-pixel matches, and PnP solves for the pose that explains them.
  • Refine with reprojection error and report the covariance; depth is usually the least certain number.
  • Symmetry makes some poses ambiguous; the solver cannot fix that, so the grasp must allow for it.
4.5

Grasp detection

The questionA bin holds many unknown parts, and a depth image shows their tops. Where should a two-finger gripper close, at what angle, with what opening, so that the part is lifted and does not slip out? Most of the answer is geometry and friction; a few candidates are worth scoring.

Intuition

Pinch a pencil between finger and thumb. It stays put because the two contact points push toward each other along the same line, and because the friction at each contact is large enough to stop the pencil sliding. A parallel-jaw grasp is exactly this: a centre, a jaw-closing direction and an opening. A good grasp has two contacts whose surface normals point straight at each other (the grasp is antipodal), and whose pushing forces stay inside the friction limit.

Build it up

Step 1: a grasp as a rectangle. Looking down at a depth image, a parallel-jaw grasp is a small rectangle: a centre (x,y), an angle \theta for the jaw line, and a width w for the opening. A 3-D grasp adds a height and an approach direction, but the top-down rectangle carries most of the information, which is why many learned detectors predict it.

Step 2: the friction cone. A contact can push along its normal, and it can also push a little sideways, up to the friction limit. With friction coefficient \mu, every force inside the cone of half-angle \alpha = \arctan\mu around the inward normal is safe. The jaw force must stay inside that cone at both contacts.

Worked example: a box, three grasp angles, μ = 0.5

The part is an 80 × 40 mm box, turned by \varphi = 20^\circ. With \mu = 0.5, \alpha = \arctan 0.5 = 26.6^\circ. Let d be the jaw direction and n_1, n_2 the outward normals at the two contacts. The grasp is safe when the angle between d and n_1, and between -d and n_2, are both at most α.

  • θ = 20°. The jaws close across the two 80 mm faces; both contact normals are along the jaw line. Angles 0° and 0°, width 80 mm. Passes.
  • θ = 50°. The jaws still span 80 mm, but the jaw line meets each face 60° away from its normal. Fails: 60° is more than 26.6°, so the contact would slip.
  • θ = 110°. The jaws close across the two 40 mm faces; angles 0° and 0°, width 40 mm. Passes.

Lowering the friction to \mu = 0.2 gives \alpha = 11.3^\circ. The θ = 20° grasp still passes, because its contact angles are 0°. But any grasp whose contact angles lie between 11.3° and 26.6° passes on a dry part and fails on a wet one.

Step 3: scoring many candidates. A learned detector proposes many rectangles, filters out those that are too wide for the jaws or fail the antipodal and friction tests, and ranks the rest by a quality score (how much margin they have, how well the object fills the jaws). The robot then executes the best-ranked candidate that is reachable.

The formal definition

g=(x,\,y,\,\theta,\,w),\qquad w \le w_{\max},\qquad \alpha=\arctan\mu
\angle(\mathbf d,\ \mathbf n_1)\le\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad \angle(-\mathbf d,\ \mathbf n_2)\le\alpha

Lab

Try this
  1. Press Sweep θ. Watch the jaw line turn. The grasp turns green only in some ranges: note the angles where the contact turns red and why (the yellow friction-cone edges no longer contain the purple normal).
  2. Set the friction to 0.2 and sweep again. Count how many green ranges disappear.
  3. Set the jaw maximum to 70 mm. Find the angles where the width is fine but the grasp is still rejected for slip, and the angles where it is rejected for width.
  4. Change the object angle φ. The same θ gives a different result: a grasp is always a pair of object angle and jaw angle, not a jaw angle alone.
Common traps
  • Treating the top view as the whole object. A depth image shows only the visible surface; the hidden side may be a different shape, and the centre of mass may be far from the contacts.
  • Ignoring the opening. A grasp can be antipodal and still impossible if the object is wider than the jaws.
  • A fixed friction value. Real μ varies with material, dust and water. Plan with a margin, or use the smallest μ you expect.
  • Suction is not a parallel-jaw grasp. Suction cups need a seal on a flat, non-porous surface and use a different model; do not score them with the friction cone.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Dex-Net 2.0 learned grasp quality for bin picking from synthetic depth images, and the GraspNet-1Billion benchmark evaluates grasp detectors on cluttered scenes.
  • Learned detectors (convolutional and transformer-based) predict the grasp rectangle for every image pixel at once, so ranking candidates is cheap.
  • Grasp scores are also used as rewards or filters in learned manipulation policies, which links this lesson to Module 7.
Pipeline positionStage 2 (estimate, the grasp candidates) and the hand-off to stage 3 (plan). Grasp detectors run once per scene or once per re-grasp, typically in 50–300 ms, so they sit outside the fast control loop. The stored product is a ranked list of grasps in the robot frame.

Check yourself

1. μ = 1.0. Is a grasp with contact angles of 40° and 40° safe?
α = arctan 1.0 = 45°. Both angles are below 45°, so the grasp is safe. At μ = 0.5 (α = 26.6°) the same grasp would slip.
2. A grasp has contact normals perfectly opposite, a width of 90 mm, and the jaws open to 80 mm. Pass or fail?
Fail. The antipodal and friction tests are satisfied, but the width (90 mm) exceeds the jaw maximum (80 mm). The jaws cannot close on the object at that spot.
3. A grasp passes at μ = 0.5 with contact angles of 20°. Wet conditions reduce μ to 0.2. Does it still pass?
No. With μ = 0.2, α = arctan 0.2 = 11.3°, and 20° exceeds it. The same geometry is safe in dry conditions and unsafe in wet ones, so friction must be part of the plan.

Takeaways

  • A parallel-jaw grasp is a centre, a jaw angle and an opening; it is useful only if the contacts are antipodal and inside the friction cone.
  • \alpha = \arctan\mu: lower friction narrows the set of safe angles.
  • Learned detectors rank many candidate rectangles; the geometric tests still decide which ones are physically possible.
4.6

Visual servoing

The questionA camera is mounted on the robot's wrist. The task is to move the camera until a marker appears exactly where a reference photo shows it, even though nobody has measured the marker's position precisely. Which way should the camera move at each instant, and how fast?

Intuition

The camera already tells you the error: the marker is 30 pixels to the left of where it should be. Move the camera so that the marker moves right, and the error shrinks. Visual servoing closes the control loop on image features rather than on a measured 3-D position. In image-based visual servoing (IBVS), the controller works only with pixels. It never needs to know where the marker is in the room, only how the image changes when the camera moves.

Build it up

Step 1: one feature, one axis. Let a feature sit at normalised image coordinates (x, y) = (X/Z, Y/Z), where Z is its depth. If the camera slides sideways at speed v_x, the feature moves the other way in the image, at \dot x = -v_x/Z. Closer features move faster.

Worked example: a single axis, numbers

The feature is at depth Z = 0.5 m and its error is e = 0.05 (normalised units). Choose the gain \lambda = 0.5\ \text{s}^{-1} and the control v_x = \lambda Z e:

v_x = 0.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.05 = 0.0125\ \text{m/s}
\dot x = -\frac{v_x}{Z} = -\frac{0.0125}{0.5} = -0.025 = -\lambda e

So the error decays exponentially: e(t) = 0.05\,e^{-0.5t}. After 1 s it is 0.0303; after 2 s, 0.0184; after 3 s, 0.0112. The time constant is 1/\lambda = 2 s.

Step 2: the interaction matrix. For a camera with translational velocity (v_x, v_y, v_z) and rotational velocity (\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z), a point at (x,y) and depth Z moves as

\dot x=-\frac{v_x}{Z}+\frac{x}{Z}v_z+xy\,\omega_x-(1+x^2)\,\omega_y+y\,\omega_z,\qquad \dot y=-\frac{v_y}{Z}+\frac{y}{Z}v_z+(1+y^2)\,\omega_x-xy\,\omega_y-x\,\omega_z

Written as \dot s = L\,v, the matrix L is the image Jacobian (interaction matrix). The lab uses the planar part only (v_x, v_y, \omega_z), where \dot x = -v_x/Z + y\,\omega_z and \dot y = -v_y/Z - x\,\omega_z. A single point gives two equations for the three unknowns, so the system is underdetermined; four points give eight equations.

Step 3: the control law. Let e = s - s^* be the current feature positions minus the desired ones. Pick the camera velocity that makes \dot e = -\lambda e: v = -\lambda L^{+}e, where L^{+} = (L^{\top}L)^{-1}L^{\top} is the least-squares inverse. Using more features than unknowns averages out measurement noise and keeps one bad detection from dominating.

Worked example: the depth is only an estimate

The controller needs Z for L, but in practice only an estimate \hat Z is available. On one axis, \hat L^{+} = -\hat Z and the command is v = \lambda\hat Z e, so the true feature motion is \dot x = -\lambda(\hat Z/Z)\,e. If \hat Z = 1.4\,Z, the decay rate becomes 1.4\lambda: faster. If \hat Z = 0.6\,Z, it becomes 0.6\lambda: slower, but still converging. The error keeps falling for moderate depth errors, which is why IBVS works without a precise depth.

The formal definition

\dot{\mathbf s}=L\,\mathbf v,\qquad \mathbf v=-\lambda\,\hat L^{+}\,\mathbf e,\qquad \mathbf e=\mathbf s-\mathbf s^{*}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \dot{\mathbf e}\approx-\lambda\,L\hat L^{+}\mathbf e
s, s*current and desired image features (normalised coordinates for points)
efeature error, a vector of length 2 × (number of points)
vcamera velocity command (m/s for translation, rad/s for rotation)
L, L̂the true and the estimated interaction matrix, which uses the true and estimated depths
λgain (s-1); it sets the time constant 1/λ

Common traps
  • Losing features from the field of view. A large move can carry a point off the edge of the image, and then the controller has no measurement. Plan the path or the initial pose so that the features stay visible.
  • The rotation problem. With a large initial roll or yaw, IBVS can move the camera along a curved path that swings through the scene. Position-based servoing avoids that but needs a 3-D pose estimate.
  • Collinear features. If the points lie on one line, L loses rank and the control becomes unreliable. Use points spread across the image.
  • High gain plus delay. A large λ with a slow camera (or a slow image pipeline) can oscillate. The continuous-time analysis is only a guide; check the loop at the real frame rate.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Classic IBVS is the reference method for camera-guided arms in assembly and surgical robots, and the 2006 Chaumette and Hutchinson tutorials are the standard references.
  • Learned visual policies (Module 7 and Module 8) learn the same mapping from image to motion, but they usually lack the explicit error and gain that make classical servoing easy to analyse.
  • Visual feedback also runs inside grasp execution: the robot re-centres the gripper on the object as it approaches, using the same loop.
Pipeline positionStage 4 (control), with stage 1 (sense) supplying the feature measurements. The loop runs at the camera rate (typically 30–60 Hz), and the stored product is the error history and the commanded velocity.

Check yourself

1. The depth Z doubles from 0.5 m to 1.0 m, and the camera speed vx is unchanged. How does the feature speed ẋ change?
It halves: ẋ = −vx/Z, so doubling Z halves the image motion for the same camera speed. The controller's factor Z in v = λZe compensates: a distant feature makes it command a larger camera motion for the same image error.
2. λ = 0.5 s⁻¹ and e = 0.05 at time 0. What is e after 2 s?
e(2) = 0.05 · e−0.5 × 2 = 0.05 · e−1 = 0.05 × 0.3679 = 0.0184.
3. Why use four points for a planar problem with three unknowns?
Four points give eight equations for three unknowns, so the least-squares solution averages out pixel noise and tolerates one bad detection. A single point cannot determine all three motions; using more points than unknowns is the usual design.

Takeaways

  • Image-based servoing moves the camera to reduce pixel error: v = -\lambda L^{+}e, with L the interaction matrix.
  • Depth enters L; an estimated depth slows convergence but does not usually break it.
  • Watch the feature field of view, the initial rotation and the gain: these, more than the algorithm, decide whether the servo behaves well.
