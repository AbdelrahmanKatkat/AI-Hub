Module 6 · Optimization

Training a model means walking downhill on a landscape you can never see from above. This module shows what that landscape looks like, why a step opposite to the gradient works, how fast it converges (and why it sometimes zig-zags or explodes), how noise, momentum and adaptive scaling change the walk, why the step size should change over time, and how rules such as "keep the weights small" become constrained optimisation.

6.1

Loss landscapes & convexity

The questionA language model has 100 million adjustable knobs and one number, the loss, that says how badly it is doing. Somewhere in a 100-million-dimensional space there are knob settings with a small loss. How do we even describe "a lowest point" in a space we cannot draw — and if our search stops where the slope is zero, how do we know we have found a valley floor and not a hilltop or a mountain pass?

Intuition: a mountain range hidden in fog

Imagine standing on a hillside in thick fog. Your position on the ground is the setting of the knobs \theta (with two knobs, east–west and north–south are the two coordinates). Your altitude is the loss L(\theta): high ground means a bad model, low ground a good one. The whole surface of altitudes over all positions is the loss landscape. Training is the quest to walk to a low place when all you can perceive is the ground right under your feet.

Whenever the ground under your feet is perfectly level — the slope is zero in every direction — you are at a critical point. There are three kinds, and the fog makes them feel identical:

  • Minimum — the bottom of a bowl. Every direction you step leads uphill.
  • Maximum — the top of a dome. Every direction leads downhill.
  • Saddle — a mountain pass or a horse's saddle: uphill if you walk along the ridge line, downhill if you cross it. Level ground, yet neither top nor bottom.

The slope alone (the gradient) cannot tell these apart. What distinguishes them is how the ground curves — and curvature is what the second derivative measures.

Build it up

One knob. With a single parameter, the landscape is an ordinary curve f(x). Critical points satisfy f'(x)=0. The second derivative f''(x) is "how fast the slope is changing": positive means the curve bends upward like a smile (bottom of a bowl), negative means it bends downward like a frown (top of a dome).

Worked example — one knob

f(x)=x^3-3x. Then f'(x)=3x^2-3=0\Rightarrow x=\pm1, and f''(x)=6x.

At x=1: f''=6>0, a local minimum with f(1)=-2. At x=-1: f''=-6<0, a local maximum with f(-1)=2. Notice the minimum is only local: far to the left, x^3-3x goes down to -\infty, so there is no lowest point at all.

Many knobs. With n knobs there are n directions to bend in, and the second derivative becomes the Hessian matrix H of all second partial derivatives (Module 2). Because H is symmetric, it has real eigenvalues \lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n with perpendicular eigenvectors. Each eigenvector is a "principal direction" of the landscape and its eigenvalue is the curvature along that direction: positive → bends up, negative → bends down. At a critical point:

  • all \lambda_i>0 → local minimum;
  • all \lambda_i<0 → local maximum;
  • some positive, some negative → saddle;
  • some \lambda_i=0 → the test is inconclusive (look at higher-order terms; think of x^4 versus x^3 at 0).
Worked example — a saddle in disguise

f(x,y)=x^2+4xy+y^2 looks harmless, like a sum of squares. Gradient: \nabla f=(2x+4y,\;4x+2y), which is zero only at the origin. Hessian: H=\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\4&2\end{pmatrix}.

Eigenvalues: \det(H-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2-16=0\Rightarrow\lambda=2\pm4, i.e. 6 and -2. One of each sign: a saddle.

Check by walking: along the eigenvector (1,1) (eigenvalue 6) we get f(t,t)=t^2+4t^2+t^2=6t^2, uphill. Along (1,-1) (eigenvalue −2) we get f(t,-t)=t^2-4t^2+t^2=-2t^2, downhill. The origin is a pass.

Convexity: landscapes with no traps

Some landscapes are friendly: a single smooth bowl. We capture this with convexity. Pick any two points on the graph and stretch a string (a chord) between them. If the string never dips below the graph — for every pair of points — the function is convex. A bowl passes the test; a hill with two humps fails.

Why this matters: for a convex function every local minimum is a global minimum, and gradient-based methods have strong guarantees. A convex set is the same idea for regions: the straight segment between any two points of the set stays inside it (a disc is convex; a crescent is not). Convex functions are easy to recognise through curvature: f''\ge0 everywhere in 1D, or the Hessian having all eigenvalues \ge0 ("positive semidefinite") everywhere in many dimensions.

Worked example — the chord test with numbers

Take f(x)=x^2 and the points x=0 and x=2, so f=0 and f=4. At the midpoint x=1 the chord has height \tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12\cdot4=2, while the curve has height f(1)=1. Curve ≤ chord ✓ (and this holds for every chord, so x^2 is convex).

Now f(x)=-x^2: values 0 and −4, chord height at the midpoint -2, curve height f(1)=-1>-2. The curve rises above the chord: not convex. A single failing chord is a proof of non-convexity.

Local versus global. In a non-convex landscape a local minimum is only the lowest point nearby; a deeper valley may exist elsewhere. Modern neural networks are non-convex, yet they train well. Two reasons are commonly given. First, in very high dimensions a critical point is almost never a minimum: it needs all n curvatures to be positive, and for large n the overwhelming majority of critical points are saddles with at least one downhill direction, so you can keep going. Second, many of the minima that are found have very similar loss, so landing in "a" good valley is enough. Neither fact is a theorem about all networks, but together they explain why the pessimism about non-convexity has largely not matched practice.

The formal definitions

\nabla f(\theta^\star)=0\quad(\text{critical point}),\qquad H=\nabla^2 f(\theta^\star),\quad H_{ij}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial\theta_i\,\partial\theta_j}
f\ \text{convex}\iff f\big((1-t)a+tb\big)\le(1-t)f(a)+t\,f(b)\quad\text{for all }a,b\text{ and }t\in[0,1]
θ★a point where we are testing for a minimum
∇fthe gradient: the vector of slopes in each knob direction (Module 2)
H, ∇²fthe Hessian: the symmetric matrix of second derivatives = curvature in every direction
λᵢeigenvalues of H; the sign of each is whether that principal direction bends up or down
(1−t)a + t bthe point a fraction t of the way from a to b (t = 0 is a, t = 1 is b)

Equivalent statement: convex ⇔ the Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere (when f is twice differentiable).

Common traps
  • "Gradient zero means minimum." It only means critical point. Saddles and maxima also have zero gradient.
  • "Convex means there is a minimum." e^x is convex and never reaches a minimum. Convexity says every local minimum, if one exists, is global.
  • Zero curvature is silent. f''(0)=0 for x^4 (a minimum) and for x^3 (neither). The second-derivative test says nothing.
  • Local minima are not necessarily bad. In deep learning the real trouble is more often flat plateaus and badly scaled valleys (next lessons) than poor local minima.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Convex models: linear regression with squared error, logistic regression, support vector machines. Their loss has a single basin, so training always reaches the optimum. The Hessian of mean squared error for a linear model is \tfrac{2}{N}X^\top X, which is positive semidefinite because v^\top X^\top Xv=\|Xv\|^2\ge0.
  • Non-convex models: every neural network with hidden layers. Training still works, and tools such as Hessian eigenvalue spectra and loss-surface visualisation are used to study how "sharp" or "flat" a solution is.
  • Symmetry saddles: permuting the neurons of a hidden layer gives a different parameter vector with an identical function, so landscapes contain huge families of equivalent minima.

Check yourself

1. Classify the critical points of f(x) = x⁴ − 2x².
f′ = 4x³ − 4x = 4x(x−1)(x+1) = 0 → x = 0, ±1. f″ = 12x² − 4. At x = 0: f″ = −4 < 0, a local maximum (f = 0). At x = ±1: f″ = 8 > 0, two local minima (f = −1). Two equal minima: non-convex.
2. At a critical point the Hessian is [[3, 0],[0, −1]]. What is it, and which direction goes downhill?
Eigenvalues 3 and −1 (the matrix is diagonal): one positive, one negative → a saddle. It curves up along the first axis and down along the second, so moving along the second axis (either way) decreases f.
3. Why is mean squared error for a linear model, L(w) = (1/N)‖Xw − y‖², convex?
Its Hessian is (2/N)XᵀX. For any vector v, vᵀXᵀXv = ‖Xv‖² ≥ 0, so the Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere: every eigenvalue ≥ 0, hence convex. Every local minimum is global.

Takeaways

  • Loss landscape = altitude over parameter space; training walks downhill in fog.
  • ∇f = 0 marks critical points; the Hessian's eigenvalue signs classify them as minimum, maximum or saddle.
  • Convex ⇔ chords never dip below the graph ⇔ Hessian ⪰ 0; then local minima are global.
  • Deep nets are non-convex, but high-dimensional saddles can be escaped and good minima are plentiful.
6.2

Gradient descent

The questionYou are on the foggy hillside again. You can feel the slope under your feet but see nothing further. Which direction should you step, how long should the step be, and — the question that decides whether training works at all — when does a sequence of such steps actually arrive at the bottom instead of wandering, zig-zagging or flying off the mountain?

Intuition: the ball and the fog

Place a ball on the hillside. It does not need eyes: gravity pushes it opposite to the slope, i.e. straight along the steepest downhill direction, and it keeps going until the ground is flat. Gradient descent is exactly that, in slow motion and with steps we choose. At each point: (1) feel the local slope, (2) take a small step in the downhill direction, (3) repeat.

The slope in several dimensions is the gradient \nabla L, the vector that points uphill in the steepest way, and whose length is how steep that is. So the downhill direction is -\nabla L. The step length is controlled by a number \eta>0 called the learning rate (or step size): small values are cautious, large values are bold.

Build it up: why stepping against the gradient must help

We never see the whole landscape, only its tangent. Zoom into a tiny region and any smooth landscape looks like a tilted flat plane — that is the first-order Taylor expansion (Module 2):

L(\theta+\Delta)\;\approx\;L(\theta)+\nabla L(\theta)\cdot\Delta

The term \nabla L\cdot\Delta says how much the altitude changes when we move by \Delta. Among all moves of a fixed length \|\Delta\|=\epsilon, the dot product is \|\nabla L\|\,\epsilon\cos\alpha where \alpha is the angle between \Delta and the gradient. It is most negative when \cos\alpha=-1, i.e. when \Delta points exactly opposite to the gradient. So −∇L is the direction of steepest descent. Choosing \Delta=-\eta\nabla L gives

L(\theta-\eta\nabla L)\;\approx\;L(\theta)-\eta\,\|\nabla L(\theta)\|^2\;\le\;L(\theta)

The loss decreases by \eta\|\nabla L\|^2, a non-negative amount. But the key word is approximately: the Taylor plane is only accurate nearby. If the step is too long, the landscape curves and the real loss can go up. A cleaner statement: suppose the curvature never exceeds L_{\max} (we say the gradient is L-smooth; we call this number L in the quadratic below). Adding the second-order term bounds the true change by -\eta\|\nabla L\|^2+\tfrac12L_{\max}\eta^2\|\nabla L\|^2=-\eta\bigl(1-\tfrac{L_{\max}\eta}{2}\bigr)\|\nabla L\|^2, which is negative exactly when \eta<2/L_{\max}. Curvature sets the speed limit.

The algorithm, in one line:

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\,\nabla L(\theta_k)
Worked example — one knob, by hand

Loss L(w)=(w-3)^2+1, so L'(w)=2(w-3). Start at w_0=0, learning rate \eta=0.1.

Step 1: slope 2(0-3)=-6; w_1=0-0.1\cdot(-6)=0.6; loss 10 → 6.76.

Step 2: slope 2(0.6-3)=-4.8; w_2=0.6+0.48=1.08; loss → 4.6864.

Step 3: slope 2(1.08-3)=-3.84; w_3=1.08+0.384=1.464; loss → 3.3593.

Pattern: the distance to the minimum (3) is multiplied by 1-2\eta=0.8 each step: 3,\,2.4,\,1.92,\,1.536,\dots so w_k=3-3\cdot0.8^k (check k=3: 3-3\cdot0.512=1.464 ✓). Notice steps shrink as we approach: the slope shrinks, so even with a fixed \eta the steps naturally slow down near the bottom.

Convergence on a quadratic: step size and the condition number

Quadratics are the model problem of optimisation, because near any minimum a smooth loss is a quadratic (Taylor again) with A= Hessian. Take f(x)=\tfrac12x^\top Ax with A symmetric positive definite; the minimum is at 0 and \nabla f=Ax. One step: x_{k+1}=x_k-\eta Ax_k=(I-\eta A)x_k. Describe x in the eigenvector basis of A (Module 1): each coordinate evolves independently and is multiplied by (1-\eta\lambda_i) every step. So after k steps, coordinate i is (1-\eta\lambda_i)^k times its start. It shrinks to zero iff |1-\eta\lambda_i|<1, i.e. 0<\eta\lambda_i<2. For all coordinates at once we need

0<\eta<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}=\frac2L

(Here L=\lambda_{\max} is the steepest curvature.) The overall speed is set by the worst coordinate, the factor \rho=\max_i|1-\eta\lambda_i|. The best fixed step balances the steepest direction (which wants small \eta) against the flattest (which wants large \eta): \eta^\star=\frac{2}{\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max}}, giving

\rho^\star=\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1},\qquad\kappa=\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}}\ \ (\text{condition number})

When \kappa=1 (a perfectly round bowl) \rho^\star=0: one step lands on the minimum. When \kappa is huge, \rho^\star\approx1-\tfrac2\kappa: the iterates barely creep. The picture: a long thin valley. The steep walls force a small \eta (so we do not bounce out), but then progress along the gentle floor is painfully slow — the ball zig-zags from wall to wall while inching down the valley.

Worked example — zig-zag on an ellipse

f(x,y)=\tfrac12(x^2+10y^2), so A=\operatorname{diag}(1,10), \lambda_{\max}=L=10, \kappa=10. The limit is 2/L=0.2. Take \eta=0.18 and start at (10,1), where f=55.

The gradient is (x,10y). Step 1: gradient (10,10) → (10-1.8,\;1-1.8)=(8.2,\,-0.8), f=36.82. Step 2: gradient (8.2,-8) → (8.2-1.476,\;-0.8+1.44)=(6.724,\,0.64), f=24.65.

Look at y: 1\to-0.8\to0.64, multiplied by 1-0.18\cdot10=-0.8 each time — it flips sign (zig-zag) and slowly shrinks. Look at x: multiplied by 1-0.18=0.82, a smooth slow decay. The slow coordinate dictates the total time.

How long? With the best step \eta^\star=2/11, \rho=9/11\approx0.818, so shrinking the error by 1000× takes \ln(10^{-3})/\ln(9/11)\approx34.4, i.e. about 35 steps. For \kappa=100: \rho=99/101 and it takes about 346 steps — ten times longer, for a ten-times larger condition number.

The formal definition

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\,\nabla L(\theta_k);\qquad\text{on }f=\tfrac12x^\top Ax:\quad x_{k+1}=(I-\eta A)x_k,\ \ \text{converges}\iff0<\eta<\tfrac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}
θₖthe knob settings after k steps (θ₀ is the starting guess)
ηlearning rate / step size, a positive number you choose
∇L(θₖ)the gradient at the current point: uphill direction, length = steepness
Asymmetric positive definite matrix = Hessian of the quadratic

Common traps
  • A learning rate that is fine in one direction is fatal in another. The limit 2/\lambda_{\max} is set by the steepest direction, even if you only care about the gentle one.
  • Loss going up means η is too big (or the gradient is wrong), not that "training failed". Halve η first.
  • "Converged" means the gradient is small, not that the loss is zero or that you are at the global minimum — you may sit on a plateau or a saddle.
  • The gradient lives in parameter space, not data space. It tells you how to change \theta, not the inputs.
Where it shows up in AI
  • The training loop. Every framework repeats the same three lines: loss = model(x, y), loss.backward() (compute \nabla L, Module 7), optimizer.step() (apply \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla L or a refined version).
  • Feature scaling. For linear regression the Hessian is the feature covariance (times 2/N). If one feature has variance 1 and another 100, then κ ≈ 100 and descent zig-zags; standardising features makes κ ≈ 1. This is why we normalise data.
  • Sharpness and learning-rate limits. In deep networks the largest Hessian eigenvalue ("sharpness") really does bound the stable learning rate; training runs often hover at the edge \eta\approx2/\lambda_{\max}.

Check yourself

1. L(w) = w², η = 0.25, w₀ = 4. Compute w₁ and w₂ and the shrink factor per step.
L′ = 2w. w₁ = 4 − 0.25·8 = 2. w₂ = 2 − 0.25·4 = 1. Factor 1 − 2η = 0.5 each step. (Here L = f″ = 2, ηL = 0.5.)
2. For L(w) = 5w², what is the largest learning rate for which gradient descent converges?
f″ = 10, so the curvature limit is 2/10 = 0.2. For 0 < η < 0.2 the factor |1 − 10η| < 1; at η = 0.2 it equals −1 (oscillates forever); above it diverges.
3. A = diag(2, 50). Find κ, the best fixed η and the resulting ρ.
κ = 50/2 = 25. η* = 2/(2+50) = 1/26 ≈ 0.0385. ρ* = (κ−1)/(κ+1) = 24/26 ≈ 0.923, so the error shrinks by about 8% per step; ×1000 reduction takes ln(10⁻³)/ln(0.923) ≈ 86 steps.

Takeaways

  • θ ← θ − η∇L; the first-order Taylor expansion shows −∇L is the steepest descent direction and the loss drops by ≈ η‖∇L‖².
  • On a quadratic every eigen-direction shrinks by (1 − ηλᵢ); convergence needs 0 < η < 2/λₘₐₓ.
  • Speed is governed by the condition number κ: best rate (κ−1)/(κ+1). Large κ = zig-zag.
  • Scaling inputs and conditioning the problem are as important as the algorithm.
6.3

Stochastic & mini-batch gradient descent

The questionA language model trains on a trillion tokens. The true gradient is the average of per-example gradients over all of them. One exact gradient step would require reading the entire dataset — and we need millions of steps. How can we take useful steps without ever computing the true gradient?

Intuition: polling instead of a census

To learn the average opinion of a country you do not ask everyone: you ask a random sample. The sample average is not exact, but it is unbiased (right on average) and gets more accurate as the sample grows. Stochastic gradient descent does the same with gradients: estimate the full-data gradient from a random handful of examples, step as if it were exact, and repeat with a fresh handful. Each step is noisier than a full-batch step, but it is cheap — and we can afford hundreds of thousands of them.

Picture the fog again, but now your compass is slightly drunk: each reading points roughly downhill with a random wobble. If the wobble averages out to zero, you still make steady progress on the whole, only along a shaky path.

Build it up

The training loss is an average over N examples: L(\theta)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\ell_i(\theta) where \ell_i is the loss on example i. Gradients are linear, so the true gradient is g=\frac1N\sum_i\nabla\ell_i. A mini-batch is a random subset \mathcal B of size B and its gradient estimate is

\hat g_B=\frac1B\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}\nabla\ell_i(\theta),\qquad\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\hat g_B

It is unbiased. Each example is equally likely to be chosen, so \mathbb E[\hat g_B]=g. Its noise shrinks with batch size. Let \sigma^2=\frac1N\sum_i\|\nabla\ell_i-g\|^2 be how much single-example gradients disagree with the mean. Averaging B independent samples divides the variance by B (Module 3), so \mathrm{Var}(\hat g_B)=\sigma^2/B and the typical error is \sigma/\sqrt B. (Sampling without replacement multiplies the variance by the finite-population factor \frac{N-B}{N-1}, which is exactly 0 when B=N.)

Worked example — three data points

Data a=\{1,2,6\}, per-example loss \ell_i(w)=\tfrac12(w-a_i)^2, so \ell_i'=w-a_i. The mean loss is minimised at the mean \bar a=3. Stand at w=0.

Per-example gradients: -1,-2,-6. The true gradient is their mean: -3. Their spread: \sigma^2=\frac{(2)^2+(1)^2+(-3)^2}{3}=\frac{14}{3}\approx4.67, so \sigma\approx2.16.

Batch size 1 with \eta=0.5: picking example 1 gives step 0.5\cdot1=0.5 (the true step would be 1.5); picking example 6 gives step 3. Wildly different, but the average of the three possible steps is (0.5+1+3)/3=1.5: unbiased ✓.

Batch size 2 (without replacement): the three possible batches give gradients -1.5,\,-3.5,\,-4. Mean -3 ✓ (unbiased again). Their variance is \frac{1.5^2+0.5^2+1^2}{3}\approx1.167; the formula \frac{\sigma^2}{B}\cdot\frac{N-B}{N-1}=\frac{4.667}{2}\cdot\frac12=1.167 ✓. Batch size 3: every batch is the whole set, zero noise.

What does the noise do? With a constant learning rate the iterate never settles: far from the minimum the true gradient is large and dominates, so SGD marches downhill nearly as well as full-batch. Close to the minimum the true gradient shrinks to 0 while the noise does not, so the iterate jitters around the optimum in a "noise ball" whose size grows with \eta and with \sigma^2/B. Two cures: decrease \eta over time (next lessons), or increase B. This is the central trade-off:

  • Small B: cheap steps, high noise, many steps per pass over the data (an epoch = one pass), good for exploring.
  • Large B: expensive steps, low noise, better hardware parallelism, but diminishing returns: beyond a "critical batch size" extra examples mostly repeat information.

Rules of thumb: noise falls as 1/\sqrt B, so 4× more data per step halves the noise, not quarters it. When B is multiplied by k it is common to multiply \eta by about k (the "linear scaling rule") because the batch gradient is a better estimate and tolerates a bigger step — a heuristic, valid up to a point. Some researchers argue the noise of small batches also nudges training towards flatter minima that generalise better; this is plausible but not a proven law.

The formal definition

\hat g_B=\frac1B\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}\nabla\ell_i(\theta_k),\quad\mathbb E[\hat g_B]=\nabla L(\theta_k),\quad\mathbb E\|\hat g_B-\nabla L\|^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{B}\cdot\frac{N-B}{N-1}
Ntotal number of training examples
Bmini-batch size, 1 ≤ B ≤ N (B = 1 is pure "SGD", B = N is full-batch)
𝓑the random set of B example indices chosen this step

Common traps
  • Mean vs sum reduction. If the loss is a sum over the batch instead of an average, the gradient (and the right learning rate) scales with B. Changing the batch size silently changes the effective step.
  • "Bigger batch is always better." It reduces noise but costs more per step; wall-clock time and generalisation can both get worse, and the optimal η changes with B.
  • Not shuffling. Batches must be random (or the data reshuffled each epoch); ordered data (all cats then all dogs) makes the gradient estimate heavily biased.
  • Judging by one noisy loss value. Mini-batch losses jump around; track a smoothed average.
Where it shows up in AI
  • All deep learning. "Training with Adam, batch 256" means mini-batch SGD with an adaptive step. Large language models use batches of millions of tokens.
  • Gradient accumulation. When the desired batch does not fit in memory, sum gradients from several small micro-batches before one update — mathematically the same as one big batch.
  • Data parallelism. Each GPU computes the gradient of its own shard of the batch, then the gradients are averaged (an "all-reduce") — it is precisely the mini-batch average formula.
  • Noise as a tool. The "gradient noise scale" estimates the batch size beyond which more data per step stops helping.

Check yourself

1. At batch size 1 the gradient noise (standard deviation) is 8. Roughly what is it at B = 16, and how big must B be to halve it again?
Noise scales as 1/√B: 8/√16 = 2. To halve 2 → 1 we need √B to double again: B = 64 (4× the data for 2× less noise).
2. Why is a random mini-batch gradient an unbiased estimate of the full gradient?
Every example has the same chance of being picked, so the expected value of the batch average equals the average over all examples, which is the true gradient (expectation is linear). Unbiased means correct on average; any single batch can still be far off.
3. Near the minimum, which method gets closer with a constant learning rate: full-batch GD or SGD with B = 1?
Full-batch GD: its gradient shrinks to 0 at the minimum, so the steps vanish. SGD's noise does not vanish, so it keeps jittering in a noise ball whose size grows with η. Decaying η (or a larger B) shrinks that ball.

Takeaways

  • Replace the exact gradient by a random mini-batch average: unbiased, with variance σ²/B.
  • Far from the optimum SGD makes good progress cheaply; near it a constant η leaves a noise floor.
  • Batch size trades noise against compute per step; the learning rate must be retuned with B.
  • Every large-scale model is trained this way.
6.4

Momentum, RMSProp & Adam

The questionOn a long narrow valley, plain gradient descent bounces between the walls and crawls along the floor. And in a neural network, some knobs (an embedding for a rare word) get tiny, occasional gradients while others (a bias) get big ones every step — one learning rate for all of them is wrong for most. Can the optimiser remember where it has been, and adapt the step of every knob separately?

Intuition 1: the heavy ball

A real ball rolling downhill does not follow the instantaneous slope: it has momentum. Slope changes its velocity a little at a time; the velocity then carries it. In a zig-zag valley the slope across the valley alternates sign (push left, push right) — those pushes cancel in the velocity. The slope along the valley floor always points the same way — those pushes add up, so the ball accelerates along the floor. Momentum automatically turns "noisy, back-and-forth" components down and "steady" components up.

Intuition 2: the exponential moving average

The tool behind everything in this lesson is the exponential moving average (EMA): a running average that forgets the past gradually, like a smoothed temperature reading. With a decay rate \beta between 0 and 1:

m_k=\beta\,m_{k-1}+(1-\beta)\,g_k

Unroll it: m_k=(1-\beta)\bigl(g_k+\beta g_{k-1}+\beta^2g_{k-2}+\cdots\bigr). The newest value has weight 1-\beta, the one before it (1-\beta)\beta, then (1-\beta)\beta^2, … a geometric fade-out. The weights add up to 1, and the "memory length" is about 1/(1-\beta) steps: \beta=0.9 averages roughly the last 10 values, \beta=0.99 the last 100.

Build it up

Momentum (heavy ball). Keep a velocity v that accumulates gradients with decay \beta and move by it:

v_k=\beta\,v_{k-1}+g_k,\qquad\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\,v_k

(This form omits the factor 1-\beta; it is the EMA times 1/(1-\beta). If the gradient were constant, v\to g/(1-\beta), so the steady-state step is \eta/(1-\beta) — ten times bigger for \beta=0.9.) Unrolled, v_k=g_k+\beta g_{k-1}+\beta^2g_{k-2}+\cdots: the step is a weighted sum of recent gradients. The lab below draws these weighted gradients as arrows laid end to end — their sum is the step.

Worked example — momentum versus plain GD

f(w)=\tfrac12w^2, so g=w. Start w_0=10, \eta=0.1, \beta=0.9, v_0=0.

k=1: v=0.9\cdot0+10=10, w=10-0.1\cdot10=9. k=2: v=0.9\cdot10+9=18, w=9-1.8=7.2. k=3: v=0.9\cdot18+7.2=23.4, w=7.2-2.34=4.86. k=4: v=25.92, w=2.268. k=5: v=25.596, w=-0.2916 (overshoot!).

Plain GD multiplies by 0.9 each step: 9, 8.1, 7.29, 6.561. After 4 steps momentum is at 2.27, GD at 6.56. The velocity keeps growing for a few steps (10, 18, 23.4, 25.9) — the ball accelerating — and then, like a real ball, shoots past the bottom and has to come back: momentum oscillates around the minimum while it settles.

RMSProp: a separate step size for every knob. Instead of averaging the gradient, average its square with an EMA, s_k=\beta_2s_{k-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_k^2. Then \sqrt{s_k} is the typical size (root-mean-square, RMS) of recent gradients for that knob. Divide the gradient by it:

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\,\frac{g_k}{\sqrt{s_k}+\varepsilon}

A knob with huge gradients gets a smaller effective step; a knob with tiny gradients gets a bigger one: every coordinate moves at roughly the same "typical" speed \approx\eta regardless of its gradient's scale. (\varepsilon\sim10^{-8} only prevents division by zero.) This is a cheap way to fight bad conditioning (Lesson 6.5), because it rescales each axis separately.

Worked example — RMSProp by hand

One knob, \theta_0=1, gradients g=4,\,2,\,-1, \beta_2=0.9, \eta=0.1, s_0=0.

k=1: s=0.9\cdot0+0.1\cdot16=1.6; update 0.1\cdot4/\sqrt{1.6}=0.3162; \theta=0.6838.

k=2: s=0.9\cdot1.6+0.1\cdot4=1.84; update 0.1\cdot2/\sqrt{1.84}=0.1474; \theta=0.5363.

k=3: s=0.9\cdot1.84+0.1\cdot1=1.756; update 0.1\cdot(-1)/\sqrt{1.756}=-0.0755; \theta=0.6118.

Notice step 1: the true RMS of the gradient so far is 4, which would give an update of 0.1, but s_1=1.6 — far too small because the EMA started from 0. The first update is inflated by 3×. That start-up bias is why Adam adds a correction.

Adam = momentum + RMSProp + bias correction. Adam keeps both averages — m for the gradient's direction, v for its squared size — and fixes the start-up bias. Because m_0=v_0=0, early values are shrunk towards 0. After k steps the weights of an EMA sum to 1-\beta^k rather than 1, so dividing by 1-\beta^k corrects it exactly:

\begin{aligned}m_k&=\beta_1m_{k-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_k,&\quad v_k&=\beta_2v_{k-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_k^2\\ \hat m_k&=\frac{m_k}{1-\beta_1^{\,k}},&\quad\hat v_k&=\frac{v_k}{1-\beta_2^{\,k}}\\ \theta_{k+1}&=\theta_k-\eta\,\frac{\hat m_k}{\sqrt{\hat v_k}+\varepsilon}\end{aligned}
Worked example — Adam by hand (3 steps)

Same data: \theta_0=1, g=4,2,-1, \eta=0.1, \beta_1=0.9, \beta_2=0.999, \varepsilon\approx0.

k=1. m=0.1\cdot4=0.4; v=0.001\cdot16=0.016. Corrections: 1-0.9=0.1, 1-0.999=0.001 → \hat m=4, \hat v=16. Update =0.1\cdot4/\sqrt{16}=0.1; \theta=0.9.

k=2. m=0.9\cdot0.4+0.1\cdot2=0.56; v=0.999\cdot0.016+0.001\cdot4=0.019984. Corrections: 1-0.81=0.19, 1-0.999^2=0.001999 → \hat m=2.9474, \hat v=9.997. Update =0.1\cdot2.9474/3.1618=0.09322; \theta=0.80678.

k=3. m=0.9\cdot0.56+0.1\cdot(-1)=0.404; v=0.999\cdot0.019984+0.001=0.020964. Corrections: 1-0.729=0.271, 1-0.999^3=0.002997 → \hat m=1.4908, \hat v=6.995. Update =0.1\cdot1.4908/2.6448=0.05637; \theta=0.75042.

Why bias correction matters. Without it, step 1 would be 0.1\cdot0.4/\sqrt{0.016}=0.3162 — about 3.16\,\eta (because 0.1/\sqrt{0.001}=3.16), a wild jump that depends on the arbitrary start. With it, the first update is \eta\,g/|g|=\eta exactly: the step size is controlled by η alone, whatever the gradient's scale. Step 2 and 3 show the momentum effect: the gradient dropped from 4 to 2 to −1, but the averaged \hat m falls more gently (4 → 2.95 → 1.49), remembering the past.

The formal definition

The three updates above are the whole story; their symbols:

β, β₁EMA decay for the gradient (momentum), typically 0.9
β₂EMA decay for the squared gradient, typically 0.999 (long memory) — LLM recipes often use 0.95
mₖ, vₖEMA of g and of g², element-wise (every parameter has its own)
m̂ₖ, v̂ₖbias-corrected versions: divide by 1 − βᵏ
√v̂ₖRMS size of recent gradients for that parameter (the "scale")
εtiny constant (≈10⁻⁸) preventing division by zero
ηbase learning rate; a parameter moves by ≈ η per step when its gradient is steady

Common traps
  • Two conventions for momentum. Frameworks use v\leftarrow\beta v+g (PyTorch SGD) or the EMA form with (1-\beta)g; with the second, the learning rate is scaled by 1-\beta relative to the first. Check which one you are reading.
  • v is not variance. It is the (uncentred) average of g^2, the typical gradient size, even when the gradient has a consistent mean.
  • Adam is not tuning-free. The learning rate still matters a lot, and a bad \varepsilon or \beta_2 can destabilise training.
  • L2 penalty ≠ weight decay in Adam. Adding \lambda\theta to the gradient lets Adam rescale it with everything else; AdamW applies decay directly to the weights (Lesson 6.6).
  • Memory. Adam stores two extra numbers per parameter (m and v): the optimiser state costs 2× the model size.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Adam/AdamW is the default optimiser for transformers and LLMs (typical β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95–0.999, ε = 10⁻⁸). Its per-parameter scaling is why embeddings of rare tokens still learn.
  • SGD with momentum remains common in computer vision (ResNets), where it often generalises well with careful tuning.
  • Relatives: Adafactor and 8-bit Adam reduce optimiser memory, Lion uses the sign of a momentum average, Nesterov momentum evaluates the gradient at a "look-ahead" position.
  • EMAs everywhere: EMA of model weights for evaluation, BatchNorm running statistics, and the target networks in reinforcement learning are all the same \beta m+(1-\beta)x recipe.

Check yourself

1. Roughly how many past gradients does an EMA with β = 0.99 average over? What is the steady-state velocity of heavy-ball momentum (v ← βv + g) with β = 0.9 and constant gradient g?
Memory ≈ 1/(1−β) = 100 steps. With constant g the fixed point satisfies v = 0.9v + g, so v = g/(1−0.9) = 10g: steps are 10× longer than plain GD.
2. Why is Adam's first update ≈ η·sign(g) after bias correction, and what would it be without correction (β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999)?
m₁ = 0.1g, v₁ = 0.001g², and the corrections divide by 0.1 and 0.001, so m̂ = g, v̂ = g² and the update is ηg/|g| = ±η. Without correction: 0.1g/√(0.001g²) = ±0.1/0.0316 = ±3.16η, about three times too big.
3. Two parameters have gradients of typical size 100 and 0.01. What are their RMSProp/Adam steps relative to plain SGD?
Plain SGD steps are η·100 and η·0.01 — 10⁴ apart. With RMS scaling both are ≈ η·g/RMS(g) ≈ ±η: equal. The huge-gradient parameter gets a much smaller step than SGD would give; the tiny-gradient parameter a much larger one.

Takeaways

  • EMA = geometric forgetting; memory length ≈ 1/(1−β).
  • Momentum is a heavy ball: velocity = EMA of gradients, cancelling zig-zag and accelerating consistent directions (steady step ≈ η/(1−β)).
  • RMSProp divides by the RMS of recent gradients: per-parameter step sizes.
  • Adam = momentum + RMSProp + bias correction (divide by 1 − βᵏ); the default for training LLMs.
6.5

Learning-rate schedules & conditioning

The questionEvery recipe for training a large model says "learning rate 3·10⁻⁴, warm up for 2,000 steps, then decay with a cosine curve". Why not simply pick one good value and keep it? And why does a landscape that is "badly conditioned" make training so much harder, no matter which rate we choose?

Intuition: a hike in the fog

Early in the hike you are far from the goal and the path is steep and clear: long strides save time. But right at the start, in unfamiliar territory, the ground may curve sharply and your estimates (the optimiser's running averages, the random weights) are unreliable — a long stride could throw you off a ledge, so you begin with small careful steps (warm-up). Near the end you need to land on one precise spot; with the compass shaking (SGD noise) long strides keep you jittering around it, so you shorten the stride gradually (decay). Fast in the middle, careful at both ends: that is the shape of nearly every good schedule.

Build it up

Why decay. From Lesson 6.3 the SGD noise floor grows with \eta. A large rate gets you to the right valley quickly, but cannot settle in it; shrinking \eta reduces the jitter and lets the loss fall further. Similarly, on a quadratic, a rate near 2/L is the fastest at first but oscillates at the end.

Why warm-up. Three reasons usually combine. (1) At random initialisation the loss surface can be very sharp (large curvature L), so the stability limit 2/L is small at first and grows as training "flattens" the landscape; the toy lab models that. (2) Adam's second-moment estimate v is based on only a few gradients at the start and is unreliable. (3) Early gradients are large and nearly random; big steps can wreck the weights irreversibly. A short warm-up with \eta rising linearly from ≈0 to its peak avoids all three.

The common schedules (t = step number, T = total steps, \eta_{\max} = peak rate, \eta_{\min} = final rate):

  • Constant: \eta_t=\eta_{\max}. Simple; leaves a noise floor.
  • Step decay: multiply by \gamma (say 0.1) every fixed number of steps/epochs: \eta_t=\eta_{\max}\gamma^{\lfloor t/s\rfloor}. Classic in image models.
  • Exponential decay: \eta_t=\eta_{\max}\,\rho^{\,t/T} smooth version.
  • Cosine: \eta_t=\eta_{\min}+\tfrac12(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\bigl(1+\cos\tfrac{\pi t}{T}\bigr): starts flat, falls fastest in the middle, ends flat. No extra hyper-parameter besides T.
  • Linear warm-up: \eta_t=\eta_{\max}\,\tfrac{t}{W} for t<W, then any decay above (so "warm-up + cosine").
Worked example — cosine and warm-up

Cosine, \eta_{\max}=10^{-3}, \eta_{\min}=0, T=100. Using \cos0=1, \cos\tfrac\pi4=0.7071, \cos\tfrac\pi2=0, \cos\tfrac{3\pi}4=-0.7071, \cos\pi=-1:

t=0: \tfrac12\cdot10^{-3}\cdot2=10^{-3}. t=25: \tfrac12\cdot10^{-3}(1.7071)=8.54\cdot10^{-4}. t=50: 5\cdot10^{-4}. t=75: \tfrac12\cdot10^{-3}(0.2929)=1.46\cdot10^{-4}. t=100: 0.

Notice that after a quarter of the training the rate has fallen only 15%, while half of the total decrease happens in the middle half.

Linear warm-up, W=2000, \eta_{\max}=3\cdot10^{-4}: at step 500, \eta=\tfrac{500}{2000}\cdot3\cdot10^{-4}=7.5\cdot10^{-5}; at step 2000 the full 3\cdot10^{-4} and the cosine decay takes over.

Conditioning: why the landscape's shape matters

Lesson 6.2 showed that the rate of gradient descent on a quadratic is governed by the condition number \kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}. No schedule fixes a bad \kappa: the stability limit 2/\lambda_{\max} forces small steps, which then crawl in the flat directions. The better cure is to change the problem so that \kappa is small.

Worked example — standardising a feature

Linear regression with two inputs: feature 1 has variance 1, feature 2 has variance 100 (e.g. a size in metres and a price in thousands). The loss Hessian is proportional to the feature covariance, so (up to a constant) A=\operatorname{diag}(1,100), \kappa=100. Best rate \rho=99/101=0.980: shrinking the error 1000× takes \ln(10^{-3})/\ln(0.980)\approx346 steps.

Divide feature 2 by 10 (standardise): A=\operatorname{diag}(1,1), \kappa=1, \rho=0: one step. A one-line preprocessing change replaced 346 steps by 1.

Deep networks have the same problem inside: activations in different layers can have wildly different scales. Remedies that act on conditioning:

  • Input standardisation (zero mean, unit variance per feature).
  • Normalisation layers (BatchNorm, LayerNorm, RMSNorm) re-centre and rescale activations at every layer, keeping each layer's inputs well scaled so the Hessian's eigenvalues stay in a reasonable range.
  • Good initialisation (Module 7) keeps early activations and gradients at a sane scale.
  • Adaptive optimisers (Adam) divide each coordinate by its gradient scale — a diagonal rescaling that acts like a cheap preconditioner. (True second-order methods multiply by H^{-1}, which makes the problem perfectly round but costs far too much for large models.)
  • Gradient clipping caps the gradient norm to survive occasional huge gradients — a safety net, not a conditioning fix.

The formal definition

\eta_t=\begin{cases}\eta_{\max}\dfrac{t}{W}&t<W\\[6pt]\eta_{\min}+\dfrac12(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\Bigl(1+\cos\dfrac{\pi(t-W)}{T-W}\Bigr)&t\ge W\end{cases}
tcurrent optimisation step (0, 1, 2, …)
Ttotal number of steps planned
Wnumber of warm-up steps
η_maxpeak learning rate, reached at the end of warm-up
η_minfinal learning rate; often 0 or 10% of the peak
γ, sstep-decay factor and step length (in step decay)
κcondition number of the (local) Hessian

Common traps
  • The schedule is tied to T. A cosine over 100k steps stopped at step 50k leaves a still-large rate; changing the training length means re-planning the schedule.
  • Warm-up too short or absent with large batches and Adam is a classic cause of early loss spikes or divergence.
  • Decaying to exactly 0 is not required; many recipes end at 10% of the peak.
  • Do not confuse learning-rate decay (shrinking the step) with weight decay (shrinking the weights, Lesson 6.6).
  • Chasing a schedule to repair bad conditioning. If κ is huge, standardise or normalise first.
Where it shows up in AI
  • LLM pretraining recipes. Typical: AdamW, peak \eta\approx3\cdot10^{-4} (smaller for bigger models), linear warm-up over ~1–2k steps, cosine decay to ~10% of the peak (the LLaMA-style recipe). Newer "warm-up–stable–decay" recipes hold the rate constant and decay only at the end, so training can be extended without a rewrite.
  • Fine-tuning uses much smaller rates (10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁴) with short warm-up and linear decay.
  • Vision. The classic ResNet recipe: SGD with momentum, rate 0.1, divided by 10 at fixed epochs (step decay).
  • Normalisation layers exist largely because they improve conditioning: they are why transformers and ResNets tolerate large rates.

Check yourself

1. Compute the cosine learning rate at t = 75 for η_max = 10⁻³, η_min = 0, T = 100.
η = ½·10⁻³·(1 + cos(3π/4)) = ½·10⁻³·(1 − 0.7071) = 1.46·10⁻⁴.

3. Why does standardising features reduce the number of gradient-descent steps, and by how much for variances 1 and 100?
It makes the Hessian eigenvalues equal, so κ drops from 100 to 1. The best-case shrink factor per step goes from (κ−1)/(κ+1) = 99/101 ≈ 0.98 to 0, cutting ≈ 346 steps (to shrink the error 1000×) down to ~1.

Takeaways

  • Warm-up protects the unstable start (sharp landscape, unreliable Adam statistics); decay removes the SGD noise floor at the end.
  • Cosine, step and exponential decays are all "big early, small late"; the peak rate and length T matter more than the exact curve.
  • The condition number κ of the landscape sets the speed limit; fix it by standardising, normalising and sensible initialisation.
  • Adaptive optimisers help by rescaling each coordinate, but good conditioning is free speed.
6.6

Constraints, Lagrange multipliers & regularization

The questionFind the point with the smallest x^2+y^2 — but only among points that satisfy x+y=2. The unconstrained minimum (0, 0) is forbidden. And in machine learning: we want the lowest loss subject to the weights being small, because small weights generalise better. How do constraints change the answer, and why does one kind of "small" (the L1 norm) produce weights that are exactly zero while another (L2) does not?

Intuition: a hiker on a fenced path

A hiker wants the lowest altitude but must stay on a fenced trail. Free to roam, they would walk straight downhill. On the trail, the ground may still slope — but only the slope along the trail matters, because the fence cancels any pull across it. Where the hiker finally stops, the ground does not slope along the trail: the only remaining downhill pull points straight at the fence, perpendicular to the trail. In mathematics: at a constrained optimum, the gradient of the loss is parallel to the normal of the constraint.

Equivalent picture with contour lines (curves of equal loss): start at the lowest loss and grow a contour outward. The first moment the contour touches the allowed region is the constrained optimum, and there the contour and the boundary are tangent — they have the same normal direction. The lab at the bottom of this lesson animates exactly this.

Build it up

Equality constraint. Minimise f(x) subject to g(x)=0. The normal to the curve g=0 is \nabla g (the gradient of a function points perpendicular to its own level curves). Parallel gradients mean one is a multiple of the other:

\nabla f(x^\star)=\lambda\,\nabla g(x^\star),\qquad g(x^\star)=0

The number \lambda is the Lagrange multiplier. The trick to remember: define the Lagrangian \mathcal L(x,\lambda)=f(x)-\lambda g(x). Setting all its partial derivatives to zero reproduces both equations at once (\partial_x\mathcal L=0 is the parallel condition, \partial_\lambda\mathcal L=0 is the constraint). We have turned a constrained problem into solving equations.

Worked example — the question

Minimise f=x^2+y^2 subject to g=x+y-2=0. \nabla f=(2x,2y), \nabla g=(1,1). Parallel condition: 2x=\lambda,\;2y=\lambda → x=y. Constraint: x+y=2 → x=y=1, \lambda=2, f=2.

Check: at (1,1) the gradient (2,2) is a multiple of the normal (1,1) ✓. Compare another feasible point, (2,0): f=4>2 ✓. Meaning of \lambda: if the constraint were x+y=c the best value would be c^2/2, whose rate of change at c=2 is c=2=\lambda. The multiplier is the price of the constraint: how much the optimum improves per unit of relaxation.

Inequality constraints g(x)\le0 (such as "weights inside a ball") split into two cases. Either the unconstrained minimum already satisfies the constraint: the fence is irrelevant and \lambda=0; or the optimum lies on the boundary, the fence is "active", and the parallel condition holds with a sign: the loss's downhill direction -\nabla f must point out through the fence (otherwise we could move inside and do better), so \lambda\ge0. These are the KKT conditions: \nabla f+\lambda\nabla g=0,\;\lambda\ge0,\;g\le0,\;\lambda g=0 (the last, "complementary slackness", says either the constraint is active or its multiplier is 0).

Regularization is a constraint in disguise

Regularisation means discouraging complicated models. The standard way is to add a penalty to the loss: minimise L(w)+\lambda\,R(w) with R(w)=\|w\|_2^2 (L2, also "ridge" or "weight decay") or R(w)=\|w\|_1=\sum|w_i| (L1, "lasso"). By the Lagrangian view, this is the same as minimising L(w) subject to R(w)\le r for some budget r that depends on \lambda: a stronger penalty = a smaller ball. The allowed region is a ball: a circle for L2 (w_1^2+w_2^2\le r^2) and a diamond for L1 (|w_1|+|w_2|\le r).

Why L1 gives sparsity and L2 does not. Look at the shapes. A circle is smooth everywhere: a contour approaches it and touches at a generic point where both coordinates are non-zero. The diamond has corners on the axes, where one coordinate is exactly zero. Corners stick out the furthest, so growing contours very often hit a corner first — and at a corner there is not one normal but a whole fan of normals, so many different loss directions are all "balanced" by the same point. The result: the L1-constrained solution lies on an axis, i.e. some weights are exactly zero (sparsity = automatic feature selection). In higher dimensions the L1 ball has even more corners and edges lying in coordinate subspaces, so more weights become zero.

Worked example — circle versus diamond

Loss f=(w_1-3)^2+(w_2-0.5)^2 (contours are circles around (3, 0.5)), budget r=2. The unconstrained minimum (3, 0.5) is outside both balls, so the constraint is active.

L2 ball w_1^2+w_2^2\le4: by symmetry the solution is the point of the circle closest to (3, 0.5), along the line from the origin: w^\star=2\cdot\frac{(3,0.5)}{\sqrt{9.25}}=(1.973,\,0.329). Both weights non-zero, loss 1.084.

L1 ball |w_1|+|w_2|\le2: the corner (2, 0) has loss 1+0.25=1.25. Is it optimal? There -\nabla f=2(3-2,\;0.5-0)=(2,1). The normal cone at the corner is spanned by the two face normals (1, 1) and (1, −1); and (2,1)=1.5\,(1,1)+0.5\,(1,-1) with both coefficients \ge0 ✓. So (2, 0) satisfies the KKT condition: w_2=0 exactly (a brute-force search along the diamond's boundary agrees). The weight w_2 was killed.

In one dimension the effect is a clean formula. Minimise (w-a)^2+\lambda R(w): for L2, w^\star=\dfrac{a}{1+\lambda} (always shrinks, never reaches 0); for L1, w^\star=\operatorname{sign}(a)\max\bigl(|a|-\tfrac\lambda2,\,0\bigr) ("soft thresholding": it shrinks by a fixed amount and snaps to exactly 0 when |a|\le\lambda/2). With a=1.5 and \lambda=3: L2 gives 1.5/4=0.375; L1 gives \max(1.5-1.5,0)=0.

Weight decay in gradient descent. With the L2 penalty \tfrac\lambda2\|w\|^2, the gradient gains a term \lambda w and one step is w\leftarrow w-\eta(\nabla L+\lambda w)=(1-\eta\lambda)\,w-\eta\nabla L: every step first shrinks the weights by the factor 1-\eta\lambda ("decay"), then moves along the loss gradient. For w=2, \eta\lambda=0.01 the shrink alone gives 1.98.

The formal definition

\mathcal L(x,\lambda)=f(x)-\lambda\,g(x),\qquad\nabla_x\mathcal L=0,\quad g(x)=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\min_x\ f(x)\ \text{ s.t. }\ g(x)=0
\min_w\ L(w)+\lambda\|w\|_p^p\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\min_w\ L(w)\ \text{ s.t. }\ \|w\|_p\le r\qquad(p=1:\ \text{diamond},\ p=2:\ \text{circle})

Common traps
  • Lagrange gives candidates, not proofs. Solutions of \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g can be maxima or saddles of the constrained problem; check the values, or use convexity.
  • The multiplier's sign depends on your convention (f-\lambda g versus f+\lambda g); the geometry (parallel gradients) is convention-free.
  • Penalty strength and radius are inversely related: raising \lambda shrinks the ball. They are two parametrisations of the same trade-off.
  • L2 does not give exact zeros; it makes weights small but dense. Only L1 (and similar corner-shaped constraints) yield sparsity.
  • Penalising the bias and the normalisation scales is usually not intended; weight decay is normally applied to weight matrices only.
  • L2-in-the-loss ≠ weight decay for adaptive optimisers like Adam; AdamW applies the decay (1-\eta\lambda)w outside the adaptive rescaling.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Weight decay (typically 0.01–0.1 in AdamW) is the L2 constraint used in nearly all neural-network training; L1 penalties are used for sparse linear models (Lasso, feature selection) and for pruning-friendly networks.
  • Support vector machines. The hard-margin SVM minimises \tfrac12\|w\|^2 subject to y_i(w\cdot x_i+b)\ge1 for every training point. Each constraint gets a multiplier \alpha_i\ge0; by complementary slackness only the active constraints (points exactly on the margin) have \alpha_i>0 — these are the support vectors — and w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i.
  • Trust regions. TRPO maximises reward subject to a KL-divergence budget between the old and new policy; PPO and RLHF's KL penalty are the Lagrangian relaxation of that constraint (the multiplier is the KL coefficient).
  • Projected gradient descent (take a step, then project back onto the allowed set) is used for adversarial examples inside an \ell_\infty ball, for weight-norm constraints and for probability simplices.

Check yourself

1. Minimise f = x + y subject to x² + y² = 2. Find the optimum and λ (convention ∇f = λ∇g, g = x² + y² − 2).
∇f = (1, 1) = λ(2x, 2y) → x = y = 1/(2λ). Constraint: 2x² = 2 → x = ±1. So (1, 1) with λ = 1/2 gives f = 2 (the maximum on the circle) and (−1, −1) with λ = −1/2 gives f = −2, the minimum. Both satisfy the parallel condition; comparing values decides.
2. One weight, unregularised optimum a = 2, penalty strength λ = 1 in (w − a)² + λR(w). Find the L2 and L1 solutions.
L2: a/(1+λ) = 2/2 = 1. L1: sign(a)·max(|a| − λ/2, 0) = 2 − 0.5 = 1.5. Neither is 0 here because |a| = 2 > λ/2 = 0.5; with a = 0.4 the L1 answer would be exactly 0 while L2 would give 0.2.
3. Why does the L1 diamond produce sparse solutions but the L2 circle does not?
The diamond has corners on the coordinate axes (where one weight is 0) that stick out furthest and have a wide fan of normals, so a growing loss contour often touches a corner first. The circle has no corners: a contour typically touches it at a generic point where both coordinates are non-zero.

Takeaways

  • At a constrained optimum ∇f is parallel to the constraint normal: ∇f = λ∇g; the Lagrangian f − λg encodes it and λ is the price of the constraint.
  • Inequality constraints add λ ≥ 0 and complementary slackness (active or λ = 0).
  • Penalty regularisation ⇔ a norm-ball constraint; stronger penalty = smaller ball. L2 = circle (small, dense weights), L1 = diamond (exact zeros, sparsity).
  • Weight decay shrinks weights by (1 − ηλ) each step; SVMs, TRPO/RLHF and adversarial robustness are all constrained problems.
