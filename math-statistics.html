Module 4 · Statistics

Probability asks: given the world, what data will I see? Statistics turns it around: given the data, what can I say about the world? That reversal is the whole of machine learning. By the end you will know why training a network means maximising a likelihood, why weight decay is a belief about the weights, why a model can be too flexible, and how to report a result without fooling yourself.

4.1

Estimation & maximum likelihood

The questionA coin is tossed 10 times and lands heads 7 times. What is its true probability of heads? You will never know for certain — but one number is a more reasonable answer than the others. Which, and what does "reasonable" mean precisely? The same question, scaled up, is "which weights should my network have, given this training set?"

Intuition

In Module 3 the coin was known: "heads has probability 0.5", and you asked what data it would produce. Real life is the reverse. The data are in your hands; the coin's bias is hidden. We need a way to run probability backwards.

Two words to separate before anything else:

  • A parameter is a fixed but unknown property of the world — the coin's true bias p, the true average height \mu of a population, the ideal weights \theta of a model. It does not change from sample to sample.
  • An estimator is a recipe that turns data into a guess of the parameter, and the number it returns is an estimate, written with a hat: \hat p ("p-hat"). A different sample gives a different estimate, so the estimate is itself random while the parameter is not. Keep that sentence in mind; all of the lesson 4.4 grows out of it.

Now the key idea. Suppose I propose a value for the bias, say p=0.9, and ask: "if the coin really were like that, how probable was it that I would see exactly the data I saw?" For 7 heads out of 10 that probability is low. For p=0.7 it is much higher. For p=0.1 it is nearly hopeless. We prefer the story that makes our observation least surprising. That preference, made precise, is maximum likelihood. A detective does the same: among all suspects, favour the one under whom the evidence is most probable.

Build it up: the likelihood of a coin

Say the flips are independent and each lands heads with probability p. The probability of one particular sequence is the product of the probabilities of each flip: p for every head, 1-p for every tail. With h heads and t tails (total n=h+t):

L(p)=p^{h}(1-p)^{t}

This is the likelihood function. Read it carefully: the data (h,t) are now fixed numbers and the thing that varies is the parameter p. The formula is the same as a probability, but we look at it as a function of the hypothesis, not of the data. It is not a probability distribution over p (it need not add up to 1); it only ranks candidates.

Worked example — 7 heads, 3 tails

L(p)=p^7(1-p)^3. Try three candidates:

p=0.5:\ 0.5^{10}=0.000977

p=0.7:\ 0.7^7\cdot0.3^3=0.0823543\times0.027=0.002224

p=0.9:\ 0.9^7\cdot0.1^3=0.4782969\times0.001=0.000478

So p=0.7 makes our data 2.3× more probable than a fair coin and about 4.6× more probable than p=0.9. Is 0.7 the very best? Calculus will tell us.

Switch to logarithms. A product of many small numbers underflows on a computer (a product of 1000 probabilities of 0.5 is about 10^{-301}) and is awkward to differentiate. The logarithm is increasing, so the p that maximises L also maximises \ln L, and it turns products into sums (Module 0):

\ell(p)=\ln L(p)=h\ln p+t\ln(1-p)

Now differentiate and set to zero, as in any maximisation problem (Module 2):

\ell'(p)=\frac{h}{p}-\frac{t}{1-p}=0\;\Longrightarrow\;h(1-p)=t\,p\;\Longrightarrow\;h=(h+t)\,p\;\Longrightarrow\;\hat p=\frac{h}{n}

The second derivative \ell''(p)=-h/p^2-t/(1-p)^2 is negative everywhere on (0,1), so this stationary point is a maximum, not a minimum. For our coin, \hat p=7/10. The maximum-likelihood answer to "what fraction of heads?" is simply the observed fraction — a reassuring sanity check, and a typical result: MLE often gives the answer you would have guessed, now with a reason.

Build it up: a Gaussian mean, and where least squares comes from

Suppose data x_1,\dots,x_n are independent draws from a Gaussian with unknown mean \mu and known standard deviation \sigma. Each point has density \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}e^{-(x_i-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}. Independence means the likelihood is the product, and the log-likelihood the sum:

\ell(\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]=-\frac n2\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu)^2

Look at how \mu enters: only through the sum of squared errors \sum(x_i-\mu)^2, preceded by a minus sign and a positive constant. The first term does not depend on \mu at all. So maximising the log-likelihood is exactly the same problem as minimising the sum of squares. Differentiate: \ell'(\mu)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum(x_i-\mu)=0\Rightarrow\hat\mu=\frac1n\sum x_i=\bar x. The sample mean is the maximum-likelihood estimate.

Worked example

Data 2, 4, 9. The MLE of the mean is \bar x=15/3=5. Squared distances at \mu=5: 9 + 1 + 16 = 26. Try \mu=6: 16 + 4 + 9 = 29 — larger, so a worse fit (lower likelihood). Try \mu=4: 4 + 0 + 25 = 29, equally worse on the other side. The minimum is at 5.

The same trick for \sigma^2: differentiating gives \hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}}=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=26/3\approx8.67. Note it divides by n. The familiar "divide by n-1" version (13 here) is the unbiased variant — a correction we will understand properly in 4.3.

Now the big step to machine learning. A regression model predicts \hat y_i=f(x_i;\theta). Assume the truth is the prediction plus Gaussian noise: y_i=f(x_i;\theta)+\varepsilon_i with \varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2). That says y_i is Gaussian with mean f(x_i;\theta) — exactly the situation above with \mu replaced by the model's output. Therefore

\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}=\arg\max_\theta\;-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i\big(y_i-f(x_i;\theta)\big)^2=\arg\min_\theta\;\frac1n\sum_i\big(y_i-f(x_i;\theta)\big)^2

Mean-squared-error training is maximum-likelihood estimation under Gaussian noise. The squared error was not an arbitrary choice: it is what "the noise is Gaussian" looks like after taking -\ln. For one-parameter linear regression \hat y=wx, minimising \sum(y_i-wx_i)^2 gives \hat w=\sum x_iy_i/\sum x_i^2.

Build it up: classification and cross-entropy

For a binary label y_i\in\{0,1\} a model outputs p_i=P(y_i=1\mid x_i;\theta) (e.g. a sigmoid output). Each example is a Bernoulli trial with its own p_i: the probability of what happened is p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i} — p_i if y_i=1, 1-p_i if y_i=0. Multiply over independent examples, take the log, flip the sign (we like to minimise losses):

-\ell(\theta)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[y_i\ln p_i+(1-y_i)\ln(1-p_i)\Big]

This is binary cross-entropy, the standard classification loss — now seen to be the negative log-likelihood of Bernoulli data. With K classes, the model outputs a probability vector (softmax) and the probability of the observed class c_i is p_{i,c_i}, so the loss is -\sum_i\ln p_{i,c_i}: categorical cross-entropy. Language models minimise exactly this, with the "class" being the next token.

Worked example

Three examples with labels 1, 0, 1 and predicted probabilities of class 1 equal to 0.9, 0.2, 0.7. The probability the model assigned to what actually happened: 0.9, 0.8 (= 1 − 0.2), 0.7. Likelihood = 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.7 = 0.504. Negative log-likelihood = −(ln 0.9 + ln 0.8 + ln 0.7) = 0.1054 + 0.2231 + 0.3567 = 0.6852; the average per example is 0.2284. A perfectly confident correct model would give 0; a confident wrong prediction (probability 0.01 for the true class) costs −ln 0.01 = 4.6 on its own.

The formal definition

L(\theta)=p(x_1,\dots,x_n;\theta)\overset{\text{iid}}{=}\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(x_i;\theta),\qquad \ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln p(x_i;\theta),\qquad \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}=\arg\max_\theta\,\ell(\theta)
θthe unknown parameter(s) (p, \mu, network weights…)
x₁ … xₙthe observed data — fixed numbers once collected
p(xᵢ; θ)probability (or density) the model with parameters θ gives to observation i; the semicolon says "θ is a knob, not a random variable"
iid"independent and identically distributed": the factorisation into a product needs independence, and a single p for every i needs identical distribution
L(θ), ℓ(θ)likelihood and log-likelihood, as functions of θ
arg maxthe input θ that makes the function largest (Module 0)
θ̂an estimate (hat = computed from data)

Under mild conditions the MLE is consistent (it converges to the true parameter as n\to\infty) and is about as precise as any estimator can be. Its weakness is also visible: with 3 heads out of 3 flips it states \hat p=1 and is certain the coin can never land tails. Lesson 4.2 repairs this.

Common traps
  • The likelihood is not the probability of the parameter. L(0.7)=0.0022 does not mean "there is a 0.2% chance that p=0.7". The likelihood ranks hypotheses; turning it into a probability for the parameter needs a prior (lesson 4.2).
  • Maximising a product versus a sum. Taking the log changes the value but not the location of the maximum. Never compare raw log-likelihoods from different datasets as if they were probabilities.
  • MLE can overfit. It trusts the data completely: 3 heads in 3 flips gives \hat p=1. With a very flexible model it can even memorise the training set.
  • "MSE is a Gaussian assumption." It is: if your noise is heavy-tailed or the target is a probability, MSE is the wrong likelihood. Different noise model, different loss.
  • The MLE of variance divides by n and is biased low; software usually reports the n-1 version.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Almost every loss function is a negative log-likelihood. MSE ↔ Gaussian noise; binary/categorical cross-entropy ↔ Bernoulli/categorical outputs; Poisson regression for counts ↔ Poisson likelihood. "Choose a loss" really means "choose a noise model".
  • Language models are trained by maximising \sum_i\ln p(\text{token}_i\mid\text{previous tokens};\theta) over trillions of tokens. Perplexity is just e^{\text{average NLL}}.
  • Training = MLE by gradient descent. For a neural network no formula exists for \hat\theta, so we climb the log-likelihood (descend the NLL) step by step (Module 6).
  • Logistic regression, Naive Bayes, Gaussian mixtures (EM) are fitted by MLE or its variants.

Check yourself

1. A coin gives 3 heads and 9 tails. Write its log-likelihood and find the MLE of p.
ℓ(p) = 3 ln p + 9 ln(1−p). Setting ℓ′ = 3/p − 9/(1−p) = 0 gives 3(1−p) = 9p, so p̂ = 3/12 = 0.25 — the observed fraction of heads.
2. Labels 1, 1, 0 and predicted P(class 1) = 0.8, 0.5, 0.25. Compute the average binary cross-entropy.
Probabilities assigned to the true outcomes: 0.8, 0.5, 0.75. NLL = −(ln 0.8 + ln 0.5 + ln 0.75) = 0.2231 + 0.6931 + 0.2877 = 1.2039; average = 0.4013.
3. Why does maximising likelihood under Gaussian noise lead to minimising squared error rather than absolute error?
The Gaussian density contains the exponent −(y−f)²/(2σ²). The log removes the exponential and leaves a squared term; the remaining terms do not depend on θ. (Laplace noise, with exponent −|y−f|/b, would give absolute error instead.)

Takeaways

  • Parameters are fixed and unknown; estimates are random because data are.
  • The likelihood scores each candidate parameter by how probable it makes the observed data; MLE picks the best.
  • Use log-likelihood: products become sums, no underflow, easy derivatives.
  • Coin MLE = observed fraction; Gaussian mean MLE = sample mean.
  • MSE = Gaussian-noise MLE; cross-entropy = Bernoulli/categorical MLE. Losses are noise models.
4.2

Priors, MAP & regularization

The questionYou flip a fresh coin from your pocket three times and get three heads. Maximum likelihood says \hat p=1: "this coin can never land tails". You do not believe that — coins are almost always close to fair. How do we let that prior knowledge push back against a tiny amount of data? And how is this same idea hiding inside the "weight decay" knob of every deep-learning library?

Intuition

Before seeing any data you already know something. Coins are nearly fair; a house price is unlikely to be negative; a weight in a network is unlikely to be enormous. Call that knowledge a prior — a probability distribution over the parameter, drawn before looking at the data. Then data arrive and you update: the result is the posterior, your belief after seeing the evidence.

A helpful picture: the prior is a handful of imaginary flips you add to the real ones. If you believe "coins are fair", it is as if you had already seen, say, 5 heads and 5 tails before the experiment. Three real heads are then one voice against ten imaginary ones; the compromise lands near 0.5 but leans toward heads. With 3000 real flips the imaginary ones are drowned out. This "prior fades as data grows" behaviour is exactly right, and it falls out of one line of algebra.

Build it up: Bayes' rule for a parameter

From Module 3, Bayes' theorem reads P(A\mid B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)/P(B). Put the parameter \theta in the role of A and the data D in the role of B:

\underbrace{p(\theta\mid D)}_{\text{posterior}}=\frac{\overbrace{p(D\mid\theta)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{p(D)}\;\propto\;p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)

The denominator p(D) does not involve \theta; it just makes the area equal 1. For finding where the posterior is highest we can ignore it: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior ("∝" = "proportional to"). The MAP estimate ("maximum a posteriori") is the \theta where this product peaks:

\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_\theta\big[\ln p(D\mid\theta)+\ln p(\theta)\big]

It is the log-likelihood from 4.1 plus a log-prior. If the prior is flat (all values equally plausible), the second term is constant and MAP = MLE.

Worked: the coin with a Beta prior

A convenient prior for a probability p\in[0,1] is the Beta distribution, with density proportional to p^{a-1}(1-p)^{b-1}. For a=b=1 it is flat; for a=b=5 it is a hump around 0.5. Multiply by the likelihood p^h(1-p)^t:

p(p\mid D)\;\propto\;p^{h}(1-p)^{t}\cdot p^{a-1}(1-p)^{b-1}=p^{\,h+a-1}(1-p)^{\,t+b-1}

The posterior is another Beta, with the counts simply added: \text{Beta}(a+h,\;b+t). The prior parameters really are pseudo-counts. The peak, found exactly as in 4.1 (differentiate the log, set to zero):

\hat p_{\text{MAP}}=\frac{h+a-1}{n+a+b-2}
Worked example

The same 7 heads and 3 tails (n=10). MLE gave 0.7.

Prior Beta(2, 2) ("one imaginary head and one imaginary tail"): posterior Beta(9, 5), MAP = (7+2−1)/(10+4−2) = 8/12 = 0.667.

Prior Beta(5, 5) (eight imaginary flips, four of each): posterior Beta(12, 8), MAP = 11/18 = 0.611 — pulled harder toward 0.5.

And the opening example: 3 heads in 3 flips with Beta(2,2) → MAP = (3+1)/(3+2) = 0.8, not 1. The coin is no longer declared tails-proof.

Priors on weights are regularizers

Now apply the same idea to a model's weights \mathbf w. In 4.1 we showed that under Gaussian noise the log-likelihood is -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(y_i-f(x_i;\mathbf w))^2+\text{const}. Choose a prior and add its log:

Gaussian prior — "each weight is probably small, typically within \tau of zero": p(w_j)\propto e^{-w_j^2/(2\tau^2)}, so \ln p(\mathbf w)=-\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\sum_jw_j^2+\text{const}. The negative log-posterior is

\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-f(x_i;\mathbf w))^2+\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\sum_jw_j^2\;\xrightarrow{\ \times\,\sigma^2\ }\;\frac12\sum_i(y_i-f_i)^2+\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2^2,\qquad\lambda=\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}

This is least squares plus an L2 penalty — ridge regression, weight decay. A bigger \lambda means a smaller prior variance \tau^2: a stronger belief that weights are near zero, or noisier data (bigger \sigma^2) so that we trust data less.

Laplace prior — a sharply pointed distribution p(w_j)\propto e^{-|w_j|/b}: lots of probability exactly near 0 but long tails. The negative log gives \frac{1}{b}\sum|w_j|, so MAP becomes least squares plus an L1 penalty \lambda\sum|w_j| with \lambda=\sigma^2/b (the lasso).

Worked example — one weight, all the numbers

Fit \hat y=wx to x=(1,2,3), y=(1.2,\,1.9,\,3.3). Then S_{xy}=\sum x_iy_i=1.2+3.8+9.9=14.9 and S_{xx}=\sum x_i^2=14.

No penalty: minimise \tfrac12\sum(y_i-wx_i)^2; derivative -S_{xy}+wS_{xx}=0 gives w_0=S_{xy}/S_{xx}=1.0643.

L2 with \lambda=7: derivative -S_{xy}+wS_{xx}+\lambda w=0\Rightarrow w=\dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}+\lambda}=\dfrac{14.9}{21}=0.7095. With \lambda=14: 14.9/28 = 0.5321. The weight shrinks smoothly, never reaching 0.

L1 with \lambda=7: for w>0 the derivative is -S_{xy}+wS_{xx}+\lambda=0\Rightarrow w=(S_{xy}-\lambda)/S_{xx}=w_0-\lambda/S_{xx}=1.0643-0.5=0.5643. With \lambda=16 the step \lambda/S_{xx}=1.143 exceeds w_0, so the minimum is exactly w=0. This rule is called soft-thresholding: w^\star=\operatorname{sign}(w_0)\max(|w_0|-\lambda/S_{xx},\,0).

Why the difference? Near zero the L2 penalty's slope (\lambda w) vanishes, so it never pushes the last bit; L1's slope stays at \lambda, a constant pull that can reach zero and stop there. That is why L1 produces sparse models.

The formal definition

\hat{\mathbf w}_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\min_{\mathbf w}\;\underbrace{-\ln p(D\mid\mathbf w)}_{\text{data loss}}\;\underbrace{-\ln p(\mathbf w)}_{\text{penalty}}\qquad\begin{cases}p(\mathbf w)=\mathcal N(0,\tau^2I)&\Rightarrow\ \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2^2\\[2pt]p(\mathbf w)\propto e^{-\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_1/b}&\Rightarrow\ \lambda\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_1\end{cases}
p(w)the prior: belief about the weights before seeing data
p(D | w)the likelihood (4.1)
posteriorp(\mathbf w\mid D)\propto p(D\mid\mathbf w)\,p(\mathbf w)
τ², bprior width (Gaussian variance; Laplace scale): small = strong belief in zero
λpenalty strength; equals σ²/τ² (L2) or σ²/b (L1)
‖w‖₂², ‖w‖₁sum of squares / sum of absolute values of the weights
MAPmode of the posterior; "MLE with a prior"

As data grow, the likelihood term (a sum over n examples) grows while the prior term stays fixed, so MAP converges to MLE — the prior's influence fades like 1/n. Observe this in the first lab: MAP = (h + m·s)/(n + s) is a weighted average whose data weight is n/(n+s).

Common traps
  • MAP is not "the" Bayesian answer. It is the single most probable value. Full Bayesian inference keeps the whole posterior (its width = remaining uncertainty); MAP discards that.
  • The penalty is not free. Large \lambda underfits (all weights squashed to 0). Choose it with validation data (lesson 4.5), never by looking at the test set.
  • The prior cannot rescue you from a wrong model, and a very confident prior will override good data for a long time.
  • Regularize the weights, not (usually) the bias term, and put features on comparable scales first — a penalty on \sum w_j^2 treats all weights as equally "expensive", which is only fair if inputs are standardised.
  • In optimisers like Adam, "L2 penalty" and "weight decay" are no longer the same thing (decoupled AdamW), though both derive from the idea above.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Weight decay in nearly every training recipe (weight_decay=0.01 in AdamW) is a Gaussian prior on the weights.
  • Lasso / L1 feature selection: sparse linear models, and sparse activations or pruning in networks.
  • Label smoothing, dropout, early stopping also act as priors/regularizers — encoded beliefs that "smooth" solutions are more probable.
  • Bayesian neural networks, Gaussian processes keep the full posterior; LoRA/fine-tuning with KL penalties (RLHF) is "stay close to the prior model" in the same spirit.
  • Smoothing in language models and Naive Bayes ("add-one" / Laplace smoothing) is exactly the Beta(2,2)-style pseudo-count trick above.

Check yourself

1. 7 heads, 3 tails, prior Beta(3, 3). Compute the MAP estimate.
MAP = (h+a−1)/(n+a+b−2) = (7+2)/(10+4) = 9/14 ≈ 0.643 (equivalently: add 2 imaginary heads and 2 imaginary tails to the data).
2. With Sxx = 14, Sxy = 14.9 and an L2 penalty of λ = 28, what is the penalised weight?

3. Why does an L1 penalty give exactly-zero weights while L2 only gives small ones?
The derivative of λ|w| is ±λ no matter how small w is, a constant pull that can bring w all the way to 0; the derivative of ½λw² is λw, which fades as w→0 so the pull vanishes before reaching 0. Equivalently, the Laplace prior has a sharp peak at 0 while the Gaussian is flat there.

Takeaways

  • Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior; MAP maximises log-likelihood + log-prior.
  • A prior acts like imaginary data; its influence shrinks as real data grow.
  • Gaussian prior = L2 penalty (weight decay), λ = σ²/τ². Laplace prior = L1 penalty (sparsity).
  • Regularization trades a little bias for less variance — the subject of the next lesson.
4.3

Bias–variance & generalization

The questionYou fit a curve through 10 noisy points. A wiggly high-degree polynomial passes through every single one — training error exactly zero. Is it a great model? Almost certainly not: ask it about a new point and it will be wildly wrong. What exactly goes wrong, can we measure it, and how do we pick a model that is neither too simple nor too flexible?

Intuition

Imagine learning to throw darts. Two ways to be bad: your throws can land consistently off-centre (a systematic aiming mistake — bias) or scattered all over the board (inconsistency — variance). A beginner using a rigid technique has high bias and low variance; one using a hyper-sensitive technique that reacts to every tiny tremor has low bias but high variance.

A model behaves the same way. A too simple model (a straight line fitted to a sine wave) cannot represent the truth no matter how much data you give it: its average prediction is wrong — underfitting, high bias. A too flexible model has enough freedom to bend through every noisy point, so it also learns the noise; give it a different noisy sample and it draws a completely different curve — overfitting, high variance. We want the middle.

The crucial thing: the error on the data you trained on lies. Flexible models drive it toward zero. What matters is generalization — performance on new data from the same source, estimated on held-out test data.

Build it up: measuring it by repeating the experiment

Here is the thought experiment that defines bias and variance. Fix one input x_0, where the truth is f(x_0). Repeatedly: draw a brand-new training set, fit the same kind of model, record its prediction \hat f(x_0). You get a cloud of predictions (the lab below draws one ghost curve per dataset). Two numbers describe the cloud:

  • Bias = (average prediction) − (truth) = \mathbb E[\hat f(x_0)]-f(x_0). Where is the centre of the cloud relative to the target?
  • Variance = \mathbb E\big[(\hat f(x_0)-\mathbb E\hat f(x_0))^2\big]. How spread out is the cloud around its own centre?

A new observation is y=f(x_0)+\varepsilon with noise \mathbb E\varepsilon=0, \operatorname{Var}\varepsilon=\sigma^2, independent of the training set. The expected squared error of the prediction decomposes:

Derivation. Write \bar f=\mathbb E[\hat f(x_0)] for the centre of the cloud. Add and subtract it:

y-\hat f=\underbrace{(f-\bar f)}_{-\text{bias}}+\underbrace{(\bar f-\hat f)}_{\text{random}}+\underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}}

Square it. The three squares give bias², variance, noise. The cross terms vanish when we take expectations: \mathbb E[\bar f-\hat f]=0 (the cloud's centre minus itself), and \varepsilon has mean zero and is independent of the rest. (E.g. \mathbb E[(f-\bar f)(\bar f-\hat f)]=(f-\bar f)\cdot\mathbb E[\bar f-\hat f]=0, because f-\bar f is a constant.) What remains:

\mathbb E\big[(y-\hat f(x_0))^2\big]=\underbrace{\big(\mathbb E\hat f(x_0)-f(x_0)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\big(\hat f(x_0)\big)}_{\text{variance}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible noise}}
Worked example — all the numbers

At x_0 the truth is f=1, the noise has \sigma=0.5. Fit the model on four different training sets; it predicts 0.6, 0.9, 0.3, 0.6.

Centre of the cloud: \bar f=(0.6+0.9+0.3+0.6)/4=0.6. Bias = 0.6 − 1 = −0.4, so bias² = 0.16.

Variance: deviations from 0.6 are 0, 0.3, −0.3, 0 → (0+0.09+0.09+0)/4= 0.045.

Noise: \sigma^2=0.25. Total expected squared error = 0.16 + 0.045 + 0.25 = 0.455.

Check directly: squared distances from the truth 1 are 0.16, 0.01, 0.49, 0.16, average 0.205 = bias² + variance ✓, and adding the noise 0.25 gives 0.455 ✓. Nothing can reduce the 0.25; the other 0.205 is what model choice controls.

Build it up: the U-shaped test curve

Plot error against model flexibility (say, polynomial degree):

  • Training error falls steadily as flexibility rises, because the model can bend to fit more of the sample, including its noise. Training error is optimistic: it is measured on the very points the parameters were tuned to.
  • Bias² falls with flexibility (the average fit gets closer to the truth); variance rises (the fit leans on the quirks of each sample).
  • Test error = bias² + variance + σ², so it falls at first, then rises: a U shape. The best model sits near the bottom of the U. Left of it: underfitting. Right of it: overfitting.

More data pushes variance down (quirks average out) and lets you afford a more flexible model; regularization (4.2) reduces variance at the cost of some bias; averaging many models (ensembles, bagging, random forests) reduces variance without raising bias.

An intro to double descent. The U shape is not the end of the story. For modern huge models — networks with more parameters than training examples — test error often falls, rises to a peak around the point where the model can just barely interpolate (exactly fit) the training set, and then falls again as size grows further. This double-descent curve does not contradict the decomposition; it says that among the many interpolating solutions large models, and the way gradient descent picks one, behave like strongly regularized fits. Classical intuition (the U) still governs most small-model work, and the practical rule stays: judge a model on data it has not seen.

The formal definition

\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[(y-\hat f(x))^2\big]}_{\text{expected test error at }x}=\operatorname{Bias}[\hat f(x)]^2+\operatorname{Var}[\hat f(x)]+\sigma^2,\qquad\operatorname{Bias}[\hat f(x)]=\mathbb E[\hat f(x)]-f(x)
f(x)the true (unknown) function; y = f(x) + ε
f̂(x)the fitted model; it is random because it depends on the random training set
𝔼[·]expectation over everything random: training sets and noise
Biassystematic error of the average fit
Varhow much f̂(x) changes from one training set to another
σ²irreducible noise variance — a floor no model can go beneath
train / test erroraverage loss on the data used to fit / on fresh data. Generalization gap = test − train

(For squared error. Other losses decompose less cleanly, but the intuition carries over.)

Lab · The ghost-curve cloud

Try this
  1. Start at degree 1 and let datasets arrive slowly. Ghost curves bunch tightly together (low variance), but their average (green) clearly misses the dashed truth: high bias, red bar large.
  2. Move to degree 3–4: the green curve hugs the truth and the cloud is moderately tight. This is near the bottom of the test-error U in the lower chart.
  3. Move to degree 9 with only ~14 points: the ghosts explode in all directions (variance bar huge) though their average may still be near the truth. This is overfitting made visible. The lower chart shows training error (blue) near zero while test error (orange) is large.
  4. With degree 9, raise "points per dataset" to 40: variance collapses. More data tames a flexible model. Raise the noise σ instead: variance grows and the optimal degree moves left.
Common traps
  • Judging by training error. It can always be driven down by adding flexibility; a perfect training score alone proves nothing (it may be memorisation).
  • Overfitting to the test set. If you try 200 models and keep whichever scores best on the test set, that test score is now itself optimistic — you have trained on it indirectly. Keep a separate validation set for choices and touch the test set once (lesson 4.5).
  • "Bias" here is not social bias and not the intercept term b of a neuron. It means systematic error of the average prediction.
  • More parameters ≠ always worse. Double descent and the success of huge networks show that classical "too many parameters" warnings need nuance; what counts is effective flexibility, data and regularization.
  • You cannot compute bias and variance on real problems (the truth f is unknown and you have one dataset); they are a conceptual tool, which validation curves estimate indirectly.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Model selection: choosing degree, tree depth, number of layers, k in k-NN (small k = low bias/high variance), regularization strength: all ride the same U curve, located using a validation set.
  • Learning curves (error vs training-set size) diagnose the problem: large train–val gap = high variance → more data or regularize; both errors high and close = high bias → bigger model, better features.
  • Ensembles and dropout average many high-variance models; random forests are precisely variance reduction.
  • Early stopping: a network's effective flexibility grows with training time; stop when validation loss turns up.
  • Scaling LLMs: huge models trained on huge data sit on the far (double-descent) side where more parameters keep helping.

Check yourself

1. At a point the model's predictions across datasets are 0.8, 1.2, 1.0, 1.0 and the truth is 1.0, with noise σ² = 0.09. Compute bias², variance and expected error.
Mean = 1.0, so bias = 0, bias² = 0. Variance = (0.04+0.04+0+0)/4 = 0.02. Expected squared error = 0 + 0.02 + 0.09 = 0.11.
2. Training error 0.01, test error 0.50. Which is the more likely problem, high bias or high variance, and what would you try?
High variance (overfitting): large gap between train and test. Try more data, a simpler model, stronger regularization, dropout/early stopping, or ensembling.
3. Why can't we choose the model with the lowest training error?
Training error keeps decreasing as flexibility grows and eventually just measures how well the model memorised its sample, including noise. Only data not used for fitting (validation/test) estimates how the model will do on new inputs.

Takeaways

  • Expected test error = bias² + variance + irreducible noise.
  • Too simple: high bias (underfit). Too flexible: high variance (overfit). Train error falls forever; test error is U-shaped (classically).
  • More data, regularization and averaging reduce variance; bigger models reduce bias.
  • Double descent: very large models can generalise despite interpolating, so always measure on held-out data.
4.4

Uncertainty, intervals & tests

The questionModel A scores 84.0% accuracy on your test set and model B scores 85.5%. Is B really better, or did it just get lucky on this particular sample of examples? Any number computed from data wobbles from sample to sample — how much, and how do we state a conclusion that is honest about it?

Intuition

Recall from 4.1: an estimate is random, because the sample is. If you measured the average height of 25 random people, then again with another 25, you would get two different averages. The spread of those averages across hypothetical repetitions is the standard error (SE). It says how much trust to put in a single run.

The Central Limit Theorem (Module 3) gives us the key facts: the sample mean of n independent values with standard deviation \sigma has distribution approximately Gaussian, centred on the true mean, with standard deviation

\mathrm{SE}(\bar x)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}

Notice the \sqrt n: to halve your uncertainty you need four times the data. Since \sigma is usually unknown, we plug in the sample standard deviation s=\sqrt{\frac1{n-1}\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2} (that is where n-1 comes in — the sample mean is closer to the data than the true mean is, so dividing by n would underestimate the spread).

Build it up: confidence intervals

Rather than report a single number, report a range built to catch the truth most of the time. Since \bar x is within about 2 standard errors of \mu in 95% of experiments (68% within 1, 95% within 1.96, 99% within 2.58 for a Gaussian), flip it around: the interval \bar x\pm1.96\,\mathrm{SE} contains \mu in 95% of experiments. For small n with unknown \sigma replace 1.96 by the slightly bigger Student-t value t_{n-1} (it also accounts for the extra uncertainty from estimating s).

\text{CI}_{95\%}=\bar x\pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\cdot\frac{s}{\sqrt n}
Worked example

A sample of n=25 gives \bar x=52, s=10. SE = 10/√25 = 2. The t value for 24 degrees of freedom is 2.064. Interval: 52 ± 2.064 × 2 = 52 ± 4.13 = [47.87, 56.13]. (With the Gaussian 1.96 it would be [48.08, 55.92], slightly too narrow.) With four times the data, n=100, the SE halves to 1 and the interval to roughly ±2.

What the 95% means — and does not mean. The truth \mu is a fixed number. The interval is what is random: the procedure produces an interval that covers \mu in 95% of repetitions. Once computed, a particular interval either contains \mu or it does not; we just do not know which. It is not "a 95% probability that μ is in [47.87, 56.13]". The first lab makes this literal: you will watch intervals appear one by one and about one in twenty miss.

Build it up: hypothesis tests and p-values

A confidence interval answers "what are the plausible values?". A hypothesis test answers a yes/no question: "is the effect real, or could chance alone explain it?" The logic is a proof by improbable contradiction:

  1. Null hypothesis H_0: boring world — no difference (e.g. the two models are equally good).
  2. Choose a test statistic measuring the evidence against it (the observed difference in standard errors, z).
  3. Work out its distribution if H_0 were true (the "null distribution").
  4. The p-value is the probability, assuming H₀ is true, of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed.
  5. If the p-value is below a pre-chosen threshold \alpha (often 0.05) call the result statistically significant and reject H_0.
Worked example 1 — a coin

100 flips, 61 heads. Is the coin biased? Under H_0:p=0.5, \mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{0.5\cdot0.5/100}=0.05. The observed proportion is 0.61, so z=(0.61-0.5)/0.05=2.2. Two-sided p-value = P(|Z|\ge2.2)=2\times0.0139=\mathbf{0.0278}. Since 0.028 < 0.05, "significant at 5%".

Worked example 2 — comparing two models

On the same n=100 test examples, B beats A by 1.5 points on average, and the per-example differences have standard deviation 10. SE = 10/√100 = 1, z=1.5/1=1.5, two-sided p=0.1336. Not significant: a gap of 1.5 points is entirely plausible from chance on 100 examples. The honest summary: "B is 1.5 points better, 95% CI about [−0.5, 3.5]" — the interval includes 0.

Power and effect size. Two kinds of error exist: a false alarm (reject a true H_0; probability \alpha by construction) and a miss (fail to detect a real effect). The probability of detecting a real effect is the test's power. It grows with the effect size (how big the real difference is) and with n (smaller SE). With a true difference of 3 points, σ = 10 and n = 100 (SE = 1), power at α = 0.05 is P(Z>1.96-3)+P(Z<-1.96-3)\approx0.85; with n = 25 (SE = 2) it falls to about 0.32. And a tiny, practically useless effect becomes "significant" if n is huge — statistical significance is not importance; always report the effect size and its interval.

The formal definition

z=\frac{\bar x-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n},\qquad p=P\big(|Z|\ge|z_{\text{obs}}|\;\big|\;H_0\big)=2\big(1-\Phi(|z_{\text{obs}}|)\big),\qquad \text{reject }H_0\text{ if }p<\alpha
x̄, ssample mean and sample standard deviation
SE = σ/√nstandard error: standard deviation of the estimate across repeated samples
μ₀the value of the mean claimed by H₀ (0 for "no difference")
zhow many standard errors the observation is from the null value
Φthe standard normal CDF; 2(1-\Phi(|z|)) counts both tails (two-sided test)
αsignificance level: the tolerated false-alarm rate, chosen before looking
p-valueP(data this extreme or more | H₀ true) — a statement about data given H₀
tn−1Student-t critical value (≈ 1.96 for 95% when n is large)

Multiple comparisons. If you run 20 independent tests of true null hypotheses at \alpha=0.05, the chance of at least one false "discovery" is 1-0.95^{20}=0.64. Compare many models, features or hyperparameters and "significance" is guaranteed by luck. Remedies: decide the tests in advance, correct the threshold (Bonferroni: use \alpha/m), and replicate.

Common traps — p-value misreadings
  • "p = 0.03 means there is a 3% chance the null is true." No. The p-value is P(\text{data}\mid H_0), not P(H_0\mid\text{data}). Flipping the conditional is the base-rate fallacy of Module 3: the second needs a prior.
  • "p > 0.05 proves there is no effect." No: it only says the data are not surprising under H₀. A small study can fail to detect a real effect (low power). Absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence.
  • "Significant means important." With a million test examples a 0.05-point accuracy gain is "highly significant" and useless. Report effect size and a CI.
  • p-hacking. Trying many metrics, subsets, seeds or models and reporting the one that crossed 0.05 makes the real false-alarm rate far above 5% (20 tries → 64%). Fix the analysis before you look; correct for multiple comparisons.
  • "95% CI = 95% chance the truth is inside this interval" — the 95% belongs to the procedure, not the one interval.
  • Ignoring dependence. The formulas assume independent examples. Test examples from the same user, same document or consecutive time steps are correlated, so the real SE is larger than σ/√n.
  • For two models evaluated on the same test set, use a paired analysis of per-example differences, as in the worked example; comparing two separate error bars throws information away.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Comparing models honestly: a benchmark score is an estimate with an SE. Accuracy \hat p on n examples has \mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}; at 85% on 1000 examples that is ±1.1 points (×1.96 ≈ ±2.2), so a leaderboard gap of 0.5 points is noise. Tools: paired bootstrap, McNemar's test, paired t-test across seeds.
  • A/B tests of recommender and ranking changes use exactly the z-test above; online platforms track power and run-length for them.
  • Seed variance: RL and deep-learning results vary wildly between random seeds; reporting a mean ± CI across seeds is the modern standard.
  • Calibration and uncertainty: error bars on predictions, conformal prediction and Bayesian posteriors are generalisations of the confidence interval.
  • Evaluation of LLMs on small human-rated sets demands intervals: a "win rate 55%" from 100 comparisons has an SE of 5 points.

Check yourself

1. A sample of n = 64 has s = 12. Compute the standard error and the approximate 95% interval if x̄ = 40.
SE = 12/√64 = 1.5. Approximate 95% CI = 40 ± 1.96×1.5 = [37.06, 42.94] (the exact t value for 63 d.f. is ≈ 2.00, giving about ±3.0).
2. Eleven independent tests are each run at α = 0.05 and all nulls are true. What is the probability that at least one is "significant"? How would you fix this?
1 − 0.95¹¹ ≈ 1 − 0.5688 = 0.431. Use a corrected threshold (Bonferroni: α/11 ≈ 0.0045), pre-register the tests, or control the false-discovery rate.
3. A study reports p = 0.04 for a new method. State precisely what the 0.04 is, and one thing it is not.
It is the probability of seeing a difference at least this extreme if the null hypothesis (no real difference) were true. It is not the probability that the method does not work, nor the probability the result will replicate, nor a measure of how big or useful the effect is.

Takeaways

  • Estimates wobble; the standard error σ/√n measures by how much — 4× data halves it.
  • A 95% confidence interval is produced by a procedure that covers the truth 95% of the time; it is not a 95% probability for one interval.
  • A p-value is P(data this extreme | null), not P(null | data); significant ≠ important; non-significant ≠ no effect.
  • Power depends on effect size and n; multiple testing inflates false alarms. Report effect sizes with intervals and compare models with paired analyses.
4.5

Resampling & evaluation

The questionYou have 1000 labelled examples and one chance to get a trustworthy answer to "how well will this model do on future data?". If you test on the data you trained on, the answer is flattering and wrong. If you hold out half for testing, you waste half your data. And once you have tuned the model many times, which numbers can you still believe? How do we squeeze the maximum honest information out of limited data?

Intuition

Think of an exam. A student who has seen the exact questions beforehand will score well and prove nothing. A trustworthy exam must consist of questions the student has never seen. Training a model is studying; evaluation is the exam. Rule zero of evaluation: never grade on what the model studied from. Everything in this lesson is a technique for building an exam that is fair and, in the second half, for knowing how much to trust the grade it gives.

The standard layout uses three separate piles:

  • Training set — the model's parameters are fitted here (4.1).
  • Validation set — used to make choices: regularization strength, degree, architecture, when to stop. Looking at it is allowed, repeatedly.
  • Test set — locked away until the end; used once to report the final number. Every time a choice is made by looking at the test score, the test set silently turns into a validation set and its number stops being honest.

A typical split is 60/20/20 or 80/10/10; for huge datasets even 1% can be enough, because the test SE (4.4) is already small.

Build it up: cross-validation

With little data, a single validation set is both small (a noisy estimate, SE large) and wasteful. k-fold cross-validation solves this by letting every example take a turn as the exam:

  1. Shuffle the data and cut it into k equal folds.
  2. For i=1,\dots,k: train on the other k-1 folds; measure the error on fold i.
  3. Average the k scores. That is the CV estimate; their spread indicates its reliability.

Every example is used for validation exactly once and for training k-1 times, and each score comes from a model that never saw its own validation fold. Common choices: k=5 or 10. The extreme k=n is leave-one-out: lowest bias of the estimate, but n fits and highly correlated models. Cost: k times the training. (For classification keep class proportions in each fold: stratified folds; for time series, always validate on the future, never shuffle.)

Worked example

10 examples, k=5 → each fold has 2 examples; the model is trained on 8, validated on 2, five times. Fold errors: 0.30, 0.20, 0.25, 0.35, 0.15.

Mean = (0.30+0.20+0.25+0.35+0.15)/5 = 0.25. Deviations from the mean: 0.05, −0.05, 0, 0.10, −0.10; squares sum to 0.0025+0.0025+0+0.01+0.01 = 0.025; sample variance = 0.025/4 = 0.00625; sd = 0.0791. A rough standard error of the mean is 0.0791/√5 = 0.035 (an approximation: folds share training data, so they are not fully independent). Report: "CV error 0.25 ± 0.04".

Build it up: the bootstrap

In 4.4 the standard error of a mean came from a formula that required a theory (CLT). For the median, a correlation, an F1 score or the AUC no such tidy formula exists. The bootstrap gets the answer by brute force, using one clever stand-in: treat your sample as if it were the population.

  1. You have a sample of n values. Draw n values with replacement from it (pick one at random, write it down, put it back, repeat). This is a bootstrap resample: some original values appear twice or thrice, some not at all.
  2. Compute the statistic of interest on the resample.
  3. Repeat B times (1000–10000). The spread of the B statistics approximates how the statistic would vary across real new samples.
  4. Standard error ≈ standard deviation of those B values; a 95% percentile interval = the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.
Worked example

Original sample (3, 5, 8), mean 5.33. One resample of size 3 with replacement could be (5, 5, 8), with mean 6. There are 3³ = 27 equally likely ordered resamples. The chance a particular value, say 3, is never chosen is (2/3)^3=8/27=0.296. In general each point is missed with probability (1-1/n)^n\to e^{-1}\approx0.368: about 37% of examples are left out of each bootstrap resample and about 63% appear. Those left-out ones are the out-of-bag examples — a free validation set for that resample (the principle behind random forests' OOB error).

Build it up: leakage, the silent killer

All of the above relies on one condition: information from the held-out data must not reach the model before the final test. When it does, we call it data leakage, and scores become fantasy. Typical leaks:

  • Preprocessing before splitting. Standardising features with the mean and std of the whole dataset, selecting the "best 20 features" using all labels, fitting PCA or an imputer or a vocabulary on everything. The test rows influenced the pipeline. Correct order: split first; fit every transformation on the training part only; apply it unchanged to validation/test.
  • Duplicates and near-duplicates across splits (the same image augmented, the same user's records, overlapping text windows). Split by group (user, patient, document), not by row.
  • Time leakage: using the future to predict the past. Split by time.
  • Target leakage: a feature that secretly encodes the answer (a "refund issued" flag when predicting churn).
  • Tuning on the test set (see 4.3 and the three-pile rule), including benchmark contamination: evaluation items that appeared in an LLM's training data.
Worked example — leakage on pure noise

With n=60 examples whose labels are coin flips and d=1000 features of pure noise, no pipeline can honestly beat 50%. Yet among 1000 noise features some will correlate with the labels by luck: each correlation has size about 1/\sqrt n\approx0.13, and the largest of 1000 is around 0.43 (≈ 3.3 standard deviations — a simulation of this exact setting gives 0.43). If you pick the 20 "best" features using all labels and only then split to train a classifier, the test rows have helped choose those features, so the held-out accuracy is measured at about 91% in simulation. Choose the 20 features inside the training rows only and the accuracy is an honest 50%. Same data, same model, same code apart from the order of two steps. The lab reproduces this.

The formal definition

\widehat{\mathrm{Err}}_{\text{CV}}=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{|F_i|}\sum_{(x,y)\in F_i}\ell\big(y,\;\hat f^{(-i)}(x)\big),\qquad \mathrm{SE}_{\text{boot}}(T)\approx\sqrt{\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\big(T^{*b}-\bar T^{*}\big)^2}
knumber of folds
Fithe i-th fold (the held-out examples), |Fi| its size
f̂(−i)the model trained on all data except fold i
ℓthe loss (squared error, cross-entropy, 0–1 error…)
Tany statistic of the data (mean, median, AUC…)
T*bthe statistic computed on the b-th bootstrap resample; B resamples in total
T̄*the average of the B bootstrap statistics

The bootstrap interval is only as good as the sample: it cannot invent information about regions your data never visited, and it behaves poorly for extremes (the maximum) or very tiny samples.

Common traps
  • Reusing the test set. Each peek that influences a decision leaks information. If you must iterate, make a fresh validation set or use nested cross-validation (an outer CV for the final estimate, an inner one for tuning).
  • Preprocessing on all the data. Scalers, imputers, PCA, feature selection, target encoding, tokenisers/vocabularies: fit on training folds only — inside every CV fold, not once outside. Use a Pipeline object so the order is enforced.
  • Random splits when data are grouped or ordered (users, patients, videos, time). Use group-aware or time-ordered splits.
  • Reporting the best fold or best seed. Report the mean and spread over folds/seeds, with intervals (4.4).
  • Bootstrap on the wrong unit. Resample independent units (patients, documents), not correlated rows. And the bootstrap does not fix a biased or unrepresentative sample.
  • Mismatch between validation and deployment. A perfectly clean estimate of performance on the wrong distribution is still wrong (distribution shift, covered later).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Hyper-parameter search (grid/random search, Bayesian optimisation) scores each candidate by cross-validation or a validation split; scikit-learn's GridSearchCV does exactly the loop above.
  • Early stopping monitors the validation loss; the test set is only for the final report. Deep-learning benchmarks (ImageNet, GLUE, MMLU) formalise the locked test set, and benchmark contamination in LLMs is a leakage problem.
  • Bagging and random forests are bootstrap resamples + averaging (reduces variance, 4.3); the out-of-bag 37% gives free validation.
  • Confidence intervals on metrics (accuracy, F1, AUC, BLEU) by bootstrapping the test set — the honest way to compare two models.
  • MLOps: time-based splits for forecasting; group splits in medical imaging (all images of one patient in the same fold); data-leak audits before release.

Check yourself

1. You standardise a dataset with its global mean and standard deviation, then run 5-fold CV. What is wrong, and how do you fix it?
The scaler used statistics that include the validation fold's rows, so information from held-out data leaked into the pipeline. Inside each fold, compute the mean/std from the training folds only and apply them to the validation fold (e.g. put the scaler in a Pipeline).

3. 5-fold CV on 500 examples: how many examples train each model, and how many times is each example used for validation?
Each fold holds 100 examples, so each model trains on 400. Every example is in the validation fold exactly once (and in the training set 4 times).

Takeaways

  • Train to fit, validation to choose, test once to report. Never grade on what the model studied.
  • k-fold CV lets every example serve as validation once and gives an estimate plus a spread.
  • The bootstrap resamples with replacement to get standard errors and intervals for any statistic; ≈ 37% of points are left out of each resample.
  • Leakage (preprocessing before splitting, duplicates, time, target) produces impressive but fictional scores: split first, fit everything on training data only.
