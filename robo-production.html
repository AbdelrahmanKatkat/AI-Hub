Module 9 · Safety, deployment & operations

A robot that works in a demo is not yet a robot that people can trust next to them. This module is about the last mile: deciding what can go wrong and how bad it would be, watching the machine while it runs, limiting what a learned policy is allowed to do, testing changes before they reach hardware, fitting the control loop into its time budget, and keeping a fleet calibrated and updated. By the end you will compute a stopping distance by hand, size a watchdog, filter an unsafe command, read a test gate honestly, check a latency budget and plan a calibration interval.

6 lessons≈ 2 hoursNeeds Modules 0–8Safety is stated, not certified
Read this firstNothing on this page makes a robot safe to deploy. Safety comes from a documented risk assessment, a validated design, independent checks and the people who sign off on them. The simulations here are illustrative: a point on a rail, one joint, a synthetic fleet. Real certification needs a qualified assessor, and the standards named here (ISO 10218 for industrial robots, ISO 13482 for personal care robots) must be read in their current editions, not from this page.
9.1

Safety standards & risk assessment

The questionAn arm in a shared workcell moves its gripper at 1.0 m/s toward a shelf. A person steps into the arm's path. How far does the arm travel before it is fully stopped, and does its sensor notice the person early enough for the arm to stop short of them?

Intuition

Start with the chain of events, not with the robot. Something causes a situation (a person steps into the path). That situation becomes a hazardous event (the person is inside the arm's reach while the arm is moving). The event can lead to harm (a pinch or a blow). Every link in that chain is a place where you can intervene: remove the cause, separate the people from the motion, slow the motion, or stop it.

Risk assessment is the habit of walking that chain for every task and asking two questions: how bad could the harm be (its severity), and how often will someone be exposed to the hazard (its likelihood)? A common teaching device multiplies a severity score and a likelihood score. It is a way to rank problems, not a legal test, and the standards define their own methods.

Protective stop is the simplest safety function on most robots. When a sensor sees a person in a zone, the robot halts its motion. The hard part is not the decision to stop. It is making sure the robot is already stopped before it reaches the person. A robot cannot stop instantly, so the sensor has to see the person early enough.

Standards are shared, agreed recipes for doing this well. ISO 10218 covers the safety of industrial robots, the arms in factories. ISO 13482 covers personal care robots, the machines that work near or with people in homes and care settings. A team that designs to a standard still needs an independent assessment before it can say it complies.

Build it up: how far does the arm travel?

Step 1: the arm does not stop at once. When the stop command is issued, the robot first keeps moving for a short reaction time t_r (the controller, the drives and the brakes all take time to respond). During that time it covers v\,t_r metres, where v is its speed.

Step 2: then it brakes. Braking at a constant deceleration a starts at speed v and ends at zero. From the standard kinematics formula v_f^2 = v_0^2 - 2a\,\Delta x with v_f=0, the braking distance is v^2/(2a).

Step 3: add the two. The total stopping distance is the reaction part plus the braking part. The faster the arm moves, the longer both parts become, and the braking part grows with the square of the speed.

Step 4: place it against the hazard. Let the sensor trip at a distance D in front of the arm's starting point and the hazard zone begin at a distance H. The arm stops at D + v\,t_r + v^2/(2a), and this must be less than H.

Worked example: the shelf and the person

Speed v = 1.0\text{ m/s}, reaction time t_r = 0.10\text{ s}, braking a = 2.0\text{ m/s}^2.

Reaction part: v\,t_r = 1.0 \times 0.10 = 0.10\text{ m}. Braking part: v^2/(2a) = 1.0^2/(2 \times 2.0) = 0.25\text{ m}. Stopping distance from the trip point: 0.10 + 0.25 = 0.35\text{ m}.

Sensor trips at D = 0.60\text{ m}; the hazard starts at H = 1.00\text{ m}. The arm stops at 0.60 + 0.35 = 0.95\text{ m}, which is 0.05\text{ m} short of the hazard. The margin is small, so the design is fragile: a slower sensor or a longer reaction time would break it.

Now double the speed to 1.5\text{ m/s}. The reaction part is 0.15\text{ m} and the braking part is 2.25/4 = 0.5625\text{ m}, so the stop from the trip point is 0.7125\text{ m}. The arm now stops at 1.31\text{ m} and enters the hazard. Speed is the lever that breaks a design fastest.

A risk matrix ranks what you found. Here a teaching version scores severity S from 1 (slight) to 3 (severe) and likelihood L from 1 (rare) to 3 (frequent), and multiplies them. The pinch at the gripper, with severe harm and occasional exposure, scores 3 \times 2 = 6 out of 9, so it needs a design change, not only a warning sign.

Common traps
  • Stopping distance is not a constant. It depends on speed, load, payload inertia, the braking capacity and the reaction time, and all of these can change with wear. A stop measured once on a new arm is only a starting point.
  • A simulation that stops is not a safety case. The lab stops because its equations say so. A real system has sensor latency, dropouts and friction that the simulation leaves out.
  • Average speed hides the worst case. Design for the highest speed the robot can reach in that zone, including the speed it reaches on a path that is not the planned one.
  • A risk score is not a verdict. The 1 to 9 matrix ranks problems. The standards and an assessor decide what is acceptable.
Where it shows up in AI
  • A learned policy (Module 7 and 8) can produce speeds the designers never tested, so the stopping budget must be checked against the actions the policy can actually command, not only the ones a human planned.
  • Human-robot collaboration is a perception problem as well as a control problem. The person must be detected reliably, which links to the detectors of Module 4 and to the safety filters of the next lesson.

Check yourself

1. A robot moves at 0.8 m/s with tr = 0.12 s and a = 2.5 m/s². How far does it travel after the trip before it stops?
Reaction part: 0.8 × 0.12 = 0.096 m. Braking part: 0.8² / (2 × 2.5) = 0.64 / 5 = 0.128 m. Total dstop = 0.224 m.
2. Why does doubling the speed more than double the braking distance?
Braking distance is v²/(2a), which grows with the square of v. Doubling v multiplies it by 4. The reaction part v·tr only doubles.
3. A severe injury (S = 3) happens about once a week in a shared cell (L = 2). What does the teaching matrix give, and what does it tell you to do?
R = 3 × 2 = 6 out of 9. It is a high ranking, so the design must reduce the hazard (separation, speed limits, guarding), not just warn people. The matrix ranks; the standards and an assessor decide acceptability.

Takeaways

  • Walk the chain cause → hazardous event → harm, and act at the link that is cheapest to change.
  • Stopping distance is v·tr + v²/(2a); the sensor trip must leave room for it.
  • Standards (ISO 10218 for industry, ISO 13482 for personal care) set the rules. Compliance needs a qualified assessor, not a simulation.
9.2

Runtime monitoring & fault handling

The questionA robot arm is moving at 0.5 m/s. Its controller hangs for a moment, or a cable to a camera comes loose and the camera freezes on its last frame. Nothing crashes, and the arm keeps going. How does the robot notice that something is wrong, and what does it do during the first few tens of milliseconds after it notices?

Intuition

Think of a train driver's dead-man's switch. The driver must keep pressing a button; if the button is released, or the driver stops pressing it for too long, the train brakes. The absence of a signal is itself the alarm. A watchdog timer does exactly this in software: the controller must send a heartbeat at a regular period P, and if the watchdog hears nothing for longer than its timeout T, it assumes the worst and trips.

A watchdog catches silence. It does not catch wrong data. A frozen camera or a sensor stuck at a plausible value keeps sending messages, so the heartbeat stays alive. For that you need a second idea: a residual check. The robot has a model of what it expects to measure, for example the distance to a wall it knows is 1.20 m away. The residual is the difference between what the sensor reports and what the model predicts. If the residual is much bigger than the sensor noise, something is wrong.

Once a fault is detected, the robot should not jump straight from full speed to emergency stop every time. Robots use graceful degradation: a ladder of states, for example nominal, reduced speed, hold position, and controlled stop. Each rung trades a little performance for safety, and the robot climbs down only as far as the fault requires.

Build it up: the watchdog in time

Step 1: heartbeats arrive every P. With P = 10\text{ ms}, the controller sends beats at 0, 10, 20, … ms. The last beat before any moment is the last multiple of P that has passed.

Step 2: the silence grows. The watchdog measures how long it has been since the last beat. Even with a healthy stream, the silence swings from 0 up to just under P between beats. So the timeout must be larger than the period, or the watchdog trips on a healthy system.

Step 3: the trip. If the heartbeat stops at time t_f, the last beat was at \lfloor t_f/P \rfloor P, and the watchdog trips at that beat plus T.

Step 4: the coast. Between the fault and the trip the robot keeps moving, so the distance it covers before braking begins is the speed times that delay.

Worked example: a fault at 52 ms

Heartbeat period P = 10\text{ ms}, timeout T = 30\text{ ms}, speed v = 0.5\text{ m/s}, heartbeats stop at t_f = 52\text{ ms}.

Last beat before the fault: \lfloor 52/10 \rfloor \times 10 = 50\text{ ms}. Trip time: 50 + 30 = 80\text{ ms}. Delay from fault to trip: 80 - 52 = 28\text{ ms}.

Coast distance: v \times 0.028\text{ s} = 0.5 \times 0.028 = 0.014\text{ m} (14 mm) before the brakes act.

The residual check. The model predicts \hat z = 1.20\text{ m}. The sensor reports z = 1.85\text{ m}, and the sensor noise is \sigma = 0.05\text{ m}. With a gate multiplier k = 3, the residual is r = z - \hat z = 0.65\text{ m}, while the gate is k\sigma = 0.15\text{ m}. Since |r| > k\sigma, the reading is flagged. A single flag can be a noise spike, so a real monitor requires several flags in a row.

The formal definition

\text{trip if } t - t_{\text{last}}(t) > T, \qquad t_{\text{last}}(t) = \left\lfloor \frac{\min(t,\,t_f)}{P} \right\rfloor P
\text{flag if } |z_k - \hat z_k| > k\,\sigma \text{ for } N \text{ consecutive samples}

Common traps
  • A timeout shorter than the period. Healthy silence is always between 0 and P, so T ≤ P trips a working system (lab, step 2).
  • A watchdog on the thing that hangs. If the watchdog runs on the same processor as the controller and that processor freezes, the watchdog freezes too. It needs an independent timer.
  • A stuck sensor passes a heartbeat test. A camera that repeats its last frame sends perfectly regular messages. Only a model-based or content check notices it.
  • One flag stops the robot every time. Without a persistence rule (N consecutive samples), ordinary noise causes nuisance stops, and operators learn to ignore the alarm.
  • Degradation without a test. Each rung of the ladder must be tested on hardware. An untested fallback is a fault waiting to happen.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Perception models can fail silently, returning confident wrong answers. Monitors compare the model's outputs with cheaper physical checks such as a range reading or joint encoders, then trigger the same ladder as any other fault.
  • Learned controllers need an out-of-distribution monitor as well: when the input looks unlike anything in training, the robot should slow down rather than extrapolate.

Check yourself

1. P = 20 ms and T = 50 ms. The heartbeat stops at 133 ms. When does the watchdog trip, and how long after the fault?
Last beat: ⌊133/20⌋ × 20 = 6 × 20 = 120 ms. Trip: 120 + 50 = 170 ms. Delay from the fault: 170 − 133 = 37 ms.
2. Why is a residual check needed in addition to a heartbeat watchdog?
A heartbeat only proves that a message was sent. A frozen or stuck sensor can keep sending regular messages with wrong values. The residual compares the reading with what the model predicts, so it catches wrong data that the heartbeat misses.
3. Why require N consecutive flags rather than one?
Noise occasionally produces a large value by chance. Requiring several flags in a row keeps the false-alarm rate low while a real fault persists and so is still caught a few samples later.

Takeaways

  • A watchdog turns silence into a fault; its timeout must exceed the heartbeat period.
  • The coast distance v·(ttrip − tf) is what the braking must absorb after the fault.
  • Pair heartbeats (is it alive?) with residuals (is it right?), and degrade in steps rather than only stopping.
9.3

Safety filters & constraint enforcement

The questionA learned policy, trained on demonstrations, says “move the wrist at 0.9 rad/s”. The wrist is 0.3 rad away from its mechanical limit. Retraining the policy takes weeks. How do you guarantee, today, that the joint never passes its limit, and that the motion slows down sensibly as it approaches?

Intuition

Put a filter between the policy and the motors. The policy proposes a command; the filter checks it against the robot's safety rules and passes on only what is allowed. This is the same role a speed governor plays on an engine. The policy can be as clever or as clumsy as it likes; the filter enforces the rules whatever it proposes.

The simplest rule is a clamp: never exceed a maximum speed. That is easy, but it does not stop a joint from driving into its limit at full speed and then stopping dead. A better rule uses a barrier. Define a number h that is positive when the robot is safe and reaches zero at the boundary, for example the distance to the joint limit. Then require that the robot slows down in proportion to how much room is left. A joint far from its limit can move quickly; one just short of it can barely move. This is the intuition behind control barrier functions, which are used in safety-critical control.

Build it up: three limits, one command

Step 1: the speed limit. The motor has a maximum safe speed v_{\max}. Any command above it is cut to that value.

Step 2: the room to the limit. Let q be the joint angle and q_{\max} its upper limit. The room left is h = q_{\max} - q. When h is large, the barrier allows anything up to v_{\max}. When h is small, the barrier allows only a slow speed.

Step 3: the barrier condition. Require \dot h \ge -\gamma h, which says the room shrinks no faster than a fraction \gamma of the room left each second. Since \dot h = -\dot q, this gives \dot q \le \gamma (q_{\max} - q).

Step 4: combine. The filter applies the most cautious of the proposed speed, the speed limit and the barrier limit. The lower limit works the same way, mirrored.

Worked example: the wrist near its limit

Limits q_{\max} = 1.5\text{ rad}, v_{\max} = 0.5\text{ rad/s}, barrier gain \gamma = 2\text{ s}^{-1}. The policy proposes \dot q_{\text{prop}} = 0.9\text{ rad/s}.

A check in discrete time. The rule is continuous, but the controller acts every 10 ms. At q = 1.20, a step of \Delta t = 0.1\text{ s} with 0.5 rad/s moves the joint to 1.25 rad, safely inside the limit. A filter must also check the next step, not only the instant: if a step would cross the limit, cut the speed to (q_{\max} - q)/\Delta t, here 0.3/0.1 = 3\text{ rad/s}, which is not the binding limit at this point.

Common traps
  • A filter is only as good as its model. The barrier uses the joint limit you typed in. If the real limit is at 1.45 rad because of wear or a collision, the filter is wrong, and the safety margin is an assumption, not a fact.
  • Continuous rules need discrete checks. A rule that holds at every instant can still be crossed between two 10 ms updates at high speed. Check the next step explicitly (worked example).
  • Filtering is not a plan. A filter can slow a motion to zero near a limit, and the task may then stall or deadlock. The policy must learn to avoid those states, and the monitor must catch a stall.
  • Clamping hides the bug. If the filter clips constantly, the policy is badly trained. Log the clipped fraction and treat a high rate as a signal to retrain, not as success.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Safe reinforcement learning uses a shield: a filter that overrides unsafe actions chosen by a learned policy, which is the same architecture as this lesson applied to a whole policy (Module 7).
  • Foundation-model robots (Module 8) output action chunks that can be large. A safety filter sits between the chunk and the motors, so the learned part can be replaced without changing the safety rules.

Check yourself

1. q = 1.30 rad, qmax = 1.5, γ = 2 s⁻¹, vmax = 0.5 rad/s, proposal 0.9 rad/s. What is the applied speed?
h = 0.20, γh = 0.40. Applied = min(0.9, 0.5, 0.40) = 0.40 rad/s. The barrier binds.
2. Why does the barrier condition give a smooth slowdown, while a plain speed clamp does not?
The barrier makes the allowed speed γ·h shrink to zero as h shrinks to zero, so the joint decelerates as it approaches the limit. A speed clamp allows vmax right up to the limit and then cannot prevent a sudden stop there.
3. A learned policy's commands are clipped on 40 % of steps. Is the filter working or is something wrong?
The filter is enforcing its rules, but a 40 % clip rate means the policy keeps asking for unsafe motions. Treat it as a training problem to investigate, not as a success, and check whether the task still completes.

Takeaways

  • Put a filter between any learned policy and the motors; it enforces rules whatever the policy proposes.
  • Use a barrier h(q) ≥ 0 so the allowed speed shrinks smoothly toward a limit: vsafe ≤ γ·h.
  • Check the next discrete step, log every clipped command, and never trust a filter whose limits are wrong.
9.4

Testing: simulation, hardware-in-the-loop & logs

The questionYour team has changed the grasp policy. In its last 50 trials on the real arm it succeeded 46 times, so it looks like 92 % and better than before. Can you release it? Testing every change on the physical arm is slow and risky, yet trusting a simulator blindly is worse. What evidence is enough, and how sure can you be from 50 trials?

Intuition

Testing is a set of trade-offs between cost and realism. Cheap tests run in seconds and cover many small pieces. Realistic tests take hours and cover the whole system. The test pyramid says to build many cheap tests at the bottom, fewer scenario tests above them, and only a handful of expensive tests on real hardware at the top. Each layer catches what the layer below cannot: a unit test cannot tell you the arm will drop an object, and a field trial is too late to learn that a function returns the wrong sign.

Three kinds of test matter most for robots. A unit test checks one function, such as the inverse-kinematics solver on a set of known poses. A simulation test runs the whole software against a model of the world. A hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) test runs the real controller board or computer against a simulated plant, so the timing and the drivers are real even though the arm is not moving. A log replay feeds yesterday's recorded sensor data into today's software and compares the commands it would have sent with the ones that were sent. Replay is the cheapest way to test on real data, because the world does not need to be set up again.

The key idea for evaluation is that a success rate measured from a few trials is an estimate with uncertainty. You must report the uncertainty before you can compare two policies honestly.

Build it up: how sure is 46 out of 50?

Step 1: the point estimate. 46 successes in 50 trials gives \hat p = 46/50 = 0.92. This is the best single guess, but it is not certain. Repeat the 50 trials and you would probably get 44 or 48 instead.

Step 2: the interval. A 95 % confidence interval is a range that, over many repeated experiments, contains the true success rate 95 % of the time. The Wilson score interval is a good choice for success rates, especially with few trials or rates near 0 or 1, where the simple ±1.96·√(p(1−p)/n) formula misbehaves.

Step 3: the decision rule. Do not compare the point estimate with a threshold. Compare the lower bound of the interval with the threshold you require. Then you release only when you are confident the rate is above it.

Worked example: 46 of 50, and 1 of 10

For n = 50, s = 46, z = 1.96, the Wilson interval is

\frac{\hat p + \dfrac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \dfrac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \dfrac{z^2}{n}}

With z^2 = 3.8416, the denominator is 1 + z^2/n = 1.0768. The centre is (0.92 + 0.0384)/1.0768 \approx 0.8900. Inside the square root, 0.92 \times 0.08/50 + 3.8416/10000 \approx 0.00186, whose root is 0.0431, so the half-width is 1.96 \times 0.0431/1.0768 \approx 0.0784. The interval is [0.8116, 0.9685], or about [0.81, 0.97].

Suppose you require a lower bound of at least 0.85. The point estimate 0.92 passes, but the lower bound 0.8116 does not. The honest verdict is not yet: run more trials, or accept a lower threshold.

Now compare with 1 success in 10 trials. The point estimate is 0.10, but the 95 % interval is [0.018, 0.404]. The data cannot tell a 2 % policy from a 40 % one. Ten trials are a smoke test, not a measurement.

Replay. A 1-D controller in the log approaches a unit step as 1 - e^{-2s}, where s is seconds after the step. The new build, with gain k = 2.5, approaches it as 1 - e^{-2.5s}. Replaying the same step through both gives two positions, and their difference is D(s) = e^{-2s} - e^{-2.5s}. At s = 0.5\text{ s} the log is at 0.632 and the new build at 0.714, a difference of 0.081. The difference peaks at s = \ln(k/2)/(k-2) \approx 0.446\text{ s}, where it is about 0.082. A regression gate compares that peak with its tolerance.

  4. With 0 failures in n trials, the 95 % Wilson upper bound on the failure rate is z²/(n + z²) = 3.8416/(n + 3.8416). Estimate the smallest n that brings it below 5 % using the rule of three (3/n), then evaluate the exact formula by hand for n = 72 and n = 73. Which one passes?
Common traps
  • Reporting the point estimate alone. 92 % from 50 trials is an interval of about 81 % to 97 %. Quote the interval.
  • Testing on the trials that you used to tune. A policy checked on the scenes it was tuned on will look better than it is. Keep held-out scenes, as in Module 8.
  • A passing simulator is not a passing robot. The simulator omits friction, latency and sensor noise. Use HIL and field trials to find what it misses.
  • Replay that matches exactly proves little. If the new build reproduces the log exactly, the test checks the logging. The question is what changed, and whether the change is intended.
  • Fixing a seed to make a test pass. A scenario that passes for one random seed only is a lucky run. Run many seeds and report the failures.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Embodied evaluation (Module 8) uses the same interval logic: success rates on held-out scenes with confidence intervals, and simulation results checked against real benchmarks.
  • Offline evaluation of a learned policy on logged demonstrations is cheap, and it is a form of replay. Its results must still be confirmed on hardware, since a policy can score well offline and fail when it changes the world it observes.

Check yourself

1. A policy succeeds 9 times in 10 trials. Is a release threshold of 0.85 on the lower bound met? (Wilson 95 %: about [0.60, 0.98].)
No. The point estimate is 0.90, but the lower bound is about 0.60, far below 0.85. Ten trials cannot demonstrate a success rate above 0.85 at 95 % confidence.
2. Why does a unit test not replace a hardware-in-the-loop test?
A unit test checks one function in isolation, with fixed inputs. It cannot see timing, driver behaviour, the real controller's scheduling, or the interactions between modules. HIL runs the real controller at its real rate against a simulated plant, which exposes those issues.
3. In the replay example, why is the largest deviation not at s = 0?
At s = 0 both trajectories are at the step (their difference is e0 − e0 = 0). The two responses separate as time passes, and the difference rises to a maximum near s ≈ 0.446 s and then decays because both go to zero.

Takeaways

  • Use a test pyramid: many cheap tests, few expensive ones, each layer catching what the one below cannot.
  • Report success rates with intervals (Wilson 95 %), and release on the lower bound.
  • Replay recorded logs through new software, compare the outputs with a tolerance, and confirm the important cases on hardware.
9.5

Onboard compute & latency budgets

The questionA grasp camera feeds a perception model every 100 ms (10 Hz), while the motor torque loop must run every 1 ms (1 kHz). Where should each computation run? And how do you know that every cycle finishes before the next one is due, rather than most of the time?

Intuition

Every control loop has a period: the interval at which it must run. The period is the robot's budget. Inside one cycle the robot must sense, compute and act, and every stage takes time: a camera has an exposure, a model takes time to run, a message takes time to cross a network, and a motor command takes time to reach the drive. The stage times add up. If they add up to more than the period, the cycle misses its deadline, and the command goes out late, from a stale picture of the world.

A real-time system guarantees its deadlines, or at least bounds how often it misses them. A best-effort system usually meets them but makes no promise. Most robots need both: a fast real-time loop for motors, which must never be late, and a slower best-effort loop for perception and planning, which can be late occasionally if its results are handled safely.

Finally, a fixed average is not enough. Each cycle takes a slightly different time because of jitter (scheduling, cache misses, network queues, thermal throttling). The question is not what the average cycle takes, but how often a cycle exceeds the period.

Build it up: the numbers for two loops

Step 1: the period. A loop at rate f has period T = 1/f. At 1 kHz that is 1 ms; at 10 Hz it is 100 ms; at 30 Hz it is 33.3 ms.

Step 2: add the stages. List every stage on the path from sensor to actuator, with its time.

Step 3: compare with the budget. The margin is T - \sum d_i. A negative margin is a guaranteed miss; a small positive margin is a miss waiting for jitter.

Worked example: the grasp loop at 10 Hz

Budget T = 1000/10 = 100\text{ ms}. Stages: camera capture 33 ms, perception 40 ms, planning 12 ms, network hop 8 ms, actuation command 2 ms.

Total 33 + 40 + 12 + 8 + 2 = 95\text{ ms}. Margin 100 - 95 = 5\text{ ms}.

That 5 ms margin is thin. With 10 % jitter on each stage, a typical cycle is about 95 ms but some cycles exceed 100 ms. The lab shows how often.

Worked example: the torque loop at 1 kHz

Budget T = 1\text{ ms}. Stages on the local bus: sense 0.05 ms, estimate 0.20 ms, control 0.30 ms, actuate 0.10 ms. Total 0.65\text{ ms}, margin 0.35\text{ ms}.

Now route the same loop through a network hop of 0.4 ms. Total 1.05\text{ ms}, which misses the deadline on every cycle. The fix is architectural: keep the 1 kHz loop on the local bus, and send only setpoints or slow commands across the network.

Where to compute. A GPU gives very high throughput for perception models, but its scheduling and memory transfers add variable delay, so it is a poor place for a 1 kHz servo loop. A microcontroller or real-time processor runs short, fixed-time loops deterministically but has little compute. Most robots therefore split the work: fast servo loops on a deterministic controller, perception on a GPU module, and the boundary between them designed with explicit latency numbers.

The formal definition

T = \frac{1}{f}, \qquad \tau_k = \sum_{i} d_i\,(1 + \varepsilon_{ik}), \qquad \text{deadline met in cycle } k \iff \tau_k \le T
\text{miss rate} = \frac{\#\{k : \tau_k > T\}}{\#\{k\}}
floop rate (Hz)
Tperiod, or budget per cycle (ms when f is in Hz: T = 1000/f)
d_inominal time of stage i, such as capture, perception, planning, network, actuation (ms)
\varepsilon_{ik}random fractional jitter of stage i in cycle k (dimensionless, typically a few percent to tens of percent)
\tau_kactual total time of cycle k (ms)
Pipeline positionStages 1 to 6 all meet here. The loop rate sets the budget, and each stage writes its time into the log. It stores timing histograms per stage, which show where the budget goes.

Lab

Try this
  1. Press play at the default settings (10 Hz, stages totalling 95 ms, jitter 10 %). Read the margin in the first cycle, then watch the history strip for red cycles. Roughly how many of the last 60 cycles miss?
  2. Set jitter to 0 %. Do any cycles still miss? What does that tell you about the difference between nominal and actual timing?
  3. Raise perception to 50 ms, so the total is 105 ms. Predict the miss rate before you press play, then check it.
  4. Set the loop to 1000 Hz with capture 0, perception 0, planning 0.5, network 0, actuation 0.1 and jitter 10 % (total 0.6 ms). Then raise network to 0.5 ms (total 1.1 ms). Which cycles miss, and what does this say about where a 1 kHz loop may run?
Common traps
  • Forgetting the sensor. Camera exposure and readout are latency. A 33 ms capture delays the picture before any model sees it.
  • Judging by the average. A mean of 95 ms with a 100 ms deadline sounds safe, but a 5 ms margin with jitter means real misses. Report the tail (the 99th percentile or the miss rate).
  • Putting a hard loop behind a network. Network queues vary. Keep deadline-critical loops on the local bus.
  • Stale data that looks fresh. A late perception result may be used as if it were current. Stamp every message with its capture time and reject old ones.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Vision-language-action models (Module 8) may take hundreds of milliseconds per inference. Action chunks let the robot execute several steps while the next chunk is computed, so the slow model runs asynchronously and the fast loop never waits for it.
  • Quantising or distilling a policy is often driven by this budget: a smaller model that meets the deadline on the robot's own compute can be more useful than a larger one that misses it.

Check yourself

1. A 500 Hz loop has stages totalling 1.6 ms. Does it meet its deadline? What is the margin?
The period is 1000/500 = 2 ms. The margin is 2 − 1.6 = 0.4 ms. It meets the nominal deadline, but a 25 % jitter on the 1.6 ms (0.4 ms) would use up all the margin.
2. Why is the mean cycle time not enough to certify a loop?
A deadline is missed by the cycles in the tail of the distribution. Two loops with the same mean can have very different tails, and only the proportion of cycles above T tells you how often the robot acts on stale data.
3. Where should a 1 kHz joint servo run, and why?
On a deterministic real-time controller or on the local bus, close to the drives. It needs fixed, short timing that a GPU or a network hop cannot guarantee. Perception and planning can run on the GPU at a slower rate.

Takeaways

  • The period T = 1/f is the budget; the stage times must sum to less than T with room for jitter.
  • Judge a loop by its miss rate in the tail, not its average.
  • Split the work: deterministic loops for control, GPU or cloud for heavy perception, with explicit latency numbers at each boundary.
9.6

Fleet operations & maintenance

The questionYour company runs 1 000 robots in twelve warehouses. The force sensor in one kind of gripper drifts a little every day, so grips slowly get weaker or harder than designed. You also have a new policy to send to all of them. How do you know when each robot needs recalibrating, and how do you send an update to a thousand machines without betting the fleet on a single test?

Intuition

Once a robot works in the field, the job changes. You stop asking “does it work?” and start asking “is it still working as well as it did, and what will break next?” Telemetry is the stream of measurements that answers this: sensor offsets, motor currents, cycle times, fault codes, and the time and place each one came from. Telemetry is only useful with units, timestamps and a robot identity attached to every reading.

Drift is a slow change in a sensor or a mechanism. Gears wear, belts stretch, and sensors warm up and shift. Drift is usually close to a straight line over short periods, which makes it predictable. A simple rule, recalibrate when the predicted offset reaches a tolerance, is often enough to keep grips and positions within spec.

Staged rollouts send a change to a small group first and widen it only if the group behaves. The first group is a canary, named after the canary in a mine. A rollout also needs a rollback path, so a bad update can be reversed on every robot that took it.

Build it up: drift, then rollout

Step 1: the drift line. Suppose the offset grows by r newtons per day after calibration. The offset after t days is r\,t.

Step 2: the recalibration interval. If the tolerance is \text{tol}, the offset reaches it after \text{tol}/r days. Recalibrate at that interval, then the offset resets to zero.

Step 3: the canary. Send the update to a few robots. Count their failures. The observed failure rate from a small group is uncertain, so use the Wilson interval from lesson 9.4 before deciding.

Step 4: widen in stages. Move from the canary to a larger group, then to everyone, halting if the interval shows the failure rate is clearly above the limit.

Worked example: the drifting gripper

Drift r = 0.02\text{ N/day}, tolerance \text{tol} = 0.5\text{ N}. Recalibration interval 0.5/0.02 = 25\text{ days}.

Twenty days after calibration the offset is 0.02 \times 20 = 0.40\text{ N}, which is 80 % of the tolerance. On day 25 the offset reaches 0.50 N and the robot is recalibrated.

The canary. Suppose 10 canary robots run the new policy and 1 fails. The point estimate is 10 %, but the 95 % Wilson interval is about [1.8 %, 40.4 %]. That cannot distinguish a 2 % failure rate from a 40 % one, so the canary is undecided at a 5 % limit. Suppose instead 100 robots run it and 3 fail. The interval is about [1.0 %, 8.5 %]. It still straddles 5 %, so the decision is still open, but the picture is far sharper. Small canaries cannot promote an update, however clean they look.

The formal definition

\text{offset}(t) = r\,(t - t_{\text{cal}}), \qquad \text{recalibrate when } \text{offset} \ge \text{tol}, \qquad \Delta t_{\text{cal}} = \frac{\text{tol}}{r}
\text{promote a stage if } U < p_{\text{limit}}, \qquad \text{halt if } L > p_{\text{limit}}
rdrift rate of the sensor offset (N per day in the example)
t, t_{\text{cal}}current time and time of the last calibration (days)
\text{tol}acceptable offset before recalibration (same unit as the offset)
\Delta t_{\text{cal}}interval between recalibrations (days), assuming linear drift
L, Ulower and upper bounds of the 95 % Wilson interval for the canary failure rate
p_{\text{limit}}the failure rate that the rollout must not exceed

  2. Set the drift to 0.05 N/day. How often must the gripper be recalibrated now? Predict the day of the first recalibration, then check it.

Common traps
  • Averages hide the robot that is failing. A fleet mean looks fine while three robots drift out of spec. Monitor the worst robots, not only the mean.
  • Telemetry without units or timestamps. A reading of “12” means nothing across firmware versions. Log units, time and identity.
  • Drift assumed linear forever. Linear drift is a good short-term model. Check the residuals, because temperature, wear and a changed payload can bend the line.
  • An update with no rollback. If you cannot undo an update on the robots that received it, a staged rollout only limits the harm, it does not remove it.
  • Calibrating to the wrong reference. A recalibration against a jig that has itself drifted resets every robot to a wrong zero.
Where it shows up in AI
  • A fleet is a data source. Logs from failures and near misses are the most valuable training data a deployed robot produces, which is why the monitoring of Module 9 and the learning of Module 7 are one loop.
  • Learned models drift too. A policy trained last year can be less accurate when the lighting, the products or the shelves change, so it needs the same telemetry, canary and rollback discipline as a mechanical part.

Check yourself

1. Drift is 0.03 N/day and tolerance is 0.6 N. How often must the sensor be recalibrated, and what is the offset 15 days after calibration?
Interval = 0.6 / 0.03 = 20 days. Offset after 15 days = 0.03 × 15 = 0.45 N, which is still under the tolerance.
2. A canary of 5 robots has no failures. Can you promote the update?
Not on that evidence. With 0 of 5, the 95 % Wilson interval is roughly [0 %, 43 %], which is wide enough to hide a failure rate far above any sensible limit. A larger canary is needed.
3. Why is a staged rollout not enough on its own?
It limits how many robots a bad update reaches before someone notices, but it does not undo the damage on the robots that already have it. A tested rollback path, and a monitor that halts the rollout, are needed as well.

Takeaways

  • Log telemetry with units, timestamps and robot identity, and watch the worst robots, not only the mean.
  • Recalibrate at Δt = tol / r for linear drift, and check the residuals so the model does not go stale.
  • Roll out in stages, decide with confidence intervals, and always keep a tested rollback.
