Module 5 · Efficient adaptation & inference

A trained language model is expensive twice: once to adapt to your task, and again every time it answers. This module is about paying less in both places without changing what the model means. It sits in the adapt and compress and generate stages of the pipeline (collect and tokenise, pretrain, align, adapt and compress, generate, evaluate and guard). The six lessons share one idea: find the part of the cost that is wasted, such as a weight update that has far fewer degrees of freedom than its matrix, digits that carry no information, a memory trip repeated for every token, or experts that are idle on this token.

5.1

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA)

The questionA weight matrix in a language model is 1 024 × 1 024: more than a million numbers. You want to adapt the model to a new task. Full fine-tuning would update every number and keep an optimiser state for each one. Can you get most of the benefit by training only a small fraction of the numbers, and how small can that fraction be?

Intuition: describe the change, not the whole matrix

Fine-tuning changes a weight matrix W into W+\Delta W. The bet behind LoRA is that the change \Delta W is simple: it points along only a few directions, even though W itself is rich. A picture: a huge spreadsheet of numbers can be a whole photograph (rich), but the edit "make it a bit warmer and a bit brighter" is just two instructions. A matrix that is the sum of a few "one direction in, one direction out" patterns is called low rank, and you can store such a matrix as two thin matrices instead of one fat one. LoRA freezes W and trains just those two thin matrices.

Build it up

Rank 1 first. Take a column b with 4 entries and a row a with 4 entries. Their product (every entry of b times every entry of a) is a 4×4 matrix with 16 entries, but you only stored 4 + 4 = 8 numbers. Every row of that matrix is a multiple of the same row a: one "pattern" with different strengths.

Worked example: a rank-1 update of a 4×4 matrix

Let B=(1,\ 0.5,\ -1,\ 2)^\top (a 4×1 column) and A=(0.2,\ -0.4,\ 0.1,\ 0.3) (a 1×4 row). Then \Delta W=BA, entry (i, j) being B_i A_j:

Parameter count. The full 4×4 matrix has 4\times4=16 numbers. The pair has r(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}})=1\times(4+4)=8: half.

Using it without building it. Let the frozen W be the identity and the input x=(1,2,0,-1)^\top. Then Wx=x. The adapter path first computes Ax=0.2-0.8+0+(-0.3)=-0.9 (one number), then B(Ax)=-0.9\cdot(1,0.5,-1,2)=(-0.9,\,-0.45,\,0.9,\,-1.8). The output is Wx+BAx=(0.1,\ 1.55,\ 0.9,\ -2.8). Multiplying by the merged matrix W+\Delta W row by row gives the same four numbers, so the update can be merged into the base weights after training and costs nothing extra at inference.

At a realistic size. For a 1 024 × 1 024 matrix and rank 8: 8\times(1024+1024)=16\,384 trainable numbers against 1024\times1024=1\,048\,576, which is 1.5625%. If a 24-layer model adapts four such matrices per layer (the attention projections), the trainable total is 24\times4\times16\,384=1\,572\,864 against 24\times4\times1\,048\,576=100\,663\,296 in those matrices.

When it is not a saving. The pair holds fewer numbers only while r<d_{\text{in}}d_{\text{out}}/(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}}). For a 6×6 matrix that break-even is exactly 36/12=3: at rank 3 the pair and the matrix have 36 numbers each, and rank 4 would need 48.

How is the pair trained? Initialise A with small random numbers and B with zeros, so BA=0 and the model starts as the unchanged base model. Then ordinary gradient descent updates only A and B; the second lab below shows it fitting a 6×6 target change. A rank-r matrix cannot copy a target of higher rank, and the exact leftover is known: the best any rank-r matrix can do is set by the singular values you drop (see SVD).

The formal picture

h = W x + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,B A x,\qquad W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times d_{\text{in}}}\ \text{frozen},\ B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}\times r},\ A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\text{in}}},\ r\ll\min(d_{\text{in}},d_{\text{out}})
\#\text{trainable}=r\,(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}}),\qquad \text{ratio}=\frac{r(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}})}{d_{\text{in}}d_{\text{out}}},\qquad W_{\text{merged}}=W+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA
Symbols. x: the input vector to the layer; h: its output. W: the pretrained weights, never changed. A: a thin matrix that reads the input down to r numbers; B: a thin matrix that expands those back to the output size. r: the rank, the width of the bottleneck and the main knob. \alpha: a constant scale (many implementations use \alpha/r so that changing r does not need the learning rate retuned; the worked example used \alpha=r). The three dimensions d_{\text{in}},d_{\text{out}} and r give the counts above. Where it is applied is a choice: commonly the attention projections, often the feed-forward matrices too.

What it saves besides parameters: the optimiser (for example Adam) keeps two extra numbers per trainable parameter, so with 0.0156 of the parameters trainable the optimiser memory shrinks by the same factor. The frozen W still has to be in memory for the forward and backward pass, which is why LoRA is commonly combined with a quantised base model. A separate small adapter file per task can be swapped in without storing a full copy of the model for each.

Common traps
  • "LoRA is a smaller model." The model is the same size at inference after merging, or slightly larger if the adapter is kept separate. What shrinks is the training state, not the network.
  • "Higher rank is always better." Beyond the rank the change actually needs, extra rank fits noise and costs more. Whether a task fits a low rank is an empirical question; some large changes (such as learning a new language) may need more rank than a small style change.
  • Zero-initialising both factors. If both A and B started at zero, both gradients would be zero and nothing would ever move. One factor starts at zero and the other random for exactly this reason.
  • Counting only one matrix. The saving applies to every adapted matrix; total trainable numbers is the per-matrix count times the number of adapted matrices.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Fine-tuning on one GPU. Adapting a large pretrained model with a few million trainable numbers makes it feasible on modest hardware. See transfer and fine-tuning for the vision version of the same idea.
  • Many adapters, one base. A service can keep one copy of the base weights and load a different small adapter per customer or task.
  • Variants. Quantised-base training, learning the rank per layer, and decomposing the magnitude and direction of weights are published variants of the same idea.

Check yourself

1. A 2 048 × 512 matrix is adapted with rank 16. How many trainable numbers, and what share of the matrix?
r(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}})=16\times(512+2048)=40\,960. The matrix has 2048\times512=1\,048\,576, so the share is 40 960 / 1 048 576 = 3.90625%.
2. Why is the merged matrix W + BA free at inference, but a separate adapter is not?
After merging, there is only one matrix to multiply, the same shape as before. A separate adapter adds two extra thin multiplications per layer (A then B) and an addition, a small extra cost, in exchange for being able to swap adapters.
3. In the worked 4×4 example, what is the rank of ΔW, and why must every row be a multiple of (0.2, −0.4, 0.1, 0.3)?
Rank 1. Row i of BA is B_i times the single row A, so every row points the same way, with strengths 1, 0.5, −1 and 2.

Takeaways

  • LoRA freezes W and trains \Delta W=BA with r(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}}) numbers instead of d_{\text{in}}d_{\text{out}}.
  • It is a saving only below the break-even rank d_{\text{in}}d_{\text{out}}/(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}}), and it can only express a change whose rank is at most r.
  • Merging gives back a normal matrix, so inference cost is unchanged.
5.2

Quantisation

The questionA model's weights are stored as 16-bit numbers. Does each weight really need 65 536 possible values? What happens to a weight if you keep only 16 values (4 bits), how big is the damage, and what does it save?

Intuition: measure with a coarser ruler

Measuring a table with a ruler marked in centimetres rather than micrometres loses detail but needs far fewer marks. Quantisation replaces each weight by the nearest mark on a coarse ruler and stores only the mark number. A bit pattern of b bits can name 2^b marks. Two numbers describe the ruler: how far apart the marks are (the scale) and which mark means exactly zero (the zero point). The price is rounding error; the reward is that memory, and the data that must travel from memory (lesson 5.3), shrinks by the ratio of bit widths.

Build it up

The scheme used here is the standard uniform one (sometimes called asymmetric or affine). Find the smallest and largest weight, always including 0 in the range. Spread 2^b equally spaced marks between them: there are 2^b-1 gaps, so the gap (scale) is s=(x_{\max}-x_{\min})/(2^b-1). Mark number q stands for the value s(q-z), where the zero point z is the mark that stands for 0.

Worked example: eight weights, 4 bits

Weights: x=(-0.62,\,-0.31,\,-0.05,\,0,\,0.12,\,0.38,\,0.47,\,0.90). Range from −0.62 to 0.90, so s=(0.90-(-0.62))/15=1.52/15=0.101333. The zero point is z=\text{round}(0.62/0.101333)=\text{round}(6.118)=6. Each weight becomes q=\text{round}(x/s)+z and is read back as \hat x=s(q-z):

The mean squared error is (0.012^2+0.006^2+0.05^2+0+0.01867^2+0.02533^2+0.03667^2+0.012^2)/8=6.448\times10^{-4}, and the largest error is 0.05 at weight 3. Notice that the real 0 is stored exactly (mark 6): that is what the zero point is for, because zeros are common and must stay zero. Notice also that weight 3 (−0.05) was crushed to 0: it was closer to mark 6 than to mark 5.

More bits. At 8 bits (255 gaps): s=1.52/255=0.005961, z=104, mean squared error 1.127\times10^{-6} and largest error 0.00231. At 3 bits (7 gaps): s=0.217143, z=3, mean squared error 3.345\times10^{-3} and largest error 0.0971. Each bit removed roughly doubles the step size, hence roughly quadruples the squared error.

Memory. For one billion weights: 16 bits = 2.0 GB, 8 bits = 1.0 GB, 4 bits = 0.5 GB, ignoring the scales. If every group of 64 weights stores one 16-bit scale and one 16-bit zero point, each weight costs 4+32/64=4.5 bits, so 0.5625 GB.

Why one scale for everything is fragile. A single large weight (an outlier) stretches the range, so the gaps get big and all the ordinary small weights are rounded coarsely. The remedy is to give a ruler to each small group: one scale per output channel (a row of the matrix), or per group of, say, 64 weights. An outlier then damages only its own group. The price is storing more scales, as the bit count above shows. The second lab makes this visible.

The formal picture

s=\frac{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}{2^{b}-1},\quad z=\operatorname{round}\!\left(-\frac{x_{\min}}{s}\right),\quad q=\operatorname{clamp}\!\big(\operatorname{round}(x/s)+z,\ 0,\ 2^{b}-1\big),\quad \hat x=s\,(q-z)
|\hat x-x|\le \frac{s}{2}\ \text{for every }x\text{ inside the range},\qquad \text{bits per weight}=b+\frac{\text{bits per group for scale and zero point}}{\text{group size}}
Symbols. b: bits per weight; 2^b marks. x_{\min},x_{\max}: the smallest and largest value in the set that shares this ruler, taken with 0 included. s: scale, the gap between marks. z: zero point, the integer mark meaning 0. q: the stored integer. \hat x: the dequantised value used in the computation. The bound s/2 is the worst-case rounding error: it holds because every value lies within half a gap of some mark. A common simpler variant is symmetric quantisation, z=0 and s=\max|x|/(2^{b-1}-1), which wastes a little range but makes the arithmetic simpler. Whether to quantise weights only, or also the activations and the cached keys and values, is a design choice; each has its own error behaviour.

Common traps
  • Quoting memory as if the scales were free. The advertised bit width excludes the scales and zero points; they add 32/\text{group size} bits per weight in the example scheme.
  • Assuming error is uniform. Worst-case error is s/2 but one badly placed weight (like the −0.05 above) can matter more than its size suggests; the effect on model quality is measured on real tasks, not inferred from rounding error alone.
  • Forgetting zero. If the range excluded 0, zero would not be representable. The range is therefore always widened to include 0.
  • Storing dequantised numbers. The saving only exists if the integers q are what is kept in memory and moved; dequantisation happens as the numbers are used.
Where it shows up in AI
  • On-device and low-cost serving. Four- and eight-bit weights let a model fit in far less memory, and because decoding is bound by memory traffic (lesson 5.3), fewer bytes per weight can also mean faster tokens.
  • Quantised fine-tuning. Keeping the frozen base in low precision and training LoRA adapters on top is a common recipe that combines lessons 5.1 and 5.2.
  • Quantisation in vision. The same uniform scheme is used for convolutional networks; see compression for vision.

Check yourself

1. Weights range from −0.4 to 0.8 and you use 3 bits. What are s and z?
s=(0.8+0.4)/7=0.171429, and z=\text{round}(0.4/0.171429)=\text{round}(2.333)=2. So mark 2 means 0, mark 0 means −0.3429 (the rounded −0.4), and mark 7 means 0.857.
2. Going from 8 bits to 4 bits, by what factor does the step size s change (same range)?
s\propto1/(2^b-1): 255 gaps become 15 gaps, so s grows by 255/15 = 17 times, and the worst-case rounding error does too.
3. Why does one huge outlier hurt a per-tensor scale more than a per-group scale?
It sets x_{\max} for the whole tensor, making s large for every weight. With groups, only its own group's s is large; the other groups keep fine rulers.

Takeaways

  • Uniform quantisation stores q=\text{round}(x/s)+z and reads back s(q-z); the error is at most s/2.
  • Memory scales with bits per weight, including the overhead of scales and zero points.
  • Outliers hurt a shared scale; per-channel or per-group scales contain the damage at a small bit cost.
5.3

Serving: prefill, decode and batching

The questionA processor can do a hundred trillion arithmetic operations a second, yet a language model typing out one answer token at a time uses a sliver of that. Why is generating text so slow compared with reading a prompt of the same length, and why does serving many users at once make each token cheaper?

Intuition: a worker who must fetch every tool for every nail

Imagine a carpenter whose tools sit in a warehouse. Hammering one nail takes a second, but fetching all the tools from the warehouse takes a minute. If he has one nail to drive, nearly all the time is spent fetching. If he has a hundred nails, he fetches once and drives them all: the fetching is shared. A language model's weights are the tools, in memory; the arithmetic is the hammering. During decoding the model produces one token for one sequence, which needs every weight once but does little arithmetic with each: fetching dominates. During prefill, the model reads the whole prompt at once, so each weight fetched is used for hundreds of prompt tokens: hammering dominates.

Build it up

The currency is arithmetic intensity: floating-point operations per byte moved from memory. A chip has a ridge point, its peak compute divided by its memory bandwidth. A job whose intensity is below the ridge waits for memory (memory-bound); above it waits for arithmetic (compute-bound). Time for a phase is the larger of the compute time and the memory time.

Worked example: a toy server with round numbers

Assumptions (all illustrative). A model with P=10^9 parameters stored as 2-byte numbers, so the weights take 2P=2\times10^9 bytes. One token through the model costs about 2P floating-point operations (one multiply and one add per weight). A made-up chip with peak compute 10^{14} operations per second and memory bandwidth 10^{12} bytes per second, so the ridge is 10^{14}/10^{12}=100 operations per byte. The cache of past keys and values costs, in this toy, 65 536 bytes per token of context (16 layers × keys and values × 1 024 numbers × 2 bytes). Activations and other traffic are ignored.

Prefill, prompt of T = 512 tokens. Operations: 2PT=2\times10^9\times512=1.024\times10^{12}. Bytes: the weights, read once, 2\times10^9. Intensity =512, above the ridge of 100. Compute time =1.024\times10^{12}/10^{14}=10.24 ms, memory time =2\times10^9/10^{12}=2.0 ms. Compute-bound, 10.24 ms: 50 000 prompt tokens per second.

Decode, one sequence (B = 1), context 512. Operations: 2P=2\times10^9. Bytes: weights plus the cache read, 2\times10^9+512\times65\,536=2\,033\,554\,432. Intensity =0.98, far below 100. Compute time 0.02 ms, memory time 2.0336 ms: memory-bound, about 100 times slower than the arithmetic alone would allow, and 491.7 tokens per second.

Batching B sequences. The weights are fetched once for all of them, so operations scale by B but the weight bytes do not. At B = 32, context 512 each: operations 6.4\times10^{10}, bytes 2\times10^9+32\times512\times65\,536=3\,073\,741\,824; memory time 3.0737 ms, compute 0.64 ms, still memory-bound but now 32 tokens per 3.0737 ms = 10 411 tokens per second. At B = 128: bytes 6.295×10⁹, 6.295 ms per step, 20 334 tokens per second. Throughput rose 41-fold for a 3-fold longer step. The cache bytes grow with B, so they eventually dominate and the gain flattens; each user's own latency per token rose from 2.03 ms to 6.29 ms. That is the latency versus throughput trade.

Continuous batching. Requests finish at different times. If the server waits for the longest request in a batch before accepting new ones (static batching), slots sit idle. Continuous (or in-flight) batching refills a slot the moment its request ends. Example: four requests need 3, 8, 2 and 5 tokens, with two slots. Static: groups (3, 8) and (2, 5) take 8 + 5 = 13 steps; 18 useful tokens out of 26 slot-steps, 69.2%. Continuous: slot 1 runs request 1 (steps 1 to 3), then request 3 (steps 4 to 5), then request 4 (steps 6 to 10); slot 2 runs request 2 (steps 1 to 8). It takes 10 steps; 18 of 20 slot-steps, 90%.

The paged KV cache. The cache of keys and values (see the transformer) grows by one entry per generated token. A naive server reserves the maximum length per request. With a 2 048-token reservation, the toy cache needs 2048\times65\,536=128 MiB per request even if the request ends at 300 tokens. A paged cache hands out small blocks (say 16 tokens) as needed: 300 tokens need \lceil300/16\rceil=19 blocks, 304 token slots, 19 MiB, and at most 15 slots of waste. The same memory then holds many more concurrent requests, which is what raises batch size and throughput.

The formal picture

I=\frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{bytes moved}},\qquad t_{\text{phase}}=\max\!\left(\frac{\text{FLOPs}}{F_{\text{peak}}},\ \frac{\text{bytes}}{\text{BW}}\right),\qquad I_{\text{ridge}}=\frac{F_{\text{peak}}}{\text{BW}}
\text{prefill: }I\approx T,\qquad \text{decode step: }I\approx\frac{2PB}{2P+B\,c\,\kappa}\le B,\qquad \text{throughput}=\frac{B}{t_{\text{step}}}
Symbols. F_{\text{peak}}: peak arithmetic rate; BW: memory bandwidth; both are the round numbers of the toy, not a real device. P: parameters, each stored in 2 bytes. T: prompt tokens processed together. B: sequences decoded together. c: context length per sequence; \kappa: cache bytes per token (65 536 in the toy). t_{\text{step}}: time for one decode step. The approximations assume the weights dominate bytes moved and ignore activations and communication.

Common traps
  • "A faster chip makes decoding proportionally faster." Decoding is limited by memory bandwidth, so extra arithmetic helps only after the batch is large enough to cross the ridge.
  • Quoting batch throughput as latency. Large batches serve more tokens per second overall, but each user waits for a longer step.
  • Forgetting the cache. The weights are fetched once per step, but the cache is fetched per sequence; for long contexts it can exceed the weights.
  • Treating the toy numbers as facts. The chip and model above are round numbers chosen for easy arithmetic. The method is real; the figures are not a benchmark.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Inference servers. Continuous batching, paged caches and scheduling of prefill against decode are core features of systems that serve language models.
  • Why quantisation speeds decoding. Fewer bytes per weight means shorter memory time for the same arithmetic (lesson 5.2), which is the dominant term at small batch.
  • Why speculative decoding works. It turns several sequential decode steps into one pass that resembles a short prefill (next lesson).

Check yourself

1. With the toy chip, at what batch size would decoding reach the ridge if the cache bytes were zero?
Intensity is 2PB/2P=B, and the ridge is 100, so at B = 100. With a cache the byte count is larger and the crossing needs a bigger batch, or it never arrives.
2. Prefill for T = 2048 on the same toy: compute or memory bound, and how long?
Operations 2\times10^9\times2048=4.096\times10^{12}, intensity 2048, compute time 40.96 ms; memory time 2 ms. Compute-bound, 40.96 ms.
3. Why can continuous batching never be slower than static batching on the same requests in this model?
Each step costs the same regardless of which slots are busy. Static batching leaves slots empty; continuous batching only ever fills them with work that static batching would run later, so it finishes the same set of tokens in no more steps.

Takeaways

  • Prefill has intensity about T (compute-bound); one decode step has intensity at most B (memory-bound for small B).
  • Batching shares the weight fetch among sequences, raising throughput at the cost of per-user latency; the cache grows with every sequence.
  • Continuous batching and paged caches keep batches full and memory unwasted.
5.4

Speculative decoding

The questionDecoding is stuck at one token per pass of the big model. Could a small, fast model guess the next several tokens, and the big model check all the guesses in one pass, without ever changing what the big model would have said? How would that be possible, and how many tokens per pass do you get?

Intuition: draft and proofread

A junior writes four words of a sentence quickly; a senior reads the four words together in one glance and either initials each or crosses out from the first mistake. Checking four words at once costs the senior about the same as writing one (lesson 5.3: a pass is dominated by loading weights, and checking four positions in parallel costs little more). The senior's rule must be designed so that the final text is exactly what the senior alone would have produced, not a blend of the two.

Build it up

Start with one position and a 3-token vocabulary. The target model (big, trusted) has distribution p; the draft model (small) has distribution q. The draft samples a token x\sim q. The target then does a random accept-or-reject:

  • Accept x with probability \min\!\big(1,\ p(x)/q(x)\big).
  • If rejected, draw a replacement from the residual distribution, proportional to \max(0,\,p(\cdot)-q(\cdot)).
Worked example: proving the rule gives exactly p

Tokens A, B, C. Target p=(0.5,\,0.3,\,0.2), draft q=(0.3,\,0.2,\,0.5).

Chance of proposing and accepting each token is q(x)\min(1,p(x)/q(x))=\min(p(x),q(x)): for A, 0.3\times\min(1,1.667)=0.3; for B, 0.2\times\min(1,1.5)=0.2; for C, 0.5\times\min(1,0.4)=0.2. Total acceptance probability \alpha=0.3+0.2+0.2=0.7.

Rejection happens with probability 1-\alpha=0.3. The residual is \max(0,p-q)=(0.2,\,0.1,\,0), which sums to 0.3 (the same 0.3, as it must), so the normalised residual is (2/3,\ 1/3,\ 0).

Probability that the output is each token = accepted proposal + rejection then resample:

tokenacceptedrejected, then pickedtotaltarget p
A0.30.3 × 2/3 = 0.20.50.5
B0.20.3 × 1/3 = 0.10.30.3
C0.20.3 × 0 = 00.20.2

The output is exactly p. The general proof is the same three lines: output probability of x is \min(p,q)(x)+(1-\alpha)\cdot\dfrac{\max(0,p-q)(x)}{1-\alpha}=\min(p,q)(x)+\max(0,p(x)-q(x))=p(x), because \sum_x\max(0,p-q)=1-\alpha and \min(a,b)+\max(0,a-b)=a.

How many tokens per target pass? Draft k tokens. The target scores all k positions (plus one extra) in one pass. Walk left to right: each draft is accepted with probability \alpha (here the same at every position); the first rejection ends the step with one replacement token; if all k are accepted the target adds one bonus token from its own distribution. So the step yields j+1 tokens where j is the number accepted, and P(j\ge m)=\alpha^m. Expected tokens =\sum_{m=0}^{k}\alpha^m=(1-\alpha^{k+1})/(1-\alpha). With \alpha=0.7,k=4: 1+0.7+0.49+0.343+0.2401=2.7731. With k=1: 1.7. With k=8: 3.1988. The limit as k\to\infty is 1/(1-\alpha)=3.333.

Speed-up. Suppose one draft step costs a fraction c=0.1 of a target step. A speculative step then costs 1+ck=1.4 target-steps for 2.7731 tokens, a speed-up of 2.7731/1.4=1.981 times.

The formal picture

P(\text{accept }x)=\min\!\Big(1,\tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}\Big),\quad \alpha=\sum_x\min\big(p(x),q(x)\big),\quad p_{\text{res}}(x)=\frac{\max(0,\,p(x)-q(x))}{1-\alpha}
\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per step}]=\frac{1-\alpha^{k+1}}{1-\alpha},\qquad \text{speed-up}=\frac{1-\alpha^{k+1}}{(1-\alpha)(1+ck)}
Symbols. p(x),q(x): target and draft probabilities for token x in the current context. \alpha: the acceptance probability for one drafted token; it equals one minus the total-variation distance between p and q, so a draft close to the target accepts often. k: the number of drafted tokens. c: cost of a draft step relative to a target pass. The formula for tokens per step assumes the same \alpha at every position; in real text \alpha varies from token to token and the formula is then an approximation using the average. If a draft token has q(x)=0 it is never proposed, and if q(x)>p(x) the token is accepted only part of the time. What the method guarantees is exactness: the output distribution equals the target's. It does not guarantee a speed-up: if \alpha is small, or the draft is not cheap, it can be slower.

Common traps
  • "It approximates the big model." No: with the accept and resample rule the output distribution is exactly the target's. If you instead keep the draft token whenever it looks plausible, you get an approximation without that guarantee.
  • Dropping the residual step. Resampling from p instead of the residual on rejection biases the output toward tokens the draft likes. The residual is what makes the algebra close.
  • Assuming a bigger k always helps. Each extra token has to survive all earlier acceptances (probability \alpha^m), while every extra draft step costs time.
  • Forgetting sampling temperature. Both p and q must be the distributions actually used for sampling (after temperature or top-p), or exactness is lost.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Faster decoding. A smaller model in the same family, or extra prediction heads on the target itself, can serve as the drafter; the verification step is a parallel pass like a short prefill (lesson 5.3).
  • Why it fits the hardware. Decoding is memory-bound, so scoring k+1 positions in one pass costs little more than scoring one.
  • Distillation helps. A draft trained to imitate the target (lesson 5.5) has a higher \alpha.

Check yourself

1. p = (0.6, 0.4) and q = (0.4, 0.6). Find α and the residual distribution.
\alpha=\min(0.6,0.4)+\min(0.4,0.6)=0.8. The residual is \max(0,p-q)=(0.2,0), sum 0.2 = 1−α, normalised (1,0): on rejection the output is always the first token. Check: output of token 1 = 0.4 + 0.2 = 0.6, token 2 = 0.4 + 0 = 0.4.
2. With α = 0.5 and k = 3, how many tokens per step on average?
1+0.5+0.25+0.125=1.875 (formula: (1-0.5^4)/0.5=1.875).
3. If the draft has c = 0.5 and α = 0.5 with k = 3, is it worth it?
Speed-up =1.875/(1+0.5\times3)=1.875/2.5=0.75: slower than plain decoding. A draft that is half the cost of the target is too expensive for that acceptance rate.

Takeaways

  • Accept with probability \min(1,p/q), else resample from \max(0,p-q) renormalised: the output is exactly the target distribution.
  • Expected tokens per target pass: (1-\alpha^{k+1})/(1-\alpha), with \alpha=\sum\min(p,q).
  • The speed-up depends on how cheap and how accurate the draft is; exactness never depends on either.
5.5

Distillation

The questionA large teacher model answers well but is expensive. You want a small student that behaves like it. Training the student on the correct labels alone throws away something the teacher knows: which wrong answers are nearly right. How can you pass that on, and what does temperature have to do with it?

Intuition: a teacher's grades beat a hard answer key

An answer key says "the answer is B". A teacher's marking says "B, but C was a reasonable mistake and D was absurd". The second carries more information per question. A model's output probabilities are that marking: 0.84 for one token, 0.11 for a plausible alternative, 0.006 for nonsense. Training a student to copy the whole distribution (soft targets) teaches the similarity structure between answers. The wrinkle is that a confident teacher gives almost all the probability to one token, so the small values are tiny and carry little gradient. A temperature divides the logits before the softmax and flattens the teacher's distribution so those small probabilities become visible.

Build it up

Worked example: a 4-token teacher at three temperatures

Teacher logits z=(4,\,2,\,1,\,-1). The softmax at temperature T is p_i=e^{z_i/T}/\sum_je^{z_j/T}.

Ttoken 1token 2token 3token 4

At T=1 (sum of exponentials: 54.598 + 7.389 + 2.718 + 0.368 = 65.073): 54.598/65.073 = 0.8390. The ratio between tokens 2 and 3 is 0.1135/0.0418 = 2.72; the student should learn that too, and at T = 1 this signal is buried under a 0.84 that dominates.

The loss. A student starting from equal logits (all 0, so uniform 0.25 each) has KL(teacher ‖ student) of 0.8301 nats at T=1 and 0.3290 at T=2. A student with logits (3,\,2.5,\,0,\,0) has probabilities (0.5861, 0.3555, 0.0292, 0.0292) at T=1, and the teacher-to-student KL is 0.1771; at T=2 the KL is 0.0711. This student has token 2 too high and has not learnt that token 3 should beat token 4.

The gradient is simple. For the loss T^2\,\mathrm{KL}(p_T\,\|\,q_T) the gradient with respect to a student logit z_i is T\,(q_{T,i}-p_{T,i}): push each logit up when the student's tempered probability is below the teacher's, down when above. The factor T^2 compensates for the 1/T that the temperature puts into every gradient, so the soft loss keeps a comparable size at different temperatures.

Hard labels only. Training the student on just the teacher's top token (cross-entropy with a one-hot target) pushes its probability toward 1 for token 1 and everything else toward 0, so it never learns that token 2 is more plausible than token 4. The lab shows that the KL to the teacher then stays large while the student keeps getting "more confident".

Distilling from generated data. For language models the student is often trained on text generated by the teacher (sequence-level distillation), either keeping the teacher's full next-token distributions at each position or just the sampled tokens. The first gives a richer signal per token; the second is much cheaper to store and works even when only the teacher's text, not its probabilities, is available. The student is then bounded by what the teacher's samples contain, including its mistakes. In practice a mix of the soft loss and the ordinary loss on real data is common.

The formal picture

p_{T,i}=\frac{e^{z^{(t)}_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z^{(t)}_j/T}},\quad q_{T,i}=\frac{e^{z^{(s)}_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z^{(s)}_j/T}},\quad \mathcal{L}=T^2\,\mathrm{KL}(p_T\,\|\,q_T)=T^2\sum_i p_{T,i}\ln\frac{p_{T,i}}{q_{T,i}}
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial z^{(s)}_i}=T\,(q_{T,i}-p_{T,i}),\qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\lambda\,\mathcal{L}+(1-\lambda)\,\mathrm{CE}(y,\,q_1)
Symbols. z^{(t)},z^{(s)}: teacher and student logits. T: temperature (1 is the ordinary softmax; larger is flatter). p_T,q_T: tempered distributions. \mathrm{KL}: the Kullback-Leibler divergence from cross-entropy and KL, zero only when the two distributions are equal. y: the true label, q_1: the student at T=1, and \lambda: how much weight the soft loss gets when combined with the ordinary cross-entropy. When a student is deployed it is used at T=1; the temperature only shapes training.
Try this
  1. With the defaults the student bars (filled) climb toward the teacher outlines and the KL curve falls toward 0. Check the final ratio of token 2 to token 3 in the readout: the student matches the teacher's 2.72.
  2. Change "Train on" to the hard label only and press Reset. The student piles onto token 1 and the KL to the teacher stops falling.
  3. Return to soft targets and try T = 1 versus T = 4. Higher T lets the student see the small probabilities, but a student that can match the teacher at T = 4 also matches it at T = 1 here, because this toy student is a free logit vector. A real student with less capacity will only approximate.
Common traps
  • Forgetting the T^2. Without it, the gradient shrinks like 1/T and a higher temperature silently reduces the effective learning rate.
  • Deploying at the training temperature. The temperature is a training device; serve the student at T=1 (or whatever sampling setting you want), not at the distillation temperature.
  • Assuming the student can exceed the teacher. It copies the teacher's behaviour, errors included; extra gains usually come from the data or from combining with real labels.
  • Confusing it with quantisation or pruning. Those shrink the same network; distillation trains a different, smaller one.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Small and fast models. A student can serve cheaply on modest hardware, and as a drafter for speculative decoding (lesson 5.4). See compression for vision for the image-model version.
  • Training on teacher-generated text. Synthetic data from a stronger model is a common way to build instruction data; its licence terms and the risk of copying the teacher's errors need checking.
  • Preference and reward signals. Distillation is also used to compress a slow reward or verifier into a fast one (see Module 4).

Check yourself

1. Teacher logits (2, 0). Compute p at T = 1 and T = 2.
T = 1: e^2=7.389, e^0=1, so (0.8808, 0.1192). T = 2: logits become (1, 0): (2.718, 1)/3.718 = (0.7311, 0.2689). The higher temperature flattens it.
2. The student has tempered probabilities (0.5, 0.5) and the teacher (0.7311, 0.2689) at T = 2. Which way do the student's logits move?
The gradient is T(q-p)=2\times(-0.2311,\,+0.2311). Gradient descent subtracts it, so logit 1 goes up and logit 2 goes down.
3. Why can hard labels not reach zero KL against a soft teacher?
A one-hot target drives the student to put all mass on one token, but the teacher puts mass on others; KL(teacher ‖ student) is then large (it grows as the student's probability on a token the teacher uses goes to zero).

Takeaways

  • Distillation trains the student on the teacher's full output distribution, which carries the similarity between wrong answers.
  • Temperature flattens the distributions so small probabilities become learnable; the T^2 factor keeps the loss scale stable.
  • For language models the teacher's generated text is a common training source; the student inherits the teacher's behaviour and its mistakes.
5.6

Mixture of experts

The questionModel quality tends to grow with the number of parameters, but so does the arithmetic per token. Can a model hold far more parameters than it uses on any one token? And if each token chooses its own parameters, what stops every token from choosing the same ones?

Intuition: a hospital, not a single doctor

A hospital has many specialists. A patient is routed to the one or two who fit, and the hospital as a whole knows far more than any one visit uses. A mixture of experts (MoE) layer replaces the single feed-forward block of a transformer with several parallel blocks, the experts, and a small router that looks at each token and picks the top few. The model stores all experts but computes only the chosen ones. The risk: the router could send everyone to the same popular specialist, leaving the others untrained and one worker swamped. A load-balancing loss nudges the router toward spreading tokens around.

Build it up

Worked example: total versus active parameters

One layer with model width d=512. Attention: 4d^2=1\,048\,576. One expert (two matrices d\times4d and 4d\times d): 8d^2=2\,097\,152. With E=8 experts and a router (one weight vector of length d per expert, dE=4\,096):

Total = 1 048 576 + 8 × 2 097 152 + 4 096 = 17 829 888. Active per token with k=2 experts = 1 048 576 + 2 × 2 097 152 + 4 096 = 5 246 976, which is 29.4% of the total. The arithmetic per token is that of a 5.2-million-parameter layer, but all 17.8 million numbers must sit in memory (and under the memory-bound regime of lesson 5.3 that matters).

Worked example: routing eight tokens among four experts

The router computes one logit per expert from the token, then a softmax. Eight tokens, four experts:

Top-2 combine. For token 1, the top two are E1 (0.757) and E2 (0.102); renormalised, the gate weights are 0.757/0.859 = 0.8808 and 0.102/0.859 = 0.1192. The layer output is 0.8808 × (E1's output) + 0.1192 × (E2's output).

Load. Top-1 counts are E1: 5, E2: 1, E3: 1, E4: 1, so the dispatch fractions are f=(0.625,0.125,0.125,0.125). The mean router probabilities are P=(0.4679,\,0.2090,\,0.1617,\,0.1614). The balancing loss E\sum_if_iP_i=4\,(0.625\times0.4679+0.125\times0.2090+0.125\times0.1617+0.125\times0.1614)=1.4358. If every expert got exactly 1/4 of the tokens and probability, the loss would be 4\times4\times0.25\times0.25=1, the minimum. Ordinarily this term is added to the main loss with a small weight; the gradient lowers the router's probability for the overloaded expert and raises it for the neglected ones.

Practical issues. Each expert has a capacity, a cap on tokens per batch; tokens beyond it may be dropped or sent to a second choice. Experts live on different devices, so tokens must be shipped to them and back, which costs communication. At small batch sizes only a few experts are touched at a time, but across a batch many experts are used, so the memory traffic of loading their weights is higher than the active-parameter count suggests.

The formal picture

y=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{TopK}(g)} \tilde g_i\,\mathrm{Expert}_i(x),\qquad g=\operatorname{softmax}(W_r x),\qquad \tilde g_i=\frac{g_i}{\sum_{j\in\mathrm{TopK}}g_j}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{bal}}=E\sum_{i=1}^{E}f_i\,P_i,\quad f_i=\frac{\#\{\text{dispatches to }i\}}{kN},\quad P_i=\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^N g_i(x_t),\qquad N_{\text{total}}=N_{\text{shared}}+E\,N_{\text{expert}},\ \ N_{\text{active}}=N_{\text{shared}}+k\,N_{\text{expert}}
Symbols. x: a token's vector; W_r: the router's weight matrix (one row per expert); g: router probabilities; TopK: the k experts with the largest g_i; \tilde g_i: the gate weights renormalised over those experts. N: tokens in the batch; f_i: the fraction of dispatches that went to expert i (counted, so not differentiable); P_i: its mean router probability (differentiable, so it carries the gradient). E: number of experts. The minimum of \mathcal{L}_{\text{bal}} is 1, reached when both f and P are uniform. N_{\text{shared}}: parameters every token uses (attention, embeddings, router); N_{\text{expert}}: parameters in one expert.

Common traps
  • "Active parameters are the model size." The memory you must hold, and the files you must ship, are the total. Active parameters set the arithmetic per token, not the footprint.
  • Quoting a single number for both. A statement of "a model with N parameters" for an MoE should say which of the two is meant.
  • Balancing as a free lunch. A strong balancing weight can force tokens to experts that fit them poorly, and a weak one allows collapse; it is a tuned trade-off.
  • Assuming experts are neat topics. Experts specialise in ways that are often hard to label; they are not guaranteed to be "the maths expert".
Where it shows up in AI
  • Large sparse models. Many published large language models use MoE layers in place of dense feed-forward layers to raise capacity at a given per-token compute.
  • Serving cost. All experts must be resident, so MoE trades memory for arithmetic; combined with quantisation (5.2) and batching (5.3) it shapes how such models are deployed.
  • Relation to attention. The attention layers of the transformer (see Module 2) are usually dense and shared; the experts replace only the feed-forward part.

Check yourself

1. d = 256, E = 16, k = 2, expert = 8d², attention 4d², router dE. Total and active?
d^2=65\,536. Attention 262 144; expert 524 288; router 4 096. Total = 262 144 + 16×524 288 + 4 096 = 8 654 848. Active = 262 144 + 2×524 288 + 4 096 = 1 314 816.
2. Four experts, all tokens dispatched to expert 1 and all mean probability on expert 1 (f = (1,0,0,0), P = (1,0,0,0)). Balancing loss?
E\sum f_iP_i=4\times(1\times1)=4, the maximum for E = 4 and four times the balanced minimum of 1.
3. Why is f not used directly to carry the gradient, and what does?
f comes from counting hard top-k choices, which has no gradient. P is the average of the smooth softmax probabilities, so the gradient flows through P while f acts as the weights (what the current loads are).

Takeaways

  • An MoE layer stores E experts but a token uses only k: total parameters grow with E, active parameters with k.
  • The router picks the top-k experts by softmax score and combines their outputs with renormalised gate weights.
  • A load-balancing loss E\sum f_iP_i (minimum 1) prevents routing collapse, at the price of a tuned trade-off.
