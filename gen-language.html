Module 1 · Language modelling & tokenisation

A language model assigns a probability to the next piece of text. This module builds that idea from the humblest possible tool, counting, and climbs to the neural version that sits at the end of every large language model. It covers the first three stages of the generative AI pipeline: collect and tokenise (cutting text into pieces), pretrain (learn next-piece probabilities, measured by perplexity), and the generate step (choosing a word from those probabilities).

1.1

N-gram language models

The questionA phone keyboard has just seen you type "the dog" and must suggest the next word. It has never been taught grammar. All it has is a pile of past text. How can it turn that pile into "ate is unlikely, sat is likely", and what should it do about a word it has never seen follow "dog" at all?

Intuition: finish the sentence by remembering who followed whom

Imagine you could read every sentence ever written. To guess the word after "the dog" you would look through all of them for the places where "dog" appears and note what came next: "sat" 40 times, "barked" 30 times, "ate" 2 times. The share of each is a sensible probability. A language model is exactly this: a rule that, given what has been written so far, gives a probability to every possible next token. The simplest one just counts.

Counting has a limit: the full history ("the dog that my neighbour bought last spring") is so specific that it almost never repeats in the pile. So we cheat and remember only a short tail of it. An n-gram model conditions on the previous n-1 words: a bigram model looks at 1 word, a trigram model at 2. The price of forgetting is that the model cannot see long-range structure; the reward is that counting becomes possible.

Build it up

We need some bookkeeping. Put a start marker \langle s\rangle before each sentence and an end marker \langle/s\rangle after it, so the model also learns how sentences begin and end. The vocabulary is the set of tokens the model may predict (here whole words plus the end marker).

Worked example: three sentences

The whole training text:

the cat sat on the mat
the cat ate the fish
the dog sat on the rug

The vocabulary has V=10 items: the, cat, sat, on, mat, ate, fish, dog, rug and the end marker. The text contains 20 adjacent pairs (7 + 6 + 7). The pair counts, written as "previous → next: count":

previousnext words and countsrow total
<s>the: 33
thecat: 2, mat: 1, fish: 1, dog: 1, rug: 16
catsat: 1, ate: 12
saton: 22
onthe: 22
mat, fish, rug</s>: 1 each1 each
ate, dogthe: 1 (after ate), sat: 1 (after dog)1 each

Counts to probabilities. Divide a cell by its row total. P(\text{cat}\mid\text{the})=2/6=0.3333, P(\text{sat}\mid\text{cat})=1/2. Each row of probabilities adds to 1.

A whole sentence. By the chain rule (see the chain rule lesson) we multiply the steps: P(\text{the cat sat on the mat})=1\cdot\tfrac{2}{6}\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1\cdot1\cdot\tfrac16\cdot1=\tfrac1{36}=0.0278 (the last factor is P(\langle/s\rangle\mid\text{mat})=1).

The sparsity problem. What is P(\text{ate}\mid\text{dog})? The word "dog" was followed only by "sat", so the count is 0 and the probability is exactly 0. Then the perfectly sensible sentence "the dog ate the fish" gets probability 0, because one factor is zero. The table has 100 cells (10 possible previous tokens times 10 next tokens) but only 15 are non-zero: most perfectly good pairs were simply never observed.

The fix: add-k smoothing. Pretend that every possible pair was seen k extra times. For k=1: P(\text{ate}\mid\text{dog})=\dfrac{0+1}{1+1\cdot10}=\dfrac1{11}=0.0909. The row still adds to 1: sat gets (1+1)/11=2/11 and the other nine words get 1/11 each, so 2/11+9/11=1. The cost: seen words lose some probability, for example P(\text{fish}\mid\text{the}) falls from 1/6=0.1667 to (1+1)/(6+10)=0.125.

The formal definition

P(w_i\mid w_{i-1})\;=\;\frac{\operatorname{count}(w_{i-1},w_i)+k}{\operatorname{count}(w_{i-1})+k\,V},\qquad P(w_1,\dots,w_m)\;\approx\;\prod_{i=1}^{m}P(w_i\mid w_{i-1})
Symbols. w_i: the i-th token. \operatorname{count}(a,b): how many times b directly followed a in the training text. \operatorname{count}(a): how many pairs start with a (the row total). V: vocabulary size (10 above). k\ge0: the smoothing constant; k=0 is plain counting. The right-hand side is the bigram approximation of the chain rule: the true next-word probability depends on the whole history, and we keep only the last word. A trigram model conditions on two words and is written with w_{i-2},w_{i-1}.

The scale of the problem: with a vocabulary of 50 000 tokens a bigram table has 50000^2=2.5\times10^{9} cells and a trigram table 50000^3=1.25\times10^{14}. No text collection is large enough to fill them, which is the deep reason that a model that generalises from similar words (lesson 1.5) replaced counting tables.

Common traps
  • Zero probability is a claim, not a gap. Without smoothing, one unseen pair makes a whole sentence impossible. A model that says "impossible" about a normal sentence will be scored as infinitely bad (see perplexity next).
  • Add-k is blunt. With a huge vocabulary, k = 1 hands a lot of probability to words that are never plausible. Better schemes exist (back-off, interpolation, Kneser–Ney); the point here is the idea, not the best smoother.
  • Forgetting the end marker. Without \langle/s\rangle the model never learns when to stop, and the probabilities of sentences of different lengths cannot be compared.
  • "More n is always better." A larger n sees more context but its counts get smaller and sparser. n-grams trade context for reliability.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Baselines and keyboards. Word suggestion on small devices, spelling correctors and old speech recognisers used n-gram tables (often with back-off), and they remain a fast, interpretable baseline to beat.
  • Counting is the training objective in disguise. A neural language model (lesson 1.5) is trained to reproduce these same conditional frequencies, but through a shared set of weights, so it can give sensible probability to a pair it never saw.
  • Smoothing reappears as label smoothing and as the temperature and truncation tricks of lesson 1.6.

Check yourself

1. In the three-sentence text, what are P(sat | the) and P(on | sat), unsmoothed?
"sat" never directly follows "the" (the is followed by cat, mat, fish, dog, rug), so P(\text{sat}\mid\text{the})=0/6=0. "on" follows "sat" both times that "sat" appears, so P(\text{on}\mid\text{sat})=2/2=1.
2. With k = 1, what is P(cat | the) and does the row for "the" still add to 1?
P( ext{cat}mid ext{the})=(2+1)/(6+10)=3/16=0.1875. The row has 10 entries: cat gives 3/16; mat, fish, dog and rug (count 1 each) give 2/16 each, 8/16 together; the five words with count 0 (the, sat, on, ate and the end marker) give 1/16 each, 5/16 together. Total: 3/16 + 8/16 + 5/16 = 16/16 = 1.
3. Why can a bigram model not get the verb right in "The keys to the cabinet ___" (are or is)?
Its prediction depends only on the previous word, "cabinet", which is singular. The plural subject "keys" is four words back and invisible to it, so it tends to prefer "is". Agreement across a distance needs a model with a longer, learned memory (a larger n helps only if that exact long phrase was seen in the training text, which almost never happens).

Takeaways

  • A language model gives a probability to the next token given the past; the simplest one counts pairs and divides by the row total.
  • Unseen pairs get probability 0 and ruin whole sentences; add-k smoothing reserves probability for them: (\text{count}+k)/(\text{total}+kV).
  • Tables explode with n and V (V^n cells), which is why learned, shared-weight models took over.

Perplexity

The questionYou have two language models and one test sentence. Model A gives the sentence probability 0.0001, model B gives 0.00002. But the sentence is short; on a longer one both would give astronomically small numbers. How do you score a model on a whole test text so that the number does not depend on how long the text is, and can be read as "how confused is the model"?

Intuition: the effective number of choices

Suppose a model is completely unsure and gives each of 8 tokens probability 1/8 at every step. It is as confused as someone choosing among 8 equally likely options. Suppose instead it always gives the true next token probability 1/2: it is as confused as a coin flip. We want one number that says "this model behaves as if it were choosing among about this many equally likely tokens at each step". That number is the perplexity: 8 in the first case, 2 in the second, 1 for a model that is always certain and right.

To make a score that is independent of length we average the per-token surprise. The surprise of a token the model gave probability p is -\log p: a certain token (p = 1) costs 0, a one-in-a-million token costs a lot. This is the same quantity as the cross-entropy loss of the information theory module, taken per token.

Build it up

Worked example 1: four tokens, by hand

A model gives the four tokens of a test text the probabilities 0.5,\ 0.25,\ 0.125,\ 0.5. Use logarithms to base 2, so the unit is the bit. Losses: -\log_2 0.5=1, -\log_2 0.25=2, -\log_2 0.125=3, -\log_2 0.5=1 bits. The mean loss is (1+2+3+1)/4=1.75 bits. The perplexity is 2^{1.75}=3.3636.

Same answer in nats. With the natural logarithm the unit is the nat; each loss is \ln 2=0.6931 times larger, so the mean is 1.75\times0.6931=1.2130 nats, and the perplexity is e^{1.2130}=3.3636. Perplexity is the same in either base, as long as the exponential matches the logarithm (2 with bits, e with nats).

Sanity check: a model that gives every one of 8 tokens probability 1/8 has loss 3 bits and perplexity 2^3=8.

Worked example 2: the bigram model of lesson 1.1

Score the sentence "the dog ate the fish" (plus the end marker, 6 predictions) with the add-1 bigram model built from the three training sentences, log base 2:

Mean loss = 16.0788 / 6 = 2.6798 bits, perplexity 2^{2.6798}=6.4076. The unigram model (no context, add-1) gives a mean loss of 3.1378 bits and perplexity 8.8016 on the same text. The bigram model is the better predictor: it is "as unsure as a 6.41-sided die" versus an 8.80-sided one.

Bits per character. The text has 20 characters (including spaces). The total cost is 16.0788 bits, so 16.0788/20=0.804 bits per character. Per-character scores are what you compare when two models use different tokenisers, because per-token perplexities count different units.

The formal definition

\mathrm{PP}\;=\;\exp\!\Big(\underbrace{-\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^{N}\ln P(w_t\mid w_{<t})}_{\text{mean cross-entropy in nats}}\Big)\;=\;2^{\,-\frac1N\sum_{t}\log_2 P(w_t\mid w_{<t})}\;=\;\Big(\prod_{t=1}^{N}\frac{1}{P(w_t\mid w_{<t})}\Big)^{1/N}
Symbols. N: the number of tokens scored. w_t: the t-th token of the test text. w_{<t}: all tokens before it (for a bigram model, just the previous one). P(w_t\mid w_{<t}): the probability the model gave the token that really occurred. The braced term is the average cross-entropy between the data and the model (cross-entropy). The three forms are equal; the last one reads "the geometric mean of the inverse probabilities". The perplexity is at least 1 and equals the vocabulary size for a model that guesses uniformly.
Try this
  1. Keep "the dog ate the fish", base 2, k = 1, bigram. Press play and read the bars: "ate" after "dog" is the costliest token (3.459 bits). The final perplexity should read 6.408.
  2. Switch the base to nats. The bar heights shrink by a factor 0.693, the loss line moves down, but the perplexity is still 6.408. Then switch to the unigram model and confirm 8.80.
  3. Type a sentence the training text strongly supports ("the cat sat on the mat") and one with an unseen word ("the bird sat"). Lower k to 0.05 and watch how unseen events are punished: with little smoothing a surprising token costs many bits.
Common traps
  • Mixing bases. A mean loss of 2.68 means perplexity 2^{2.68} only if the logarithm was base 2. Computing e^{2.68} from a base-2 loss gives a wrong number. State the base every time.
  • Comparing perplexities across tokenisers. A model with a bigger vocabulary predicts bigger pieces, so its per-token perplexity is not comparable to a character model's. Compare bits per character (or per byte) on the same text instead.
  • Tuning on the test text. Choosing k to minimise the perplexity of the very sentence you report (k = 0.1 gives 3.89 here) is cheating. Choose k on separate validation text, then score the test text once.
  • Lower perplexity is not always better text. It measures how well the model predicts real text, not whether its output is useful, safe or truthful. A model can have low perplexity and still be unhelpful.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Evaluating pretraining. The validation loss curve that practitioners watch during pretraining is exactly this average cross-entropy; perplexity is its exponential, and bits per byte is the tokeniser-independent version (see the training module).
  • Model comparison on a fixed held-out text is how language models were ranked before task benchmarks, and it remains a quick sanity check.
  • Data quality filters. A small language model's perplexity on a document is used by some data pipelines as a signal of how "ordinary" the text is.

Check yourself

1. A model gives a 4-token text the probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/4, 1/2. What is its perplexity?
Losses in bits: 1, 2, 2, 1; mean 1.5 bits; perplexity 2^{1.5}=2.8284. In nats: 1.5\times0.6931=1.0397 and e^{1.0397}=2.8284, the same.
2. A model has a mean loss of 3 nats per token. What is the perplexity, and what would a vocabulary of that perplexity mean?
e^{3}=20.09. The model is as uncertain at each step as if it chose uniformly among about 20 tokens. (Writing 2^3=8 would be the mistake of using base 2 on a base-e loss.)
3. Why is the perplexity of a model that assigns probability 0 to one test token infinite?
The loss of that token is -\log 0=+\infty, so the mean loss is infinite and \exp(\infty)=\infty. This is why every real language model keeps every token at some small positive probability (smoothing in n-gram models, the softmax in neural ones).

Takeaways

  • Perplexity = exp of the mean per-token cross-entropy: "the effective number of equally likely choices". It is 1 for a perfect model and V for a uniform guess.
  • Always state the log base (bits with 2, nats with e); the perplexity itself does not depend on it.
  • Compare models on the same text, with the same tokeniser, or use bits per character; never tune on the test text.
1.3

Tokenisation: words, characters and subwords

The questionA model works on a fixed list of symbols called a vocabulary. If the symbols are whole words, the word "unfriendliness" or a new brand name is simply not in the list. If they are single characters, every sentence becomes very long. Is there a middle path that keeps the vocabulary small, can still write any word, and uses one symbol for common words?

Intuition: build the alphabet of your own text

Text must become numbers before a model can read it, and the first step is cutting it into pieces called tokens. There are three natural choices. Words: short sequences, but the vocabulary is huge and open-ended (every misspelling, name and rare word needs a slot, and unknown words become one useless "unknown" token). Characters: a tiny closed vocabulary that can spell anything, but sequences become 4–6 times longer and each symbol carries almost no meaning. Subwords: frequent words stay whole, rare words are spelled from pieces ("un", "friend", "li", "ness"). Subwords sit in the middle on both axes.

Byte-pair encoding (BPE) is the standard recipe for finding the pieces automatically from text. The idea is almost childishly simple: start with single characters, find the two symbols that appear next to each other most often, glue them into one new symbol, and repeat. Common letter sequences like "est" quickly become single symbols; frequent whole words follow.

Build it up

We need a word list with frequencies, and a mark for the end of each word so that the model can tell "est" inside a word from "est" at the end (we write the mark as an underscore, \_). Our training text:

Worked example: the merge algorithm on four words

Words with their counts: low ×5, lower ×2, newest ×6, widest ×3 (16 words). Start with characters: l o w _, l o w e r _, n e w e s t _, w i d e s t _. The 11 starting symbols are l, o, w, e, r, n, s, t, i, d and _.

Count every adjacent pair, weighted by word frequency. The largest: es appears in newest (6) and widest (3): 9; st 6 + 3 = 9; t_ 6 + 3 = 9; we in lower (2) and newest (6): 8; lo in low (5) and lower (2): 7; ow: 7.

Merge 1. Three pairs tie at 9. Our rule (and the lab's) is to break ties by sorting the pair names, so e + s → es. Now newest is n e w es t _ and widest is w i d es t _. Recount: es t is 9, t _ is 9. Merge 2: es + t → est. Merge 3: est + _ → est_ (9). Now the pair counts are lo 7, ow 7, ew 6, ne 6, w+est_ 6 ... Merge 4: l + o → lo (7, ties with ow and loses alphabetically). Merge 5: lo + w → low (7).

The full list of twelve merges, each with the count that won, in order: es 9, est 9, est_ 9, lo 7, low 7, ew 6, ewest_ 6, newest_ 6, low_ 5, dest_ 3, idest_ 3, widest_ 3. Written as pairs: (e,s) 9; (es,t) 9; (est,_) 9; (l,o) 7; (lo,w) 7; (e,w) 6; (ew,est_) 6; (n,ewest_) 6; (low,_) 5; (d,est_) 3; (i,dest_) 3; (w,idest_) 3.

The result after 12 merges: low becomes the single symbol low_, newest becomes newest_, widest becomes widest_, and lower is low e r _ (4 symbols). The vocabulary is 11 starting symbols plus 12 merges = 23.

Encoding a new word means replaying the merges in the learned order. "lowest": l o w e s t _ → (e,s) → (es,t) → (est,_) gives l o w est_ → (l,o) → (lo,w) gives low est_: 2 tokens for a word never seen in training. "newer" gives n ew e r _ (5 tokens), "wide" gives w i d e _ (5). Rare, unseen words are spelled with small pieces; common ones are cheap.

Byte-level fallback, vocabulary size and cost

Byte-level fallback. What if the text contains a character that was never in the training list, such as "é" or an emoji? A character-based BPE would have to emit an unknown token. A byte-level tokeniser works on the UTF-8 bytes of the text instead of on characters. There are only 256 possible byte values, so every possible text is spelled from a base alphabet of 256 symbols; merges then build bigger pieces on top. In the lab, "é" is two bytes (C3 A9 in hexadecimal) and appears as red byte tokens. Many systems use this design, so that nothing can ever be "unknown".

Vocabulary size trade-off. Each merge adds one symbol to the vocabulary and shortens the sequences. A larger vocabulary means fewer tokens per text (cheaper for the model to read) but a bigger embedding table and output layer (V\times d numbers each, see lesson 1.4) and rarer, less well-trained rows for the long-tail tokens. Practical vocabularies are commonly in the tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand; the right size depends on the data, languages and budget.

Tokens and cost. Models read and write tokens, so context limits, speed and price are all counted in tokens, not words or characters. If a tokeniser needs on average r tokens per word, a text of W words costs rW tokens. A language the tokeniser was trained on poorly might need twice as many tokens per word as one it fits well: the same message then costs twice as much to process and uses up the context twice as fast. The tokeniser is a design decision of the model, not a neutral pre-processing step.

The formal definition

\text{repeat }M\text{ times:}\quad (a^\star,b^\star)=\arg\max_{(a,b)}\ \sum_{\text{words }u}f_u\cdot\#_u(a,b),\qquad \text{replace every adjacent }(a^\star,b^\star)\text{ by the new symbol }a^\star b^\star
Symbols. (a,b): a pair of adjacent symbols currently in the corpus. f_u: how often word u occurs. \#_u(a,b): how many times the pair occurs inside the current spelling of word u (the end mark is a symbol too). M: the number of merges, a hyper-parameter; final vocabulary size = number of starting symbols + M. The merge list, in order, is the tokeniser: encoding replays it. Ties must be broken by a fixed rule so the result is reproducible.

  3. The text contains "café". The red tokens are the UTF-8 bytes of "é". Type an emoji or a word in another script: nothing is ever unknown.
Common traps
  • A token is not a word. Tokens can be whole words, pieces of words, single characters, or bytes; a leading space is often part of the token. Counting words to estimate token usage is only a rough guide.
  • Spelling and arithmetic effects. If "strawberry" is two tokens, the model never sees the letters directly; questions about individual letters or digits are harder because the tokeniser hides them.
  • Changing the tokeniser changes the model. The embedding table, the output layer and every learned weight assume this exact vocabulary and merge order. You cannot swap it afterwards.
  • Greedy is not "best". BPE minimises nothing global: it just repeats the most frequent pair. Other methods (unigram language-model tokenisation, WordPiece) choose pieces differently and give different splits.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Every language model starts with a trained tokeniser; the merge list ships with the model and is applied to every prompt.
  • Cost and context. Pricing, rate limits and context windows are in tokens; efficiency of the tokeniser across languages directly changes cost and quality for different users.
  • Beyond text. The same merging idea is used on image patches and audio codes (see the representing-data lesson in Module 0).

Check yourself

1. After the 12 merges, what is the vocabulary size, and what would it be after only 5 merges?
11 starting symbols plus one per merge: 11 + 12 = 23, and 11 + 5 = 16. (Each merge adds exactly one symbol; we count symbols still used, ignoring that a few early pieces such as "es" stop appearing once merged further.)
2. Before merge 4, what is the count of the pair (l, o), and why?
7: "lo" occurs once in each copy of "low" (5 copies) and once in each "lower" (2 copies), 5 + 2 = 7. The pair (o, w) is also 7; the tie is broken alphabetically ("lo" sorts before "ow").
3. How many tokens does "widest" need before any merge, and after all 12? Why is a byte fallback useful?
Before: w i d e s t _ = 7 symbols (the end mark counts). After 12 merges: one symbol, widest_. A byte fallback guarantees that any text can be encoded, even characters absent from the training data, because every character is some sequence of the 256 byte values; there is no "unknown" token to lose information.

Takeaways

  • BPE: start from characters (or bytes), repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair, record the merge order; encoding replays the merges.
  • Vocabulary size = base symbols + merges. More merges give shorter sequences but a bigger embedding and output table.
  • Byte-level fallback makes every text encodable. Costs and context limits are counted in tokens, so the tokeniser is part of the model's design.
1.4

Embeddings

The questionA neural network does arithmetic on numbers, but a token is an ID such as 7 or 31 204. The ID has no meaning: token 7 is not "between" 6 and 8. How do you turn a token into numbers so that tokens used in similar ways get similar numbers, and so that the network can learn what each token should mean?

Intuition: give every token a row in a table

The simplest honest encoding is the one-hot vector: for a vocabulary of V tokens, token number 3 becomes a vector of V zeros with a single 1 in position 3. It has the right shape for a network, but every pair of different tokens is equally far apart: "cat" is as unrelated to "kitten" as to "truck". Nothing to learn from.

Instead we keep a table with one row of d numbers per token, an embedding table, and let training adjust the numbers. Reading the row for a token is its embedding. Because gradient descent changes the rows to help the prediction task, tokens that are used in similar contexts end up with similar rows. The picture to hold: every token is an arrow in a space of d dimensions; similar tokens point the same way.

Build it up

Worked example: six words, two numbers each

Tokens (rows): cat, kitten, dog, puppy, car, truck. We set the 2-D embeddings by hand so the picture is clear (a trained model learns its own and they are rarely this readable):

catkittendogpuppycartruck
coordinate 10.90.80.90.8−0.7−0.8
coordinate 20.60.90.30.70.1−0.5

One-hot times a matrix is a lookup. Stack the rows into the 6×2 matrix E. The one-hot vector for "dog" is x=(0,0,1,0,0,0). The product xE is 0\cdot(0.9,0.6)+0\cdot(0.8,0.9)+1\cdot(0.9,0.3)+\dots=(0.9,\,0.3): exactly row 3. So the matrix product is never actually computed in practice; the program just reads row 3. The matrix form matters because it shows the lookup is a linear layer, so gradients flow into the table by the usual rules (lesson 1.5).

Similarity by the dot product. For two vectors u,v, u\cdot v=u_1v_1+u_2v_2. cat · kitten = 0.9·0.8 + 0.6·0.9 = 0.72 + 0.54 = 1.26. cat · car = 0.9·(−0.7) + 0.6·0.1 = −0.63 + 0.06 = −0.57: negative, pointing apart.

Cosine similarity divides out the lengths, so it measures only the angle. The lengths are |{\rm cat}|=\sqrt{0.81+0.36}=1.0817 and |{\rm kitten}|=\sqrt{0.64+0.81}=1.2042. So \cos=1.26/(1.0817\times1.2042)=0.9674. Other values: cat–dog 0.9648, cat–car −0.7452, cat–truck −0.9996 (nearly opposite), car–truck 0.7645.

Tying input and output embeddings. The last layer of a language model must score every vocabulary token against the current hidden vector h. If the output table equals the input table, the score of token i is simply E_i\cdot h. Take h=E_{\rm cat}=(0.9,0.6): scores for cat, kitten, dog, puppy, car, truck are 1.17, 1.26, 0.99, 1.14, −0.57, −1.02, and the softmax of these gives the probabilities 0.2388, 0.2613, 0.1995, 0.2318, 0.0419, 0.0267. Note that "kitten" outscores "cat" itself: raw dot products also reward length, a point to remember. Tying saves parameters: with V = 50 000 and d = 768 the table has 50000\times768=38{,}400{,}000 numbers; two separate tables would need 76,800,000.

The formal definition

e_i=E_{i,:}=\mathbf{x}_i^{\top}E,\quad E\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d};\qquad \cos(u,v)=\frac{u\cdot v}{\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert};\qquad z_i=E_i\cdot h\ \ (\text{tied output})
Symbols. V: vocabulary size. d: embedding dimension (here 2; real models use hundreds to thousands). E: the embedding table, a matrix whose row i is token i's vector. \mathbf{x}_i: the one-hot vector of token i. e_i: its embedding. \lVert u\rVert=\sqrt{u\cdot u}: the length of u. h: the hidden vector the model has computed for the current position (lesson 1.5). z_i: the score (logit) of token i. See linear algebra for dot products and matrix products.
Try this
  1. Let the auto walk run. The orange band shows the one-hot "1" selecting a row, and the matching arrow lights up on the plot. Read the readout: cat’s nearest neighbours should be puppy (cosine 0.99), kitten (0.97) and dog (0.96), with the vehicles far away (negative).
  2. Pause, select "car", and drag its tip next to "cat". The cosine between them rises toward 1 and the neighbour list reorders. That is all "learning an embedding" does: move the arrows.
  3. Drag "cat" to twice its length without changing its direction. The cosine with kitten does not change but the dot product doubles, and the tied output probabilities shift. Length and direction carry different information.
Common traps
  • The axes are not meanings. Here we labelled directions "animal" and "vehicle" for teaching. A trained table has no human-readable axes, and a single coordinate means nothing alone.
  • Dot product is not cosine. A long vector has big dot products with everything. Use cosine when you want direction only.
  • Similar embedding does not mean same meaning. Words used in similar contexts get similar vectors, which includes antonyms ("hot" and "cold" appear in the same sentences).
  • The table is the biggest block of a small model. It has V\times d numbers; this is why vocabulary size matters for memory.
Where it shows up in AI
  • The first layer of every neural language model is an embedding table, and the last layer is often tied to it (weight tying) to save parameters and improve training.
  • Retrieval and search compare embeddings by cosine similarity; the applications module builds on exactly this arithmetic.
  • Embeddings beyond words: image patches, users and products in recommenders, and graph nodes are all rows of learned tables.

Check yourself

1. Compute the dot product and cosine of dog (0.9, 0.3) and puppy (0.8, 0.7).
Dot: 0.9·0.8 + 0.3·0.7 = 0.72 + 0.21 = 0.93. Lengths: \sqrt{0.81+0.09}=0.9487 and \sqrt{0.64+0.49}=1.0630. Cosine = 0.93 / (0.9487 × 1.0630) = 0.9222.
2. Why is the matrix product of a one-hot vector with E not computed in real systems?
The product just copies one row, so reading that row costs d memory reads, whereas multiplying a length-V vector by a V×d matrix would cost V·d multiplications, almost all by zero. The matrix view is still useful for the maths and the gradient: only the selected row receives a gradient.
3. A model has V = 32 000 and d = 1 024. How many numbers are in the embedding table, and how many are saved by tying the output layer to it?
32 000 × 1 024 = 32,768,000 numbers in one table. A separate output table would need another 32,768,000, so tying saves 32,768,000 parameters (half of the two-table total, 65,536,000).

Takeaways

  • An embedding table is a V×d matrix; embedding a token is reading its row, equal to one-hot × E.
  • Training moves the rows so tokens used alike point alike; similarity is a dot product or, direction only, a cosine.
  • Tying input and output tables reuses E for scoring (z_i=E_i\cdot h) and halves the parameters of the two tables.
1.5

A neural language model

The questionA count table needs a separate row for every context and cannot say anything about a context it never saw. How can one small set of weights produce a probability for every next token from any context, and how do you nudge those weights, using calculus, when the model gives the right word too little probability?

Intuition: scores, then probabilities, then a nudge

A neural language model is a pipeline with three steps. (1) Context to hidden vector: look up the embedding of the context (for a real model, a deep network turns the whole context into a vector h; here we use the last word's embedding as h). (2) Hidden vector to logits: a linear layer gives every vocabulary token a raw score called a logit. (3) Logits to probabilities: the softmax turns any list of scores into positive numbers adding to 1, by exponentiating and dividing by the total. Bigger score, bigger share.

Training asks one question: how much probability did the model give the word that actually came next? Take the negative logarithm of that probability: the cross-entropy loss. It is small when the model was confident and right, large when it was surprised. Then, as with any neural network, we compute the gradient of the loss and move the weights a small step downhill. The remarkable fact, derived below, is that the gradient with respect to the logits has a beautifully simple form: what the model said minus what was true.

Build it up

The softmax and the loss

Worked example 1: three logits

Logits z=(2.0,\ 1.0,\ 0.1). Exponentials: e^{2.0}=7.3891,\ e^{1.0}=2.7183,\ e^{0.1}=1.1052; the sum is 11.2125. Probabilities: p=(0.6590,\ 0.2424,\ 0.0986), which add to 1. If the true next token is the second (index 1), the loss is -\ln 0.2424=1.4170 nats.

Why the gradient is p minus one-hot

Let the loss for a true token y be L=-\ln p_y with p_y=e^{z_y}/S, S=\sum_j e^{z_j}. Then L=-z_y+\ln S. Differentiate with respect to a logit z_i. The first term gives -1 if i=y and 0 otherwise, which we write -\mathbb{1}[i=y]. For the second term, \partial\ln S/\partial z_i=\frac{1}{S}\,e^{z_i}=p_i. Adding them:

\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}=p_i-\mathbb{1}[i=y]\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \nabla_z L=p-\mathbf{y}

Check on the example. The gradient is (0.6590,\ 0.2424-1,\ 0.0986)=(0.6590,\ -0.7576,\ 0.0986). A numerical check by nudging each logit by \pm10^{-5} and measuring the change of the loss gives (0.659001,\ -0.757567,\ 0.098566), matching to six digits. The meaning: a wrong token with high probability is pushed down in proportion to its probability; the true token is pushed up by the missing probability 1-p_y. When the model is already certain and right (p_y\to1), the gradient vanishes.

One whole training step

Worked example 2: vocabulary (the, cat, sat, mat), context "cat", true next "sat"

Embedding dimension 2. The context embedding is x=(1.0,\ 0.5). The output table has rows U=\begin{pmatrix}0.2&0.4\\1.0&-0.5\\0.8&0.6\\-0.3&0.1\end{pmatrix} and logits are z=Ux.

Forward. z=(0.2+0.2,\ 1.0-0.25,\ 0.8+0.3,\ -0.3+0.05)=(0.40,\ 0.75,\ 1.10,\ -0.25). Softmax: p=(0.2018,\ 0.2864,\ 0.4064,\ 0.1054). The true token "sat" has p = 0.4064, so the loss is -\ln0.4064=0.9004.

Backward. p-\mathbf{y}=(0.2018,\ 0.2864,\ -0.5936,\ 0.1054). For the output table, the gradient is the outer product (p-\mathbf{y})\,x^{\top}: row "the" is (0.2018, 0.1009), "cat" (0.2864, 0.1432), "sat" (−0.5936, −0.2968), "mat" (0.1054, 0.0527). The gradient reaching the context embedding is U^{\top}(p-\mathbf{y})=(-0.1797,\ -0.4081) (for the first entry: 0.2\cdot0.2018+1.0\cdot0.2864+0.8\cdot(-0.5936)-0.3\cdot0.1054=-0.1797). Finite differences on the embedding give (-0.179710,\ -0.408083), a match.

Update with learning rate 0.5: subtract 0.5 times each gradient from U and from the embedding x. The new probability of "sat" rises from 0.4064 to 0.5929 and the loss falls from 0.9004 to 0.5227. One step, one example, in the right direction.

The formal definition

h=E_{w_{t-1}},\qquad z=U h+b,\qquad p_i=\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}},\qquad L=-\ln p_{y},\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial z}=p-\mathbf{y},\ \ \frac{\partial L}{\partial U}=(p-\mathbf{y})h^{\top},\ \ \frac{\partial L}{\partial h}=U^{\top}(p-\mathbf{y})
Symbols. w_{t-1}: the previous token (a deeper model would use the whole context). E: input embedding table; h: the hidden vector (here just the embedding row). U: the V×d output matrix; b: an optional bias (0 in the example). z: logits. p: probabilities. y: the index of the true next token and \mathbf{y} its one-hot vector. L: the cross-entropy loss for this position. The last three expressions come from the chain rule (backpropagation). Averaging L over a text and exponentiating gives the perplexity of lesson 1.2. A deeper model replaces h=E_{w_{t-1}} by a network of the whole context; the output end, z\to p\to L and the gradient p-\mathbf{y}, stays exactly the same.
Try this
  1. Press play with context "the". At first every bar is near 1/6 (the loss is about ln 6 = 1.792). Watch the bars move toward the green lines (cat 0.75, dog 0.25) and the loss curve fall toward the dashed floor, 0.347 nats, the entropy of the counts.
  2. Press "Check gradient numerically" once or twice: the analytic p-\mathbf{y} and the finite-difference numbers agree to about five decimals. Compare the readout's "p − onehot" line with the last example.
  3. Raise the learning rate to 2, then lower it to 0.05, resetting each time. Too large is jumpy, too small is slow. Tick "tie input and output embeddings", reset, and compare the parameter count and the learning speed. Then look at context "cat": it can only learn sat 2/3 and ran 1/3.
Common traps
  • Logits are not probabilities. They can be negative and do not add to 1. Only the softmax makes them probabilities. Loss functions in libraries often take logits directly, for numerical stability, and apply the softmax inside.
  • Overflow. e^{z} overflows for large z. Real code subtracts the largest logit first (it changes nothing, since the common factor cancels in the ratio); the labs do the same.
  • The model cannot beat the counts. With only the previous word as context, the best possible loss is the entropy of the conditional counts (0.347 nats here). Lower loss needs more context or more data, not a longer run.
  • Probability 0 is unreachable. The softmax gives every token positive probability, so the loss is never infinite, unlike an unsmoothed count table.
Where it shows up in AI
  • The output head of every large language model is this: a vector for the current position, a V-way linear layer, a softmax, trained with cross-entropy against the next token. Only the middle (how h is computed from the context, with attention in the next module) is different.
  • The gradient p-\mathbf{y} is the same simple expression for every classifier with softmax output: image classifiers, intent detectors and reward-model heads.
  • Generalisation by sharing. Because words with similar embeddings produce similar logits, the model can give sensible probability to a pair it never saw in training, the thing counting could not do.

Check yourself

1. Logits (1, 0, 0), true token index 0. Compute p, the loss, and the gradient.
Exponentials: 2.7183, 1, 1; sum 4.7183. p=(0.5761,\ 0.2119,\ 0.2119). Loss -\ln0.5761=0.5514. Gradient p-\mathbf{y}=(0.5761-1,\ 0.2119,\ 0.2119)=(-0.4239,\ 0.2119,\ 0.2119); the entries sum to 0, as they must (softmax outputs always add to 1, so the gradient entries add to 0).
2. Why do the entries of p minus one-hot always add up to 0?
The entries of p add to 1 and the one-hot vector adds to 1, so their difference adds to 0. Moving the logits along this gradient therefore never changes their sum: the loss depends only on differences between logits (adding the same number to all logits leaves the softmax unchanged).
3. In worked example 2, what happens to the gradient for "sat" if the model already gives sat probability 0.99?
The gradient entry for the true token is 0.99-1=-0.01 and the others are tiny positive numbers adding to 0.01. The update is correspondingly tiny: a model that is already confident and right is barely changed. The surprise-weighted gradient focuses learning on the mistakes.

Takeaways

  • Context → hidden vector → logits → softmax → cross-entropy against the true next token; one shared set of weights covers every context.
  • The loss gradient with respect to the logits is p-\text{one-hot}: the model's belief minus the truth. It was derived with L=-z_y+\ln\sum e^{z} and checked numerically.
  • Gradients then flow to the output matrix (p-\mathbf{y})h^\top and the embedding U^\top(p-\mathbf{y}); the best any model can do is the entropy of the data given its context.
1.6

Decoding and sampling

The questionA trained model hands you a probability for every possible next token. You must pick exactly one, then repeat. Always picking the most probable looks sensible but tends to give dull, repetitive text; picking at random in proportion to the probabilities can wander into nonsense. What dials give you control over the dull-versus-wild trade-off, and why are they safe to apply?

Intuition: the model proposes, the decoder disposes

Training produces a model of probabilities. Decoding is the separate procedure that turns those probabilities into actual text, one token at a time. Think of a pointer on a roulette wheel whose slices are the probabilities. Greedy decoding always takes the biggest slice. Sampling spins the wheel. Between them sit dials that reshape the wheel before the spin: temperature makes the big slices bigger or smaller relative to the others; top-k and top-p cut off the thin slivers at the unlikely end (where nonsense lives) and re-divide the wheel among the survivors.

Decoding does not change what the model knows. It only changes how it is read out. That is why the same model can serve a code assistant (low temperature, reliable) and a brainstorming tool (higher temperature, varied).

Build it up

Worked example: six candidates after "Today the weather is"

Logits for sunny, cloudy, rainy, windy, cold, purple: z=(2.0,\ 1.5,\ 1.0,\ 0.2,\ -0.3,\ -2.0). Exponentials add to 16.6867.

Temperature. Divide the logits by T before the softmax. At T = 1 the probabilities are (0.4428, 0.2686, 0.1629, 0.0732, 0.0444, 0.0081); their entropy is 1.988 bits. At T = 0.5 (sharper): (0.6490, 0.2387, 0.0878, 0.0177, 0.0065, 0.0002). At T = 2 (flatter): (0.3083, 0.2401, 0.1870, 0.1253, 0.0976, 0.0417). As T → 0 all the mass goes to the largest logit (T = 0.1 gives 0.9933 to "sunny"), which is greedy decoding; as T → ∞ the distribution becomes uniform.

Top-k, k = 3. Keep the 3 most probable (0.4428, 0.2686, 0.1629 at T = 1; they sum to 0.8743), set the rest to 0, and divide by the kept sum: 0.4428/0.8743=0.5065, 0.2686/0.8743=0.3072, 0.1629/0.8743=0.1863. Now "purple", "cold" and "windy" are impossible, and the three remaining numbers add to 1.

Top-p (nucleus), p = 0.9. Sort descending and add up until the sum reaches at least 0.9: 0.4428, 0.7114, 0.8743 (not yet), 0.9475 (reached). The nucleus has 4 tokens. Divide by 0.9475: (0.4674, 0.2835, 0.1719, 0.0773). With p = 0.8 the nucleus is 3 tokens (the cumulative sum 0.7114 is below 0.8, and 0.8743 passes it), giving the same result as k = 3. Unlike top-k, the size of the nucleus adapts: a confident model keeps few tokens, an unsure one keeps many.

Greedy versus beam search. Greedy picks the best token at each step; it can miss a sentence whose first word is a little less likely but whose continuation is much better. Beam search keeps the w best partial sentences at each step. Example: first word a (0.5), b (0.4), c (0.1). After a the best second word, x, has 0.4; after b the word x has 0.9. Greedy takes a then x: 0.5\times0.4=0.2. Beam search with width 2 keeps a and b, extends both, and finds b then x: 0.4\times0.9=0.36, the best of all 9 sentences.

The formal definition

p_i^{(T)}=\frac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}},\qquad \tilde p_i=\frac{p_i\,\mathbb{1}[i\in S]}{\sum_{j\in S}p_j},\quad S=\begin{cases}\text{the }k\text{ most probable tokens}&\text{top-}k\\ \text{the smallest set of top tokens with }\sum_{j\in S}p_j\ge P&\text{top-}p\end{cases}
Symbols. z_i: logit of token i. T>0: temperature (T = 1 leaves the model's own probabilities). p_i: probability before truncation. S: the set of tokens that survive. \mathbb{1}[i\in S]: 1 if i survives, else 0. k, P: the top-k size and top-p threshold. \tilde p_i: the final sampling probability. The division by \sum_{j\in S}p_j is the renormalisation: without it the numbers would not add to 1. In the lab the order is temperature, then top-k, then top-p; software differs on the order and on exact tie handling, which is why you should read the documentation of the system you use.
Try this
  1. Keep the default logits and T = 1: panel ① shows 0.4428, 0.2686, 0.1629 ... Move T to 0.5, then 2, and compare with the worked example. The sampled bars (④) track panel ③; with T = 2 even "purple" shows up now and then.
  2. Set T = 1 and top-k = 3: panel ② shows 0.5065, 0.3072, 0.1863 and removed words. Then reset top-k to 6 and set top-p = 0.9: four tokens survive, as computed above. Slide top-p to 0.4: a single token survives and sampling becomes greedy.
  3. Edit a logit, for example raise "purple" to 3. With top-p on, the survivor set changes with the shape of the distribution. Run at least 100 samples and watch the bars approach the marks; with the Step button you can see single draws.
Try this

Common traps
  • Temperature 0 is not a setting. The formula divides by T; "T = 0" in software means "take the argmax" (greedy). Very low T also makes results nearly deterministic, but ties and numerical details can still change them.
  • Truncate, then renormalise. Cutting tokens without dividing by the kept sum gives numbers that do not add to 1; the mass would silently be assigned to nothing.
  • Top-k and top-p are not the same. Top-k keeps a fixed number, even when the model is certain of one token (then it keeps junk) or unsure among 50 (then it cuts good options). Top-p adapts the count to the distribution.
  • Beam search is not sampling. It maximises sequence probability and is deterministic; for open-ended text it is often reported to give repetitive, generic output, because the highest-probability sequence is not the most natural one. It shines when there is one right answer (translation, transcription).
  • Repetition. Greedy and low-temperature decoding can loop: once a phrase is likely, repeating it is likely again. Penalties on repeated tokens or sampling with moderate T reduce it, at some cost in accuracy.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Chat models and code completion expose temperature and top-p (and sometimes top-k) as settings: low values for factual or code answers, higher for creative writing. These dials change style, not knowledge.
  • Constrained decoding (the applications module) sets the probability of forbidden tokens to 0 and renormalises, the same operation as truncation here.
  • Speculative decoding and best-of-n (efficiency and alignment modules) build on sampling; a good grasp of the sampled distribution is what makes their guarantees legible.

Check yourself

1. For probabilities (0.5, 0.3, 0.15, 0.05), what does top-p = 0.7 give?
Cumulative sums: 0.5, 0.8. The nucleus is the first two tokens (0.5 alone is below 0.7, and 0.8 passes it). Divide by 0.8: (0.625, 0.375, 0, 0).
2. Starting from logits (2, 1, 0), compute the probabilities at T = 1 and at T = 2.
T = 1: exponentials 7.389, 2.718, 1; sum 11.107; p = (0.6652, 0.2447, 0.0900). T = 2: logits halve to (1, 0.5, 0): exponentials 2.718, 1.649, 1; sum 5.367; p = (0.5065, 0.3072, 0.1863). Higher T flattens the distribution.
3. Why can a beam of width 1 never beat a beam of width 3 in the sequence probability it finds, on the two-word example?
Width 1 considers one first word, width 3 considers all three, and with only two words, width 3 examines all nine sentences exactly. Any sentence the narrower search returns is among the nine, so the wide search finds one at least as probable. (In longer sentences a wider beam is still not guaranteed to find the true best sentence, only to search more of the space.)

Takeaways

  • The model gives probabilities; decoding picks tokens. Greedy takes the largest, sampling follows the distribution, beam search keeps the best partial sentences.
  • Temperature divides the logits (sharper below 1, flatter above); top-k and top-p cut the tail, then divide by the kept sum so the total is 1.
  • The dials trade reliability for variety and change how the model is read out, never what it has learned.
