Module 6 · Policy-gradient & actor–critic methods

Value-based methods learn how good each action is, then pick the best one. Policy-gradient methods skip that step and adjust the policy itself: they raise the probability of actions that led to good outcomes and lower the probability of those that led to bad ones. This module builds the idea from a single softmax choice up to PPO, the method behind most practical policy learning, including robot locomotion and language-model fine-tuning.

The running setup. An agent sees a state s, chooses an action a from a policy \pi_\theta(a\mid s) with parameters \theta, receives a reward, and moves on. Its goal is to maximise the expected discounted return J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[G_0], where G_0=\sum_{t\ge 0}\gamma^t r_{t+1} and the discount is \gamma\in[0,1).

How to read the labs. Every lab is an illustrative toy with a fixed seed, so runs are reproducible. Each one states its simplifications in its caption. The numbers in the text were computed by hand and checked in code; the labs recompute them live when you change a slider or a formula.

6.1

Parameterising policies

The questionA robot arm must choose how hard to push a box, a continuous number. A game agent must choose between three moves, and in one game the best strategy is to be unpredictable. A value table cannot represent either. How do we write down a policy that we can adjust with a gradient, and that can be random on purpose?

Intuition

Think of a policy as a dial for each situation. For a state, the policy says "action 1 with 60% chance, action 2 with 30%, action 3 with 10%". A policy is stochastic when it spreads its probability over several actions, and deterministic when it puts all of it on one. Stochastic policies are not a nuisance. A rock-paper-scissors player who always plays rock loses to anyone who notices. The best strategy is to play each move one third of the time, and no deterministic policy can do that.

A policy-gradient method needs the policy to be a smooth function of its parameters \theta. If a small change in \theta changes the action probabilities a little, then we can ask which direction of change makes good outcomes more likely. The two standard constructions are a softmax over a finite set of actions and a Gaussian over a continuous action.

Build it up, one piece at a time

Step 1: scores become probabilities. Suppose that in state s each action a has a real-valued score, a logit \theta_a. Scores can be negative or large, so we pass them through the exponential, which is always positive, and divide by the total so the probabilities sum to one. That is the softmax.

Step 2: why softmax is a good parameterisation. Every probability is strictly between 0 and 1, so no action is ever ruled out completely. Adding the same number to every logit changes nothing, so only differences between logits matter. And the result is differentiable everywhere, which is what gradient methods need.

Step 3: a temperature controls how sharp the policy is. Dividing the logits by a temperature \tau>0 makes the policy close to uniform when \tau is large and close to "pick the largest logit" when \tau is small.

Step 4: a continuous action needs a density. A steering angle or a torque is a real number, so a policy must be a probability density. The usual choice is a Gaussian with a mean that depends on the state and a fixed or learned spread: a\sim\mathcal N(\mu_\theta(s),\sigma^2). Sampling from it is simple, and its log-density has a clean gradient.

Worked example: a softmax over three actions

Take logits \theta=(2,\,1,\,0) in one state and temperature \tau=1. The scores exponentiate to e^2=7.38906, e^1=2.71828, e^0=1, which sum to 11.10734. So

\pi(\cdot\mid s)=\left(\frac{7.38906}{11.10734},\ \frac{2.71828}{11.10734},\ \frac{1}{11.10734}\right)=(0.66524,\ 0.24473,\ 0.09003)

The probabilities sum to 1.00000 and the first action is the most likely. Now set \tau=2. The scores become e^{1}, e^{0.5}, e^{0} = 2.71828, 1.64872, 1, with sum 5.36700, so the policy is flatter: (0.50648,\ 0.30720,\ 0.18632). Halving the sharpness moved the first action from 67% to 51%.

For a Gaussian take mean \mu=0.8, spread \sigma=0.5 and the sampled action a=1.2. The standardised distance is z=(a-\mu)/\sigma=0.4/0.5=0.8, and the log-density is

\log\pi(a\mid s)=-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\log\sigma-\tfrac12 z^2=-0.91894+0.69315-0.32=-0.54579,

so the density itself is e^{-0.54579}=0.57938. A density is not a probability, but it is the quantity the gradient will use.

Formal definition

\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\frac{\exp\!\big(\theta_{s,a}/\tau\big)}{\sum_{b}\exp\!\big(\theta_{s,b}/\tau\big)}\qquad\qquad \pi_\theta(a\mid s)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(a-\mu_\theta(s))^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)
s, astate and action
\thetaall learnable parameters; here the logits or the mean and spread
\theta_{s,a}the logit (score) of action a in state s
\tautemperature, a positive number that flattens the softmax when it is large
\mu_\theta(s),\ \sigmamean of the Gaussian for state s and its standard deviation
zthe standardised action (a-\mu)/\sigma

Common traps
  • A probability is not a density. A Gaussian density can exceed 1 (for a small spread it does). Only the density over an interval gives a probability.
  • Logits are not probabilities. Increasing a logit by 1 does not add 1 to a probability. Its effect depends on all the other logits.
  • Stochastic does not mean random. The policy is a precise distribution; the randomness is in which action is drawn from it.
  • Always store the policy's parameters and the sampling rule together. A learned policy with a different temperature at test time is a different policy.
Where it shows up in AI

The softmax policy is the output layer of discrete-action agents, for example the move choice of game agents and the next-token distribution of a language model, which is a policy over a vocabulary. The Gaussian policy is the usual choice for continuous control of robot joints and vehicles; PPO implementations for MuJoCo locomotion typically output a mean and a learned log-standard-deviation for each joint.

Pipeline placement. Learn stage for training the parameters; act stage at every control step, where the policy is sampled. Stores: the network or table of parameters (θ) and, for continuous control, the spread or its log.

Check yourself

1. In the worked example, why does halving the sharpness (going from τ = 1 to τ = 2) reduce the first action's probability from 0.665 to 0.506?
Dividing the logits by τ = 2 halves the differences between them. The scores become e^1, e^0.5, e^0 rather than e^2, e^1, e^0, so the first action's share of the total falls to 2.71828 / 5.36700 = 0.50648.
2. For a Gaussian with σ = 0.5, what is z when the action is a = 0.3 and the mean is μ = 0.8, and which way does the log-density move compared with a = 1.2?
z = (0.3 − 0.8)/0.5 = −1.0. The log-density is −½log(2π) − log(0.5) − ½(1)² = −0.91894 + 0.69315 − 0.5 = −0.72579, which is lower than −0.54579 at a = 1.2, because the action lies one spread from the mean rather than 0.8 spreads.
3. Why is a deterministic policy a poor choice in rock-paper-scissors, even if it is the best single move against a fixed opponent?
Any fixed move can be countered by an opponent who notices it. The optimal strategy against an adaptive opponent is to randomise uniformly, and a deterministic policy cannot represent a uniform distribution over the three moves.

Takeaways

  • A policy is a distribution over actions, written with parameters θ so that a gradient can change it.
  • Softmax handles finitely many actions; a Gaussian handles continuous actions. Both are differentiable in θ.
  • Stochastic policies are sometimes optimal and always give exploration. The temperature or spread sets how much.
6.2

The policy gradient theorem

The questionWe want to raise the expected return J(\theta) by gradient ascent. But the return depends on which actions were sampled, and sampling is a random process that we cannot differentiate through directly. How can we compute \nabla_\theta J from samples at all, and why does it not require knowing how the environment works?

Intuition

Imagine a lottery whose prize depends on which ticket you draw, and whose ticket probabilities you can adjust. To improve your expected prize you do not need to know how the lottery machine works. You only need to know how each ticket's probability changes when you adjust the machine, and how much each ticket is worth. Multiply those two things, add them up over tickets, and you have the direction that improves your expected prize.

Policy gradients use the same logic. The unknown part is the environment, which turns actions into rewards and next states. The part we control is the policy's probabilities for the actions. The theorem tells us that the gradient needs only those two pieces: the policy's score function and an estimate of how good each action is.

Build it up, one piece at a time

Step 1: the score-function identity. For any probability distribution p_\theta, the derivative of a probability is the probability times the derivative of its logarithm:

\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)=p_\theta(x)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)

This is just the chain rule applied to \log. Apply it inside an expectation and the derivative moves onto the distribution's log:

\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb E_{x\sim p_\theta}[f(x)]=\sum_x f(x)\,p_\theta(x)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)=\mathbb E_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[f(x)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\big]

The right-hand side is an expectation we can estimate by sampling, because it needs only samples x and the score \nabla\log p, which we can compute from the policy's own formula. This is the score-function trick (also called the likelihood-ratio trick).

Step 2: the one-step case. Let the episode last one step, so J(\theta)=\sum_a\pi_\theta(a)\,Q(a) where Q(a) is the expected reward of action a. The identity gives \nabla J=\sum_a Q(a)\,\pi(a)\nabla\log\pi(a), which is exactly the expectation above with f=Q.

Step 3: many steps. In a multi-step problem the same argument over whole trajectories gives the policy gradient theorem. Its clean form, from Sutton et al. (2000), replaces the trajectory sum with states and actions. For a policy \pi_\theta, a state distribution \mu that the policy visits, and the action-value Q^\pi:

\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\ \propto\ \sum_s \mu(s)\sum_a Q^\pi(s,a)\,\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\ =\ \mathbb E_{s\sim\mu,\ a\sim\pi_\theta}\big[Q^\pi(s,a)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big]

The sign "∝" means equal up to a positive constant that depends on the discount and the horizon. That constant only rescales the step size, so the direction is what matters. The crucial feature is that the environment's transition probabilities do not appear. They shape which states and actions are visited, and \mu and Q^\pi are whatever the environment produces; no model of the environment is differentiated.

Worked example: a one-step gradient by hand

One state, two actions, logits \theta=(0,0) so \pi=(0.5,\,0.5), and action values Q=(1,\,3). The score of action a with respect to logit \theta_b is \mathbf 1[a=b]-\pi_b. For b=1:

\nabla_{\theta_1}J=\sum_a \pi(a)\,Q(a)\,(\mathbf 1[a=1]-\pi_1)=0.5(1)(0.5)+0.5(3)(-0.5)=0.25-0.75=-0.50

For b=2, the same sum gives 0.5(1)(-0.5)+0.5(3)(0.5)=0.50. The gradient is (-0.5,\ 0.5): gradient ascent lowers the logit of the poor action and raises the logit of the good one. Check with the direct derivative: J=\pi_1\cdot 1+(1-\pi_1)\cdot 3=3-2\pi_1 and \pi_1=\sigma(\theta_1-\theta_2), so \partial J/\partial\theta_1=-2\,\pi_1(1-\pi_1)=-0.5, which agrees.

The sampled estimate is noisy. If action 2 is drawn, the single-sample estimate is Q(2)\,(\mathbf 1[a=b]-\pi_b)=3\,(-0.5,\,0.5)=(-1.5,\,1.5), three times the true gradient in magnitude. The average of many such samples approaches (-0.5,\,0.5).

Formal definition

J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_{t\ge 0}\gamma^t r_{t+1}\Big],\qquad \nabla_\theta J(\theta)\ \propto\ \mathbb E_{s\sim\mu,\ a\sim\pi_\theta}\big[Q^{\pi_\theta}(s,a)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big]
J(\theta)expected discounted return from the start state under policy \pi_\theta
\gammadiscount factor, 0\le\gamma<1 here, so later rewards count less
r_{t+1}reward received after the action taken at time t
\mu(s)the (discounted or average) distribution of states visited under \pi_\theta
Q^\pi(s,a)expected discounted return after taking a in s and then following \pi
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)the score: which way to move θ to make a more likely in s

Conditions: the theorem assumes \pi_\theta is differentiable in \theta and that the expectations exist. The exact constant in "∝" depends on whether the problem is episodic or continuing; it does not change the direction of the gradient.

Common traps
  • Differentiating the rewards. The reward function usually is not differentiable and need not be. The theorem only differentiates the policy.
  • Forgetting the sign. The update is ascent: \theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\nabla J. Gradient descent on -J is the same thing written differently.
  • Using Q as a sample. The expectation is over actions and states, so one sampled return is an unbiased but very noisy estimate. The estimator is not the expectation.
  • Assuming the state distribution is fixed. Changing θ changes which states are visited. The theorem handles this; naive "fix the states" arguments do not.
Where it shows up in AI

Every policy-gradient method, from REINFORCE through A2C to PPO and the RL fine-tuning of language models, is a sample-based estimate of this expression. In RLHF, the "action" is a sampled response, the "reward" comes from a learned preference model, and the score is the gradient of the log-probability the language model assigns to the response.

Pipeline placement. Interact stage (samples are drawn by running the policy), then learn stage (the score-weighted sum is computed on a batch and θ is updated). Stores: the sampled states, actions and returns for one batch, and the policy parameters.

Check yourself

1. With π = (0.5, 0.5) and Q = (1, 3), compute ∇θ₂J for the one-step problem. (Use the formula, not the figure above.)
∇θ₂J = Σ_a π(a) Q(a)(1[a=2] − π₂) = 0.5(1)(0 − 0.5) + 0.5(3)(1 − 0.5) = −0.25 + 0.75 = 0.50.
2. Why does the policy gradient not need the transition probabilities of the environment?
The gradient differentiates only the policy's probabilities through the score ∇log π. The environment appears only through the expectation, which is estimated by sampling it. Its dynamics shape which states and returns occur, but they are not differentiated.
3. Suppose you draw one action a = 2 with the same numbers. What is the single-sample estimate for θ₁, and why is a single sample a poor guide?
g = Q(2)(1[a=1] − π₁) = 3(0 − 0.5) = −1.5. The true gradient for θ₁ is −0.5, so this sample overshoots by a factor of three in the opposite direction from the average behaviour. Many samples are needed to average out this noise.

Takeaways

  • The score-function identity turns the gradient of an expectation into an expectation of score times value.
  • The policy gradient needs only samples, the score of the policy, and an estimate of how good each action is.
  • The result is a direction, up to a positive constant; single samples are very noisy, so practical methods average and reduce variance.
6.3

REINFORCE

The questionThe theorem gives us a gradient in expectation. Can we turn it into an algorithm that an agent runs, with nothing but the ability to act and observe rewards? And once it runs, why does it learn so slowly and so erratically?

Intuition

Imagine a student who tries a strategy on a test, scores 80, and then makes the strategy more likely to be used again. If the score was 20, the strategy becomes less likely. REINFORCE (Williams, 1992) is exactly this: play one episode with the current policy, compute the return that followed each action, and move each action's probability in proportion to that return. Actions that were followed by high returns become more likely, and actions followed by low returns become less likely.

The method is called Monte Carlo because it waits for the episode to finish and uses the actual observed return. No value is estimated yet, so it is simple, unbiased and very noisy. Both facts follow from that one design choice.

Build it up, one piece at a time

Step 1: the return from each step. For each time t in an episode, the reward-to-go is the discounted sum of rewards from there on: G_t=\sum_{k\ge 0}\gamma^k r_{t+k+1}. Only rewards after an action can depend on it, so this is the right quantity to weight that action's score with.

Step 2: the update. Collect an episode (s_0,a_0,r_1,\dots,s_T). Then for each step take a gradient step on the score weighted by the return:

\theta\ \leftarrow\ \theta+\alpha\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,G_t

Each term is an unbiased sample of the policy gradient from the previous lesson, because G_t has the expected value of Q^\pi(s_t,a_t) given the state and action at time t.

Step 3: the algorithm in code form.

init theta
repeat for each episode:
    generate episode s0, a0, r1, s1, a1, r2, ..., s_{T-1}, a_{T-1}, r_T
        using pi_theta
    for t = 0 .. T-1:
        G = sum over k >= t of gamma^(k-t) * r_{k+1}   # computed backwards
        theta = theta + alpha * grad(log pi_theta(a_t | s_t)) * G

Step 4: why it is slow and erratic. A single episode is one random draw. A different draw can change the return by a lot, and a large return on an action that was merely lucky pushes its probability up strongly. The variance of the estimate grows with the length of the episode and with the spread of the rewards. The fix is the subject of the next lesson.

Worked example: one episode, three steps

Rewards after each action are r_1=0,\ r_2=1,\ r_3=2, discount \gamma=0.9, the sampled actions are (a_0,a_1,a_2)=(1,2,1), and the policy starts uniform: \theta=(0,0), \pi=(0.5,0.5). Learning rate \alpha=0.1.

The reward-to-go values, computed backwards from the end, are

G_2=r_3=2,\qquad G_1=r_2+\gamma r_3=1+1.8=2.8,\qquad G_0=r_1+\gamma r_2+\gamma^2 r_3=0+0.9+1.62=2.52

The score of the sampled action in the two-action softmax is \nabla\log\pi(a)=(\mathbf 1[a=1]-\pi_1,\ \mathbf 1[a=2]-\pi_2), which gives (0.5,-0.5) for action 1 and (-0.5,0.5) for action 2. The three contributions are

t{=}0:\ (0.5,-0.5)(2.52)=(1.26,-1.26)\qquad t{=}1:\ (-0.5,0.5)(2.8)=(-1.40,1.40)\qquad t{=}2:\ (0.5,-0.5)(2)=(1.00,-1.00)

They sum to (0.86,-0.86). The update is \theta\leftarrow(0,0)+0.1(0.86,-0.86)=(0.086,-0.086). The new probability of action 1 is \sigma(0.086-(-0.086))=\sigma(0.172)=0.54289, up from 0.5. The episode's actions led to positive returns on balance, so action 1 became more likely.

Notice what would happen if the episode had given returns of −2 instead: every term would flip sign and action 1 would become less likely. The same episode can push either way depending only on the sign of the return. That is why the next lesson worries about the sign and the size of the return.

Formal definition

G_t=\sum_{k=0}^{T-t-1}\gamma^k r_{t+k+1},\qquad \theta_{\text{new}}=\theta+\alpha\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}G_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)
G_tthe discounted return from time t onward (the reward-to-go)
Tthe length of the episode, after which the episode ends
\alphathe learning rate (step size)
\gammadiscount factor, here 0.9 in the worked example
r_{t+1}reward after the action at time t

Conditions: the episode must terminate (or the return must be truncated), the policy must be differentiable, and \alpha must be small enough for the update to be a gradient step. Convergence to a local optimum holds under standard stochastic-approximation conditions on \alpha; it is not a guarantee of finding the best policy.

Lab: many runs of REINFORCE on the same bandit

Try this

  2. Set the reward noise σ to 0 and then to 1.5. With no noise the runs tend to look more alike; with large noise, some runs reach a high P(best arm) quickly while others are slow to move for a long time.

Common traps
  • Using the full episode return for every step. It is unbiased but adds noise from rewards that happened before the action. Use the reward-to-go G_t.
  • Updating during the episode with the discounted return. The update needs the return, which is known only once the episode ends, so REINFORCE is an end-of-episode method.
  • Positive rewards with no baseline. If all rewards are positive, every sampled action is reinforced, and the most frequently sampled actions gain the most. This is correct in expectation but slows learning, as the lab shows.
  • Calling it "stable". Its unbiasedness does not give stability; one lucky return can move the policy far in one step.
Where it shows up in AI

REINFORCE is the simplest policy-learning algorithm and the basis of later methods. It is used as a teaching baseline and in small problems, and its update is the core of policy-gradient fine-tuning in RLHF pipelines, where the reward is given at the end of a sampled response and the score is the log-probability of its tokens.

Pipeline placement. Interact stage (one full episode per update); learn stage (the return-weighted score sum over the episode, then an update). Cost: one episode of environment steps per update, and one backward pass over the episode. Stores: the episode's states, actions and rewards, and the policy parameters.

Check yourself

1. In the worked example, what is G₁ if γ is 0.5 instead of 0.9?
G₁ = r₂ + γr₃ = 1 + 0.5(2) = 2.0. The discount reduces the weight of the later reward, so the return from time 1 is smaller than the 2.8 obtained with γ = 0.9.
2. If the same episode had produced returns G₀ = −2.52, G₁ = −2.8, G₂ = −2 (rewards all negative), what would the update to θ be?
Every term flips sign, so the sum is (−0.86, 0.86) and θ becomes (−0.086, 0.086). Action 1 becomes less likely, with π₁ = σ(−0.172) = 0.45711. A bad episode lowers the probability of the actions it used.
3. Why is REINFORCE unbiased but noisy, and which single change reduces the noise most in practice?
It is unbiased because each G_t, given (s_t, a_t), has expectation Q^π(s_t, a_t), so the average update equals the policy gradient. It is noisy because the return depends on the whole random future, not just on the action. Subtracting a state-dependent baseline from G_t reduces the noise without changing the expected update; that is the next lesson.

Takeaways

  • REINFORCE turns the policy gradient into a practical update: act for an episode, weight each action's score by its reward-to-go, and step.
  • It is unbiased, but the return is a noisy measure, so single updates can be large and erratic.
  • The remedy is to subtract a baseline, which keeps the expected update the same and removes much of the noise.
6.4

Baselines and the advantage function

The questionREINFORCE is unbiased, but its updates swing wildly from episode to episode. Suppose every action in a game is worth between 10 and 11 points. Should a policy whose returns are all near 10 be pushed hard by every single return? Can we subtract something from the returns without changing where the learning goes?

Intuition

Suppose you are grading a class where every answer scores between 90 and 100. If you reward each student by their raw score, the grades carry almost no information about who did better than usual; everyone gets a big positive push. If you reward each student by how far they were above the class average, the signal is sharp: a student at 98 is above average and one at 91 is below. The average itself does not change who is better. It only changes the size of the push.

A baseline is that class average for the policy gradient. We subtract a number b(s) that depends on the state but not on the action. The expected update is unchanged, because the subtracted part averages to zero, but each sampled update is much smaller and more informative.

Build it up, one piece at a time

Step 1: why the subtracted term averages to zero. Take the policy gradient with a baseline, \mathbb E[(G-b(s))\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s)]. The extra piece is \mathbb E[b(s)\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s)]=b(s)\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\nabla\log\pi(a\mid s). The sum is \sum_a\nabla\pi(a\mid s)=\nabla\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)=\nabla 1=0. So the expectation is unchanged, provided b does not depend on the sampled action a. It may depend on the state, and it may be a learned function of the state.

Step 2: the natural baseline is the value. The state value V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[Q^\pi(s,a)] is the average return of the policy from s. Subtracting it gives the advantage:

A^\pi(s,a)=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s)

The advantage is positive when an action is better than what the policy would do on average in that state, and negative when worse. Its average over the policy's own actions is zero. That is why it is such a clean signal: it measures the difference an action makes.

Step 3: why variance drops. The variance of the sampled update is governed by the spread of the weights multiplying the scores. Replacing Q by Q-V removes the part of the weight that is common to every action in the state, so the weights cluster around zero. In the extreme case where all actions are equally good, the advantage is exactly zero and the update is zero too, rather than a large push in a random direction.

Worked example: a baseline that removes all the noise

Two actions with deterministic rewards r(1)=10 and r(2)=11, uniform policy \pi=(0.5,0.5). The value is V=0.5(10)+0.5(11)=10.5, and the advantages are A(1)=10-10.5=-0.5 and A(2)=11-10.5=0.5. The true gradient of the first logit is

\nabla_{\theta_1}J=\sum_a\pi(a)\,r(a)\,(\mathbf 1[a=1]-\pi_1)=0.5(10)(0.5)+0.5(11)(-0.5)=2.5-2.75=-0.25

Without a baseline, each sample is r(a)\,(\mathbf 1[a=1]-\pi_1). If action 1 is drawn it is 10(0.5)=5; if action 2 is drawn it is 11(-0.5)=-5.5. The two values average to 0.5(5)+0.5(-5.5)=-0.25, which is correct, but each sample is large and the variance is 0.5(5^2)+0.5(5.5^2)-(-0.25)^2=27.5625.

With the baseline b=10.5, the weights are the advantages. Action 1 gives (-0.5)(0.5)=-0.25 and action 2 gives (0.5)(-0.5)=-0.25. Both samples are exactly -0.25. The mean is still -0.25, and the variance is 0. This is a special case, since the two actions have equal probability and the rewards differ by a constant shift of the advantage. In general the baseline reduces the variance but does not remove it.

The lesson is the same in both: the expected update is -0.25 with or without the baseline, but the baseline makes each sampled update far more accurate.

Formal definition

\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\propto\mathbb E\big[(Q^\pi(s,a)-b(s))\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big]=\mathbb E\big[A^\pi(s,a)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big]
b(s)a baseline: any function of the state that does not depend on the action
V^\pi(s)the state value, \mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[Q^\pi(s,a)], the policy's average return from s
A^\pi(s,a)the advantage, how much better action a is than the policy's average in s
\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[A^\pi(s,a)]equals zero for every state (a useful check on any advantage estimate)

Conditions: the baseline must not depend on the action being scored; a baseline that depends on the action is biased. In practice V is not known exactly and is learned, so the advantage is an estimate, and that introduces the bias–variance trade-off of the next lesson.

Lab: the baseline keeps the mean and changes the spread

Try this
  1. With the baseline at 0 (no baseline), look at the orange histogram. Its centre is the green dashed line, and it is wide. Note the variance printed in the readout.
  2. Press "Set b = V(θ) (exact)". The blue histogram narrows around the same green line. The mean does not move, but the variance falls. This is the lesson in one picture.
  3. Drag the baseline to values far from V, such as 0 or 3. The mean stays put for every b, but the variance grows again. A baseline near the expected reward is a sensible choice; the variance is not unchanged by b.
  4. Set the reward noise σ to 0. Part of the spread now comes only from which arm is drawn, and the baseline still changes it. Compare b = 0 with b = V(θ) to see the difference that remains.
Common traps
  • A baseline that depends on the action. Then the subtracted term no longer averages to zero, and the gradient is biased. Keep b a function of the state only.
  • Confusing advantage with reward. A positive reward does not mean an action was good; it must be better than the state's average.
  • Thinking a baseline makes the update exact. It reduces variance; the sampled update is still noisy unless the advantage is almost constant.
  • Using the return of the whole episode as Q. That estimate is noisy. The next lesson uses a learned value and bootstrapping to reduce it.
Where it shows up in AI

Almost every modern policy-gradient method uses an advantage. A2C and PPO both estimate A from a learned value network. In language-model RL, a per-batch mean reward is a common baseline, which is why reward models are often normalised by batch statistics before the update.

Pipeline placement. Learn stage: a value estimate is computed for each state in the batch and the advantage is formed before the update. The value network is trained in the same stage. Stores: the batch of states, actions, returns and the value estimates, and the value network's weights.

Check yourself

1. With rewards 10 and 11 and π = (0.5, 0.5), what is the advantage of action 2 if the policy is changed to π = (0.25, 0.75)?
V = 0.25(10) + 0.75(11) = 10.75, so A(2) = 11 − 10.75 = 0.25. The advantage of action 2 shrinks as the policy puts more probability on it, because the average return moves toward its value.
2. Prove that subtracting a state-dependent baseline does not change the expected gradient.
The subtracted term is E[b(s)∇log π(a|s)] = b(s) Σ_a π(a|s) ∇log π(a|s) = b(s) Σ_a ∇π(a|s) = b(s) ∇Σ_a π(a|s) = b(s) ∇1 = 0. The step uses ∇π = π∇log π and the fact that b(s) does not depend on a.
3. In the worked example, why does the baseline make the two per-sample updates identical?
The advantages are −0.5 for action 1 and +0.5 for action 2. Their score vectors are (0.5, −0.5) and (−0.5, 0.5), so the products are (−0.25, 0.25) and (−0.25, 0.25). The advantage and the score both flip sign between the two actions, so their products coincide.

Takeaways

  • A state-dependent baseline leaves the expected policy gradient unchanged, so it is free variance reduction.
  • The advantage A = Q − V measures how much better an action is than the policy's average in its state.
  • The baseline should be close to the expected return; the advantage is then the signal that drives the update.
6.5

Actor–critic methods

The questionA baseline needs the value V(s), but we do not know it. REINFORCE waits for whole episodes to estimate returns. Could the policy learn while the episode is still running, by learning a value estimate at the same time and using it to score each step?

Intuition

Think of a sports team. The actor chooses the plays. The critic watches each play and says whether it went better or worse than expected. The critic does not choose anything; it only keeps an honest running estimate of how good each situation is. After every play, the critic's surprise (how far the outcome was from its expectation) tells the actor which choices to repeat, and it also corrects the critic's own expectations.

Actor–critic methods do this with two learned functions. The actor is the policy \pi_\theta. The critic is a value estimate V_w(s). Their surprise is the TD error, which we met in the temporal-difference modules: the reward just received plus the discounted value of the next state, minus the value that was expected.

Build it up, one piece at a time

Step 1: the TD error. After taking action a_t in s_t, we observe r_{t+1} and s_{t+1}. The one-step target for the value of s_t is r_{t+1}+\gamma V(s_{t+1}), so the TD error is

\delta_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)\qquad(\text{with }V(\text{terminal})=0)

Step 2: the TD error estimates the advantage. Taking the expectation given (s_t,a_t): \mathbb E[\delta_t\mid s_t,a_t]=\mathbb E[r_{t+1}+\gamma V(s_{t+1})\mid s_t,a_t]-V(s_t)=Q^\pi(s_t,a_t)-V^\pi(s_t)=A^\pi(s_t,a_t) when V=V^\pi. So the TD error is a one-sample advantage, with no need to wait for the episode to end. This is the link to the baseline lesson.

Step 3: the two updates. The actor moves its probability in the direction the advantage suggests, and the critic moves its estimate towards the target:

\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha_a\,\delta_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t),\qquad w\leftarrow w+\alpha_c\,\delta_t\,\nabla_w V_w(s_t)

In the tabular case V_w(s) is simply a table, and the critic's update is V(s_t)\leftarrow V(s_t)+\alpha_c\delta_t. The two updates run after every step, so learning does not wait for the episode to finish.

Step 4: the price of bootstrapping. Replacing the true return by r+\gamma V(s') reduces variance, because it depends only on one step of randomness. But it is biased while V is wrong, since the critic's own errors feed into the target. Multi-step versions interpolate: the n-step advantage uses n real rewards and then bootstraps, and generalised advantage estimation (GAE) averages the TD errors with a weight \lambda that trades bias for variance.

Worked example: one step at S, then one at M

A two-step task: from S choose Up (to M, reward 0) or Down (to a terminal state). From M one step gives reward +1 and ends. Take \gamma=0.9, step sizes \alpha_c=\alpha_a=0.3, initial critic values V(S)=0.2, V(M)=0.5, and an actor with logits z=(0,0), so \pi(\text{Up}\mid S)=0.5. Suppose the agent samples Up.

At S. The reward is 0 and the next state is M:

\delta_S=0+0.9\,V(M)-V(S)=0.9(0.5)-0.2=0.25

The critic moves: V(S)\leftarrow0.2+0.3(0.25)=0.275. The actor's score for Up is \nabla_z\log\pi(\text{Up})=(1-0.5,\ -0.5)=(0.5,-0.5), so \Delta z=0.3(0.25)(0.5,-0.5)=(0.0375,-0.0375). The new probability is \pi(\text{Up}\mid S)=\sigma(0.075)=0.51874. Up was better than expected, so it became more likely.

At M. The reward is +1 and the episode ends, so the target is 1:

\delta_M=1+0-V(M)=1-0.5=0.5,\qquad V(M)\leftarrow0.5+0.3(0.5)=0.65

M has only one action, so the score is zero and only the critic moves. The critic is now closer to the true values V(M)=1 and V(S)=\gamma V^\pi(M)=0.9 under the policy that picks Up.

Formal definition

\delta_t=r_{t+1}+\gamma V_w(s_{t+1})-V_w(s_t),\qquad \theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha_a\,\delta_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t),\qquad w\leftarrow w+\alpha_c\,\delta_t\,\nabla_w V_w(s_t)
\pi_\thetathe actor: the policy that chooses actions
V_wthe critic: a value estimate with parameters w
\delta_tthe TD error, a one-sample estimate of the advantage at step t
\alpha_a,\ \alpha_cthe actor and critic step sizes; the critic is often set larger so it tracks the policy
\gammadiscount factor

Conditions: convergence of actor–critic to a local optimum requires the critic to learn on a faster time scale than the actor (or to be accurate enough), and the usual step-size conditions. These are not guaranteed in deep networks, which is one reason for the stabilisers in the next lesson.

Common traps
  • Forgetting the terminal value. At the end of an episode the next value is zero. Bootstrapping from a terminal state's learned value is a bug.
  • Two critics that disagree. Actor and critic share the same environment; if the critic is learned with a different target, the advantage can be meaningless.
  • Thinking the TD error is the reward. δ is the surprise: reward plus next value minus current value. A large reward can still give a small δ when it was expected.
  • Ignoring the time scale. If the actor moves much faster than the critic, the advantage describes an old policy and the update points the wrong way.
Where it shows up in AI

A2C (advantage actor–critic) runs many environments in parallel and uses n-step advantages with a shared network for actor and critic. A3C (asynchronous advantage actor–critic) does the same with asynchronous workers. Most continuous-control and game-playing agents, and the value heads of language-model RL, are actor–critic in this sense.

Pipeline placement. Interact stage (one step or a few steps per environment), learn stage (the TD error and both updates, every few steps rather than per episode). Cost: one critic forward pass per step plus one actor update per batch. Stores: the actor's parameters, the critic's parameters or table, and a short buffer of recent transitions.

Check yourself

1. In the worked example, what would δ_S be if the agent had sampled Down instead, with r_D = 0.3 and the terminal value zero?
δ_S = r_D + γ·0 − V(S) = 0.3 + 0 − 0.2 = 0.1. The actor would then lower the logit of Up, because a positive TD error on Down raises Down's probability.
2. Why is E[δ_t | s_t, a_t] equal to the advantage when V is exact?
E[δ | s, a] = E[r + γV(s′) | s, a] − V(s) = Q^π(s, a) − V^π(s), because Q^π(s, a) is by definition the expected reward plus the discounted value of the next state. Subtracting V^π(s) gives the advantage.
3. What does the critic gain over REINFORCE, and what does it lose?
It gains updates after every step, without waiting for episode ends, and lower variance because the target depends on one step of randomness. It loses unbiasedness, since its target uses the critic's own (possibly wrong) estimates.

Takeaways

  • An actor chooses and a critic evaluates; the critic's TD error is a one-step advantage estimate.
  • Learning happens after every step, which makes actor–critic faster than REINFORCE, at the cost of bias from bootstrapping.
  • The step sizes of the two parts must be set so that the critic keeps up with the actor.
6.6

Proximal policy optimisation

The questionOur advantage estimates say which actions to reinforce, but how far should one update move the policy? A step that is too large can destroy a good policy in one batch. A step that is too small wastes the data we collected. How do we reuse a batch of experience for several updates while keeping each new policy close to the one that collected the data?

Intuition

Imagine adjusting a wobbly table by turning one leg. A large turn may tip it over; many small turns are safe but slow. The classic remedy is a trust region: only move within a radius where the local map is still accurate. The policy's data were collected by the old policy, so the gradient is only trustworthy near the old policy. Trust regions (TRPO) enforce this with a hard constraint on the KL divergence. PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) gets most of the benefit with a much simpler device: it clips the objective so that there is no reward for moving the probability ratio too far.

Build it up, one piece at a time

Step 1: the probability ratio. Let \pi_{\text{old}} be the policy that collected the batch and \pi_\theta the policy we are optimising. For each sampled action define

r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}

At the start of the update r_t=1. A ratio of 1.3 means the new policy makes that action 30% more likely than the old one did.

Step 2: the surrogate objective. The importance-weighted advantage \mathbb E[r_t(\theta)A_t] has the same gradient as the policy gradient at \theta_{\text{old}}, so we can optimise it on the old batch. But nothing stops r_t from growing without limit. The clipped objective fixes that:

L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,A_t\big)\Big]

Step 3: read the minimum case by case. If A>0 and the ratio exceeds 1+\varepsilon, the clipped term is smaller, so the objective stops rewarding a further increase: the gradient is zero. If A>0 and the ratio is below 1-\varepsilon, the unclipped term is smaller and still gives a gradient, so the policy is free to make a good action more likely. The mirror image holds for A<0: pushing the ratio below 1-\varepsilon earns nothing more, but pushing it up is still penalised. In every case the clip removes the incentive to move far, and it never adds a reward for moving in the wrong direction.

Step 4: several epochs on one batch. Because the clip limits how far the policy is rewarded for moving, the same batch can be used for several gradient steps (epochs and minibatches). That reuse is most of PPO's efficiency gain over vanilla policy gradients.

Worked example: four ratios and their advantages

Use \varepsilon=0.2, so the band is [0.8,\,1.2]. Compute the two terms and their minimum:

ratio radvantage Ar·Aclip(r)·Amingradient flows?
1.322.62.4 (clip at 1.2)2.4no: the clipped term is the minimum
0.921.81.81.8yes: inside the band
0.7−1.5−1.05−1.2 (clip at 0.8)−1.2no: the clipped term is the minimum
0.9−1.5−1.35−1.35−1.35yes: inside the band

Row 1 is the case the clip is designed for: the action is good, the policy has already made it 30% more likely, and the objective gives no further reward. Row 3 is the mirror case, where a bad action has already been made 30% less likely and the objective stops pushing it down. Rows 2 and 4 are inside the band, and they behave like ordinary policy gradients.

Formal definition

L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_t\big[\min(r_tA_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)A_t)\big],\qquad r_t=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}
r_t(\theta)probability ratio of the new to the old policy for the action taken at step t
A_tadvantage estimate at step t (for example from GAE, computed with the critic)
\varepsilonclip range, typically 0.1 to 0.2; it sets the band [1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]
\operatorname{clip}(x,a,b)x limited to the interval [a, b]
\theta_{\text{old}}the parameters that collected the batch, fixed during the update

Full PPO usually optimises L^{\text{CLIP}}-c_1L^{\text{VF}}+c_2\,\mathcal S[\pi_\theta], adding a value-function loss for the critic and an entropy bonus that keeps the policy from collapsing too early. The clip is not a hard constraint: a single gradient step can still overshoot the band, and different samples can move the ratio in different directions. PPO is therefore an approximate trust region, and implementations often monitor the KL divergence between the old and new policy and stop the epochs early if it grows too large.

Common traps
  • Thinking the clip is a trust region. It removes the incentive to move far, but it does not bound the KL divergence. Monitor the KL in practice.
  • Forgetting the sign of the advantage. The clip is one-sided for each sign. Clipping the wrong side makes the objective reward the wrong direction.
  • Stale ratios. \pi_{\text{old}} must be the policy that collected the data, and the log-probabilities must be stored at collection time, not recomputed from the current weights.
  • Believing PPO is always stable. Its results depend on the advantage estimates, normalisation, value-loss weight and clip range, and on the seed. Run several seeds before drawing conclusions.
Where it shows up in AI

PPO is the default policy-gradient method in many research and industry settings. It trains locomotion policies for simulated and real legged robots, and it is the algorithm behind RLHF in several language-model systems, where the reward model scores sampled responses and a KL penalty to a reference model is added to the objective.

Pipeline placement. Interact stage (collect a batch with the current policy and store log-probabilities), learn stage (several epochs of minibatch updates on the clipped objective and a value loss), then evaluate and deploy stage (the trained policy runs at the control rate). Stores: the rollout buffer (states, actions, old log-probabilities, advantages, returns), and the policy and value weights.

Check yourself

1. With ε = 0.2, what is L^CLIP for r = 1.5 and A = 2?
r·A = 3.0 and clip(1.5)·A = 1.2 × 2 = 2.4. The minimum is 2.4, the clipped term. The gradient with respect to θ is zero for this sample, because the ratio is above 1 + ε and the advantage is positive.
2. Why does PPO store the old log-probabilities at collection time rather than recomputing them during the epochs?
The ratio is defined against the policy that collected the batch, π_old. If the old probabilities were recomputed with the current weights, the ratio would be 1 at every epoch and the clip would never act. Storing them keeps the reference fixed across the epochs.
3. With A < 0 and r = 0.7, ε = 0.2, why is the gradient zero, and what would happen if the ratio were 0.9 instead?
The unclipped term is −1.05 and the clipped term is 0.8 × (−1.5) = −1.2; the minimum is −1.2, the clipped one, so the gradient is zero. The action has already been made 30% less likely, and the objective stops rewarding further decrease. At r = 0.9 both terms are −1.35 and the gradient flows, since the ratio is still inside the band.

Takeaways

  • The probability ratio lets one batch of experience train a new policy, as long as that policy is close to the one that collected the batch.
  • PPO's clipped objective removes the reward for moving the ratio beyond 1 ± ε in the favourable direction, which limits the step without a constrained optimiser.
  • The clip is an approximate trust region: it helps a lot, but it does not guarantee small KL divergence, so monitor it.
