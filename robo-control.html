Control sits in the Control stage of the robot pipeline. An estimator tells the controller where the robot is; the controller compares that with where it should be and sends a command to a motor, many times a second. Every lesson below says what the loop rate is and what gets stored.

Units are given everywhere: metres (m), seconds (s), radians (rad), newtons (N). Each lab is a small illustrative model, not a robot. Its numbers come from the same equations as the lesson, so the readouts can be checked by hand.

6.1

Feedback control & stability intuition

The questionA wheel motor must turn at a setpoint speed of 1 rad/s. Its speed responds slowly to the voltage you send, and a load can push it off target at any time. What voltage should you send, and how do you keep the speed at 1 rad/s when you cannot predict the load?

Intuition

Think of adjusting a shower tap. You do not work out the right tap angle in advance. You feel the water, compare it with the temperature you want, and turn the tap a little in the direction of the difference. When the water is too cold you turn it up; when it is too hot you turn it down. You keep watching and correcting. That loop, measure, compare, act, is feedback.

The loop has four named parts. The setpoint r is what you want. The output y is what you measure. The error e = r - y is the gap between them. The controller turns the error into a command u, and the plant is the thing being controlled: the motor, the joint or the wheel.

Compare this with open loop: you pick the voltage from a model and never look at the result. Open loop works only if the model is exact. Real motors are never exact. Their resistance changes with temperature, and the load changes without warning. Feedback does not need the model to be exact, because it reads the answer and corrects it.

Build it up: from open loop to feedback

Step 1: a plant. Take a simple motor whose speed y follows the command u with a lag. A first-order model says

\tau\,\dot y = -y + b\,u

Here \tau is the time constant: how long the speed takes to get most of the way to its new value. With \tau = 2 s, a step in u takes a few seconds to show up in y. The gain b says how much output one unit of input produces at steady state. Here b = 1, so u and y share units.

Step 2: open loop fails. Suppose the true motor gain is b = 0.8, not 1, and we send u = r = 1. The steady speed settles at 0.8 \cdot 1 = 0.8, which is 20% short. Nothing in the controller knows this, so nothing corrects it.

Step 3: proportional feedback. Instead of sending r, send a command proportional to the error: u = K e = K(r - y). If the speed is low, the error is large and the command grows. As y approaches r, the command shrinks. This is called proportional (P) control, and K is its gain.

Worked example: P control on the motor

Use \tau = 2 s, b = 1, r = 1 and K = 3. Substitute u = 3(1 - y) into the plant:

2\dot y = -y + 3(1-y) = -4y + 3 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot y = -2y + 1.5

The steady state is where \dot y = 0: y_\infty = 1.5/2 = 0.75. The error that never goes away is 1 - 0.75 = 0.25. The speed rises with time constant 1/2 = 0.5 s, so at t = 0.5 s the output is 0.75\,(1 - e^{-1}) \approx 0.474.

Now the disturbance. Add a load so that \tau\dot y = -y + u + d with d = 0.2. With no feedback and u = 1, the steady speed is 1 + 0.2 = 1.2: the load moves the output by 0.2. With feedback, the steady speed is (3 + 0.2)/4 = 0.80. The load now moves the output by only 0.80 - 0.75 = 0.05, four times less. The factor is 1 + Kb = 4.

There is a catch. The error never reaches zero: e_\infty = 1/(1+Kb) shrinks as K grows but never vanishes for a finite gain. Lesson 6.2 fixes this with an integral term. There is a second catch as well, and it is more serious: feedback acts on a reading that arrives late.

The formal definition

\tau\,\dot y = -y + b\,K\,(r - y)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tau\,\dot y = -(1 + bK)\,y + bK\,r
y(t)output (measured), for example speed in rad/s
rsetpoint, in the same units as y
e = r − yerror: how far the output is from the setpoint
u = K eproportional command; K is the gain (units of u per unit of y)
τplant time constant, seconds
bplant gain: steady output per unit input (b = 1 here)
y∞ = bK r/(1 + bK)steady state of the closed loop
τ/(1 + bK)closed-loop time constant, seconds: smaller than τ, so feedback is faster

Two things hold for any plant of this form. Feedback shrinks the effect of disturbances by the factor 1 + bK. Its time constant is shorter, so the system responds faster. The price is the steady error 1/(1+bK), and the risk of delay, described next.

Lab

Try this
  1. Set K = 1 with no delay. The curve settles at 0.5, and the readout gives K/(1+K) = 0.5. Error 0.5.
  2. Move K to 3, then 8. The steady state rises towards 1 (0.75, then 0.89), but never reaches it. Notice that the time constant gets shorter.
  3. Set the delay L to 1 s with K = 1. The curve still settles. Now raise K to 3. The output overshoots and keeps ringing: the controller keeps correcting a reading that is already old.
  4. Set L = 2 s with K = 3. The loop becomes unstable and the output grows. Raising gain can make a loop worse, not better.
Common traps
  • “Feedback removes all error.” Not with proportional control alone. The steady error is 1/(1+bK), which is only small when K is large.
  • “More gain is always better.” Gain shortens the time constant but amplifies delay and noise. Past a limit the loop rings or diverges, as the lab shows.
  • Mixing units. If u is in volts and y in rad/s, the gain K has units of V per rad/s. Write the units down before you read a number off a plot.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Every joint of a robot arm runs a feedback loop: encoder in, torque or velocity command out, at a rate of hundreds of hertz.
  • Learned policies do not replace this loop. They usually send target joint positions to a lower-level feedback controller, which tracks them.
  • The same idea, measure the error and act on it, is the reason gradient descent works in training. There the “plant” is the loss function.

Check yourself

1. A plant has τ = 2 s and b = 1. You use P control with K = 1 and r = 1. What is the steady-state output and the steady error?
y∞ = bK r/(1 + bK) = 1·1/(1+1) = 0.5. The steady error is 1 − 0.5 = 0.5. Doubling the gain to K = 3 would reduce it to 0.25.
2. Open loop with u = 1 and a true gain b = 0.8 gives what steady output? Would feedback with K = 3 fix this?
Open loop gives 0.8. With feedback, y∞ = bK/(1 + bK) = 2.4/3.4 ≈ 0.706. The gain 0.8 is still wrong, but the error is now set by K, so raising K shrinks it. The controller corrects a model error it never saw directly.
3. Why does a large gain make a delayed loop oscillate?
The controller reacts to the output as it was L seconds ago. A big correction is applied while the output is still on its way, so the system overshoots, and the next correction overshoots the other way. If the gain is high enough, each overshoot is bigger than the last.
Pipeline viewStage: Control. Loop rate: a motor or joint loop typically runs at about 100 Hz to 1 kHz, so a delay of a few milliseconds matters. Stored: the gain K and the time constant used for tuning, and logs of the setpoint and output.

Takeaways

  • Feedback measures the output, compares it with the setpoint and acts on the error, so it does not need an exact model.
  • For a first-order plant with P control the steady state is bK r/(1+bK), so an offset remains; the disturbance effect shrinks by 1 + bK.
  • Delay makes large gains dangerous. Stability is a property of the whole loop, not of the controller alone.
6.2

PID control

The questionA robot joint must hold a position, and it must also cancel a constant gravity load. Proportional control leaves a steady error (lesson 6.1). How do we remove that error and still keep the joint from overshooting, when the motor can only supply a limited torque?

Intuition

Steering a boat toward a harbour uses three quantities at once. You turn the tiller in proportion to how far off course you are now. If a steady cross-current keeps pushing you sideways, you add a little extra turn for as long as the error has been building up: that is the integral, a memory of past error. And as you get close to the harbour you ease off the tiller because you are closing in fast: that is the derivative, which responds to how quickly the error is changing.

These three ideas give the PID controller: P for now, I for the past, D for the trend. Most industrial motor and joint loops run a PID, often with the derivative term switched off or filtered.

Build it up: one step by hand

Continuous time gives the formula

u(t) = K_p\,e(t) + K_i\!\int_0^t e(s)\,ds + K_d\,\frac{de}{dt}

A computer works in samples. Take a sample time \Delta t, and replace the integral by a running sum and the derivative by a difference. The discrete form is:

e_k = r - y_k,\qquad I_k = I_{k-1} + e_k\,\Delta t,\qquad D_k = \frac{e_k - e_{k-1}}{\Delta t},\qquad u_k = K_p e_k + K_i I_k + K_d D_k

To see every term at work, use a made-up discrete plant that moves the output y towards the command u each sample: y_{k+1} = 0.9\,y_k + 0.1\,u_k. Set r = 1, y_0 = 0, K_p = 2, K_i = 0.5, K_d = 0.2 and \Delta t = 0.5 s. At the first sample there is no previous error, so D_0 = 0.

Worked example: three PID steps
ky_ke_kI_kK_p e_kK_i I_kD_kK_d D_ku_ky_{k+1}
0010.52.0000.250002.2500.225
10.2250.7750.88751.5500.444−0.450−0.0901.9040.393
20.3930.6071.1911.2140.596−0.336−0.0671.7430.528

Look at the first row. The proportional term gives 2 \cdot 1 = 2. The integral has accumulated 1 \cdot 0.5 = 0.5 and contributes 0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.25. The command is u_0 = 2.25, which moves the output to 0.9\cdot 0 + 0.1\cdot 2.25 = 0.225.

In row 1, the error fell from 1 to 0.775, so D_1 = (0.775 - 1)/0.5 = -0.45. The derivative is negative: the output is catching up. It reduces the command by 0.2\cdot 0.45 = 0.09. The integral is still growing, so its contribution is up to 0.444. The sum of the three terms is u_1 = 1.550 + 0.444 - 0.090 = 1.904. Each row reuses the previous I and e, which is why the controller needs only a little memory.

Notice that the integral keeps the command above the proportional value even as the error shrinks. That is what removes the steady error of lesson 6.1. Over a long run the integral holds the torque needed to cancel a constant load.

The formal definition

e(t) = r − y(t)error, in the units of the output
K_pproportional gain, units of u per unit of e
K_iintegral gain, units of u per (unit of e × second), i.e. 1/s when u and y share units
K_dderivative gain, seconds (units of u per unit of e/s)
I_krunning sum of the error: the controller's memory
Δtsample time, seconds (here 0.5 s)
u_sat = clip(u, −u_max, u_max)the actuator can supply at most u_max; the command above the limit is lost

Windup. If the actuator saturates, the command you apply is smaller than the command the controller asked for, but the integral keeps adding up the error. When the error finally reverses, the integral is huge, and the output overshoots badly. The fix is anti-windup: when the applied command differs from the computed one, feed the difference back into the integral so that it stops growing. The lab adds a back-calculation term K_{aw}(u_{sat} - u) to the integrator's rate.

Lab

Try this
  1. Set K_i = 0 and K_d = 0 with the limit at 5. The output settles below the setpoint: this is the offset of proportional control.
  2. Raise K_i to 0.6 with the limit at 5. The offset disappears. Notice the integral term rising in the bars.
  3. Set K_i = 2 with the limit at 1.5 and anti-windup off. The output overshoots by about 17%: the integral wound up while the actuator was clipped. Switch anti-windup on and the overshoot falls to about 6%.
  4. With K_i = 2, the limit at 5 and anti-windup off, the overshoot is about 8%. Raise K_d to 1: it rises to about 15%. Watch the derivative bar: it is negative while the output rises, so it opposes motion, but on this lagging plant it lets the integral act for longer. Derivative action is not a cure-all; it also amplifies noise.
Common traps
  • Derivative on noisy signals. The derivative of an encoder that jumps by one count every few samples is a spike. Real controllers filter the derivative or use a low-pass filter.
  • Derivative kick. If the derivative acts on the setpoint, a step change in the target gives a huge spike. Acting on the measured output instead avoids it.
  • Windup. Any actuator with a limit needs anti-windup, or a long saturation will cause a large overshoot.
  • Units of the gains. K_i is per second and K_d is in seconds; mixing them up changes the controller completely.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Motor speed and joint position loops in almost every robot run PID, often on a microcontroller at 1 kHz.
  • Legged and humanoid robots frequently send joint position targets to PD controllers (proportional plus derivative) at high rates. Learned locomotion policies output those targets, and the PD loop tracks them.
  • Learned controllers are trained against simulators that model windup, saturation and delay, so that what they learn survives the real actuator.

Check yourself

1. With K_p = 2, K_i = 0 and K_d = 0, what is the command when the error is 0.5?
u = K_p e = 2 · 0.5 = 1.0. With only proportional action, the command is zero when the error is zero, which is why an offset remains when a steady load is present.
2. The actuator limit is 1.5 and the controller asks for 2.25. What is applied, and what goes wrong if the integral keeps growing?
1.5 is applied. The integral keeps summing the error, so when the error changes sign the integral is large and the output overshoots. Anti-windup stops the integral from growing while the actuator is clipped.
3. Why is the derivative usually taken on the measured output rather than on the error when the setpoint can jump?
A step in the setpoint produces an instantaneous change in the error, so the derivative of the error is a spike, called a kick. The derivative of the measured output does not jump with the setpoint, so the command stays smooth.
Pipeline viewStage: Control, the inner loop under the planner. Loop rate: typically 200 Hz to 1 kHz on a microcontroller or real-time CPU. Stored: the gains Kp, Ki, Kd, the actuator limits, and logs of error and command for tuning.

Takeaways

  • P reacts to the error now, I to the error accumulated so far, D to how fast the error changes.
  • The integral removes a steady offset but can wind up when the actuator saturates; anti-windup stops this.
  • Tune by hand in order: raise Kp until the response is fast but starts to oscillate, add Ki to remove the offset, and use Kd only if it helps, since it also amplifies noise.
6.3

State-space models & LQR

The questionA cart on a rail has a position x and a velocity v, and a motor can push it with a force u. Position and velocity are linked: velocity changes position, and the push changes velocity. How do we choose the push as a function of both x and v so that the cart returns to zero quickly without pushing too hard?

Intuition

To predict what a system will do next, you need its state: the smallest list of numbers that, together with the input, determines the future. For the cart that list is (x, v). Knowing only the position is not enough, because a cart at the origin moving fast will not stay there. A controller that looks only at x would be blind to the momentum.

A state feedback law pushes back on both numbers. A term proportional to x is like a spring pulling towards zero. A term proportional to v is like a brake that opposes motion. Choosing the two gains by hand is possible for one cart, but it becomes guesswork for an arm with six joints. LQR (linear quadratic regulator) chooses them for you, by minimising a cost that adds up how far the state is from zero and how much push you spend.

Build it up: the double integrator

Step 1: the equations. With unit mass, the cart obeys \ddot x = u. Write the state as \mathbf x = (x, v)^\top. Then the state changes as

\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x + B u,\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}

The first row says the position changes at the velocity; the second says the velocity changes at the input. This is a double integrator, the simplest model of a moving body with a force input.

Step 2: state feedback. Choose u = -K\mathbf x = -(k_1 x + k_2 v). Substituting, the closed loop is \dot{\mathbf x} = (A - BK)\mathbf x. For this cart the characteristic equation is s^2 + k_2 s + k_1 = 0. Any choice with k_1 > 0 and k_2 > 0 gives a stable cart, and the two numbers set the natural frequency and damping.

Step 3: the cost. LQR minimises

J = \int_0^\infty \left(\mathbf x^\top Q\,\mathbf x + r\,u^2\right)dt,\qquad Q=\begin{pmatrix}q_1&0\\0&q_2\end{pmatrix}

Large q_1 punishes position error, large q_2 punishes speed, and large r punishes effort. The optimal gain is K = R^{-1}B^\top P, where P solves the algebraic Riccati equation A^\top P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\top P + Q = 0.

Worked example: q₁ = q₂ = r = 1

Guess P = \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3&1\\1&\sqrt3\end{pmatrix} and check the Riccati equation. Compute A^\top P = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\\sqrt3&1\end{pmatrix} and PA = \begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt3\\0&1\end{pmatrix}. The sum is \begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt3\\\sqrt3&2\end{pmatrix}. Next PB = (1, \sqrt3)^\top, so PBB^\top P = \begin{pmatrix}1&\sqrt3\\\sqrt3&3\end{pmatrix}. Adding Q = I:

\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt3\\\sqrt3&2\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}1&\sqrt3\\\sqrt3&3\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}

So P is correct, and the gain is K = B^\top P = (1, \sqrt3), i.e. k_1 = 1 and k_2 = \sqrt3 \approx 1.732. The closed-loop equation is s^2 + \sqrt3\,s + 1 = 0: the natural frequency is \omega_n = 1 rad/s and the damping ratio is \zeta = \sqrt3/2 \approx 0.866. The cart returns with a very small overshoot. Starting at x = 1 m, the position at 1 s is about 0.72 m, and it is near 0 by about 5 s.

For this particular double integrator there is a closed form: with q_1, q_2, r general, k_1 = \sqrt{q_1/r} and k_2 = \sqrt{(q_2 + 2\sqrt{q_1 r})/r}. The lab uses it. For a general system with many states you solve the Riccati equation numerically with a library routine; the structure stays the same.

The formal definition

\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x + Bu,\qquad u = -K\mathbf x,\qquad K = R^{-1}B^\top P,\qquad A^\top P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^\top P + Q = 0
x = (x, v)ᵀstate: position (m) and velocity (m/s)
uinput: force divided by mass, here m/s²
A, Bsystem matrices: how the state and input enter the dynamics
Kstate-feedback gain (row vector), chosen by LQR
Q, Rcost weights: Q is positive semi-definite, R positive definite
Psolution of the Riccati equation; its quadratic form xᵀPx is the optimal cost-to-go
ωn, ζnatural frequency (rad/s) and damping ratio (dimensionless) of the closed loop

Two conditions matter. The pair (A, B) must be controllable, meaning the input can reach every state; here that holds because B pushes the velocity, which then moves the position. The solution P is the one that makes the closed loop stable, and it is unique under those conditions.

Lab

Try this
  1. Keep the defaults. Notice the small overshoot and the readout ζ = 0.866. Raise q₁ to 5: the cart returns faster, and the spring gain k₁ grows.
  2. Set r to 10. The effort is expensive, so the gains shrink, the push is gentler and the cart returns more slowly. Check the trace of u (purple) against the position.
  3. Set q₂ to 8 and watch the damping: the cart brakes harder and the overshoot disappears (ζ rises above 1).
  4. Move the start position to −2 m and compare the peak push with 1 m. The push is proportional to the state, so it grows with the starting distance.
Common traps
  • “LQR is optimal, so it is safe.” It is optimal only for the cost you wrote. It ignores actuator limits and any obstacle, so it can ask for a push the motor cannot give.
  • Measuring the full state. u = -K\mathbf x needs every state. If you only measure position, you must estimate the velocity first (Module 3).
  • Using a linear model far from its operating point. A and B describe the system near one configuration. Far from it the gain can be wrong.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Balancing robots, quadrotor hover and inverted pendulums use LQR or its close relatives as the inner stabiliser.
  • A linear state-feedback law is a policy: a map from state to action. Reinforcement learning generalises LQR to nonlinear policies and unknown models, with the same idea of a cost to minimise.
  • Learned policies for legged robots are often trained around a nominal controller of this kind, so that the network learns a correction rather than the whole balance.

Check yourself

1. Using the closed form, what are k₁ and k₂ for q₁ = 4, q₂ = 1 and r = 1?
k₁ = √(q₁/r) = √4 = 2. k₂ = √((q₂ + 2√(q₁ r))/r) = √((1 + 2·2)/1) = √5 ≈ 2.236.
2. With q₁ = q₂ = 1 and r = 4, what are the gains and the damping ratio?
k₁ = √(1/4) = 0.5. k₂ = √((1 + 2√4)/4) = √(5/4) ≈ 1.118. ζ = k₂/(2√k₁) = 1.118/(2·0.707) ≈ 0.79. A larger r makes the gains smaller, so the response is gentler and slower.
3. Why must the position and velocity both enter the feedback law rather than position alone?
Position alone gives no information about momentum. A cart moving quickly through the origin would be treated as at rest, and it would overshoot. The velocity term acts as damping, and without it the closed loop oscillates or is less stable.
Pipeline viewStages: Estimate provides the state (x, v), and Control computes u = −Kx. Loop rate: typically 100 Hz to 1 kHz for a real cart or quadrotor. Stored: the matrices A and B from identification, the weights Q and R, and the gain K.

Takeaways

  • The state is the smallest set of numbers that determines the future; for a cart it is position and velocity.
  • State feedback u = −Kx gives one gain per state. LQR chooses K by minimising a quadratic cost with weights Q and R.
  • The weights are design choices: they trade speed against effort. LQR assumes a linear model and ignores limits.
6.4

Stability & Lyapunov functions

The questionA controller brings a pendulum back to the bottom in your simulation from one starting angle. A robot must work from every starting angle it could be in, and nobody can simulate them all. How can we prove, for every start in a region, that the state settles to the target?

Intuition

Picture a marble in a bowl. The bottom of the bowl is a point where the marble can rest. Wherever you release it, the marble rolls down, loses height and eventually settles at the bottom. Height never increases, so the marble can never climb back out on its own. The bottom is stable.

Aleksandr Lyapunov (1892) turned this picture into a method. Find a function V(x) that measures "height" above the target: it is zero at the target and positive everywhere else, like energy. If V never increases along any trajectory, the state cannot escape to a larger "height", and it must settle. You do not need to solve the equations at all. You only need to check the sign of how V changes.

Build it up: a damped pendulum

Step 1: the model. A pendulum of unit length, with gravity scaled so that g/L = 1, angle \theta measured from the hanging position, and angular speed \omega = \dot\theta in rad/s. Friction slows it with a damping coefficient c:

\dot\theta = \omega,\qquad \dot\omega = -\sin\theta - c\,\omega

The state (\theta, \omega) = (0, 0) is the hanging position, where the pendulum rests. The upright position (\pi, 0) is also a rest point, but it is unstable: a pendulum balanced on top falls off if nudged.

Step 2: energy. Pick V(\theta,\omega) = (1 - \cos\theta) + \tfrac12\omega^2. The first term is the potential energy (height) and the second is the kinetic energy. V is zero at the hanging position and positive elsewhere in a band around it, which is what a Lyapunov function must do.

Step 3: how V changes. Along a trajectory, \dot V = \frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}\dot\theta + \frac{\partial V}{\partial\omega}\dot\omega. Here \partial V/\partial\theta = \sin\theta and \partial V/\partial\omega = \omega, so

\dot V = \sin\theta\cdot\omega + \omega\,(-\sin\theta - c\,\omega) = -c\,\omega^2

The gravity terms cancel exactly. What is left is the friction, which only removes energy. For c \ge 0, \dot V \le 0 everywhere: energy never grows.

Worked example: energy at two points

Start at \theta = 1 rad, \omega = 0, with c = 0.3. Then V = 1 - \cos 1 = 0.4597, and \dot V = -0.3\cdot 0 = 0. The pendulum is at rest at its highest point, so energy is not yet leaving; it starts to leave as soon as it moves.

Now take \theta = 0.5 rad and \omega = 0.5 rad/s. V = (1 - \cos 0.5) + \tfrac12(0.25) = 0.1224 + 0.1250 = 0.2474, and \dot V = -0.3\cdot 0.25 = -0.075 per second. Energy is falling at 0.075 units per second, as expected from friction.

Step 4: from "V decreases" to "the pendulum settles". The argument so far gives \dot V \le 0, which is not quite enough: \dot V = 0 whenever \omega = 0, even away from the bottom. The missing step is LaSalle's invariance principle. A trajectory that stays with \dot V = 0 for all time must have \omega \equiv 0, and then \dot\omega = -\sin\theta = 0, which forces \theta to be 0 or \pi. Only the hanging position can be a trajectory that stays put, so every trajectory must eventually reach it.

The formal definition

\dot x = f(x),\quad x^*=0 \text{ with } f(0)=0;\qquad V(0)=0,\ V(x)>0 \text{ for } x\ne 0,\ \dot V(x) = \nabla V(x)\cdot f(x)\le 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{stable}
ẋ = f(x)the system: the rate of change of the state x
x*an equilibrium: a state where the system does not move
V(x)Lyapunov function: zero at the equilibrium, positive elsewhere (a generalised energy)
V̇ = ∇V·fhow V changes along trajectories; ∇V is the vector of partial derivatives
stable / asymptotically stableV̇ ≤ 0 gives stability; V̇ < 0 away from x* (or LaSalle's argument) gives convergence

The method is sufficient, not necessary. If you find a Lyapunov function, the system is stable. If you do not, nothing has been proved either way: another function might work. For linear systems \dot x = Ax, the search is systematic. Choose a positive definite Q and solve A^\top P + PA = -Q for P; then V = x^\top P x works whenever P is positive definite.

Lab

Try this
  1. Keep c = 0.3 and start at 1 rad. Watch V(t) fall: the readout shows V̇ = −c·ω², which is zero at the turning points and largest at the bottom.
  2. Set c = 0. Energy is conserved: V stays flat and the pendulum swings forever. Stable, but not convergent. This is the difference between V̇ ≤ 0 and V̇ < 0.
  3. Set c to −0.1. Now V̇ = −c·ω² is positive: energy is pumped in, V rises and the swing grows. The hanging position is unstable.
  4. Start at 3 rad (almost upright) with c = 0.3. The energy starts high, so the pendulum takes longer to settle, but it still does.
Common traps
  • Forgetting positivity. V must be positive away from the equilibrium. A function that is zero at the target and negative somewhere else proves nothing.
  • Stopping at V̇ ≤ 0. That proves stability, not convergence. Add LaSalle or show V̇ < 0 except at the target.
  • Local versus global. The pendulum result holds in a region around the bottom (up to just below the top). A Lyapunov argument gives a region of attraction, not necessarily the whole space.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Safe control of robots uses Lyapunov functions to certify that a controller keeps the state inside a safe set.
  • Control Lyapunov functions are used to design controllers: choose a V and build the input so that V̇ is negative.
  • Learned controllers are checked against Lyapunov-style conditions; learning the function itself with neural networks is an active research area, not a finished toolbox.

Check yourself

1. With c = 0, what is V̇ and what does the pendulum do?
V̇ = −c ω² = 0. Energy is constant. The pendulum swings with constant amplitude forever: stable in the sense of never growing, but it does not converge to the bottom. This is why V̇ ≤ 0 is not enough on its own.
2. With c = −0.1 and ω = 1 rad/s, what is the sign of V̇, and what happens to the swing?
V̇ = −(−0.1)(1)² = +0.1 > 0. Energy grows at 0.1 units per second and the swing gets larger. Negative damping makes the hanging position unstable.
3. Why does failing to find a Lyapunov function not prove that a system is unstable?
The method is sufficient, not necessary. A stable system may have no simple energy-like function that the search happened to find. Instability needs its own argument, such as linearisation or a counterexample.
Pipeline viewStages: Control (design of the law) and Deploy & monitor (a runtime check on V). Loop rate: the design is done offline; a monitor evaluates V at the control rate, typically 100 Hz to 1 kHz. Stored: the chosen V, the safe-set bound and the logs of V for review.

Takeaways

  • A Lyapunov function is an energy-like measure that is zero at the target, positive elsewhere, and never increases along trajectories.
  • For the damped pendulum, V = 1 − cos θ + ω²/2 gives V̇ = −c ω², so V decreases for c > 0.
  • V̇ ≤ 0 gives stability; LaSalle's argument gives convergence. Finding no V proves nothing.
6.5

Model predictive control

The questionAn autonomous car must stop at a line 3 s ahead, and it must never accelerate harder than its tyres allow. A PID loop reacts only to the error now and cannot see the braking distance it will need. How can the controller plan several steps ahead, respect the limits, and still correct for surprises?

Intuition

A chess player does not pick a move by looking only at the current board. She imagines the next several moves, chooses the first move of the best sequence, and then looks again after her opponent replies. The plan is always re-made from the new position. Model predictive control (MPC) does exactly this for a robot. It predicts the next N steps with a model, chooses the whole sequence of inputs that gives the best result within the limits, applies only the first input, and then re-plans from the new measurement.

The word receding horizon names the window: it always spans the next N steps and moves forward with time. Because the plan is made again at every step, the controller reacts to surprises, even though each plan assumed the model was right.

Build it up: a two-step plan by hand

Step 1: the model. A point mass has position p (m) and velocity v (m/s). The input u is an acceleration (m/s²). With a sample time T_s, the exact discrete model is

p_{k+1} = p_k + T_s v_k + \tfrac12 T_s^2 u_k,\qquad v_{k+1} = v_k + T_s u_k

Step 2: the cost. We want the position to reach a target r and we want to avoid large accelerations. Over a horizon of N steps:

J = \sum_{k=1}^{N}(p_k - r)^2 + \rho\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}u_k^2,\qquad |u_k|\le u_{\max}
Worked example: N = 2, T_s = 1 s, r = 1 m, ρ = 0.1

Start at rest at the origin: p_0 = 0, v_0 = 0. Roll the model forward with the two inputs u_0, u_1 (T_s = 1 s):

p_1 = 0.5\,u_0,\quad v_1 = u_0,\quad p_2 = p_1 + v_1 + 0.5\,u_1 = 1.5\,u_0 + 0.5\,u_1

So J(u_0,u_1) = (0.5u_0 - 1)^2 + (1.5u_0 + 0.5u_1 - 1)^2 + 0.1(u_0^2 + u_1^2). Set both partial derivatives to zero. For u_0: 5.2\,u_0 + 1.5\,u_1 = 4. For u_1: 1.5\,u_0 + 0.7\,u_1 = 1. Solving gives

u_0 = \frac{1.3}{1.39} \approx 0.935,\qquad u_1 = \frac{-0.8}{1.39} \approx -0.576,\qquad J \approx 0.417

The plan reaches p_1 \approx 0.468 and p_2 \approx 1.115 m. Now apply the limit |u| \le 0.5. The first input of the free plan (0.935) is not allowed. Fix u_0 = 0.5 and minimise over u_1 alone: 0.7\,u_1 = 1 - 0.75 = 0.25, so u_1 = 0.357. The constrained plan is (0.5,\ 0.357) with J = 0.605. Only u_0 = 0.5 is applied; at the next step the whole problem is solved again from the new state.

Notice what changed when the limit appeared. The best unconstrained plan needs a sharp push at first and then a brake; the constrained plan pushes at the limit and corrects later. The controller did not need a new rule for the limit: the constraint is part of the optimisation.

The formal definition

\min_{u_0,\dots,u_{N-1}} J \quad\text{s.t.}\quad x_{k+1} = f(x_k,u_k),\ |u_k|\le u_{\max};\qquad \text{apply } u_0,\ \text{then re-solve at } k+1
p, vposition (m) and velocity (m/s); state x = (p, v)
u_kinput at step k: an acceleration in m/s²
T_ssample time, seconds
Nprediction horizon, in steps (N·T_s seconds ahead)
rtarget position, m
ρeffort weight: how much an acceleration costs compared with a position error
u_maxinput limit: the actuator's maximum acceleration, m/s²
Jcost of the whole plan; the solver minimises it over all N inputs at once

Two practical rules follow. The optimisation must finish inside one sample period, so the number of inputs N and the solver's speed set the control rate. And the model must be good enough over the horizon: a plan that is correct for 3 s is wasted if the robot's model is wrong by 10% at 1 s, although re-planning limits the damage.

Lab

Try this
  1. Keep the defaults (N = 6, limit 1 m/s², target 1 m). Watch the purple predicted path bend towards the target and flatten at the end of the horizon. Only the first arrow is applied.
  2. Set N to 1. The controller sees one step ahead and overshoots the target to about 1.5 m. Raise N to 12: the peak falls to about 1.03 m because the plan looks further ahead and brakes earlier.
  3. Set the limit to 0.2 m/s². The arrows are capped at that limit and the cart takes about 4 s to settle on the target, against about 2 s with the limit at 1 m/s².
  4. Set ρ to 0 and then 0.5. With no effort penalty the plan uses the largest allowed inputs; with ρ = 0.5 the cart moves more gently and settles at about 3 s instead of 2 s. Compare the arrow sizes.
Common traps
  • A horizon that is too short. If the braking distance is longer than N·Ts, the plan cannot see the stop and the controller overshoots.
  • Forgetting to re-plan. Applying the whole plan without measuring again is open-loop control with extra steps.
  • A solve that is too slow. If the optimiser takes longer than the sample period, the command arrives late. Real-time MPC is a computing problem as much as a control problem.
  • Assuming stability for free. A finite horizon without a terminal cost or constraint can make the closed loop unstable. Stability guarantees need extra terms.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Autonomous driving planners solve a short-horizon optimisation of steering and acceleration many times a second, under comfort and collision constraints.
  • Legged robots use MPC over their centre-of-mass motion to keep balance while stepping, with constraints on contact forces.
  • Learned policies are sometimes trained to imitate an MPC expert, so that the network reproduces constrained behaviour without solving an optimisation on the robot.

Check yourself

1. For the N = 2 example, what is the cost J for the plan u = (0, 0) with r = 1?
p₁ = 0 and p₂ = 0, so J = (0 − 1)² + (0 − 1)² + 0.1(0 + 0) = 2. The free plan reaches J ≈ 0.417, much better, so a plan that pushes is worth its effort.
2. Why apply only the first input and re-plan, rather than apply the whole plan?
The model is imperfect and disturbances occur. Measuring again and re-solving corrects the plan with real information. Applying the whole sequence would be open loop over the horizon.
3. In the N = 2 example with the limit |u| ≤ 0.5, why is u₀ = 0.935 not allowed, and what is applied instead?
It exceeds the actuator limit of 0.5. The constrained optimum sets u₀ = 0.5 (at the limit) and chooses u₁ = 0.357 for the rest of the plan. Only u₀ = 0.5 is applied; the rest is recomputed at the next step.
Pipeline viewStages: Plan (the optimisation over a short horizon) and Control (the first input sent to the actuator). Loop rate: the solve typically runs at about 10 Hz to 100 Hz for vehicles and legged robots, with the actuator loop running faster underneath. Stored: the model, the horizon, the weights and limits, and the planned trajectory for logging.

Takeaways

  • MPC predicts N steps with a model, optimises the whole input sequence under limits, applies only the first input and re-plans.
  • Constraints are part of the optimisation, so the controller respects limits without a special rule for them.
  • The horizon, the model accuracy and the solve time together decide whether MPC works on a real robot.
6.6

Force & impedance control

The questionA robot arm must push a peg into a hole, or a person can bump into it at any moment. If the arm holds a position very firmly, a small error in where the wall is becomes a large force. How can the robot be firm enough to do its task and soft enough that contact never makes a dangerous force?

Intuition

There are two ways to control a robot in contact. Position control says: go to this point, and push as hard as needed to get there. That is ideal in free space, but it is dangerous at a wall, because the harder the wall resists, the harder the robot pushes. Impedance control says something else: make the robot behave like a spring and damper attached to a target point. Its response to a push is then part of the design. A car's suspension is a useful picture: it does not make the car rigid, it makes the car's response to bumps predictable and bounded.

The word impedance means the relation between motion and force. An impedance controller sets that relation to a chosen mass, damping and stiffness. The robot then pushes with a force that depends on how far it is from the target, not with an unlimited force.

Build it up: a spring-damper and a wall

Step 1: the desired behaviour. Choose an effective mass M (kg), a damping B (N·s/m) and a stiffness K (N/m) around a target x_d (m). In free space the end effector obeys

M\ddot x + B\dot x + K(x - x_d) = F_{\text{ext}}

Here F_{\text{ext}} is the force from the environment. With no contact it is zero, and the robot settles at x_d.

Step 2: the natural behaviour. With M = 1 kg, B = 20 N·s/m and K = 100 N/m, the natural frequency is \omega_n = \sqrt{K/M} = 10 rad/s and the damping ratio is \zeta = B/(2\sqrt{KM}) = 20/20 = 1.0. A ratio of 1 is critical damping: the robot reaches the target as fast as possible without overshoot.

Step 3: a wall. Put a wall at x_w = 0, modelled as a spring that only pushes when the robot is inside it: F_{\text{ext}} = -k_e\max(0,\,x - x_w). The target x_d = 0.1 m lies beyond the wall, so the robot can never reach it. It will press against the wall, and the force balances at the point where the two springs cancel.

Worked example: pressing a stiff wall

Take K = 100 N/m, k_e = 2000 N/m and x_d = 0.1 m. At rest the forces balance: K(x - x_d) + k_e x = 0, so

x = \frac{K x_d}{K + k_e} = \frac{100\cdot 0.1}{2100} \approx 0.00476\text{ m},\qquad F = k_e x \approx 2000\cdot 0.00476 = 9.52\text{ N}

The robot pushes with 9.5 N. Now make the controller stiff, K = 10\,000 N/m, which is what a rigid position controller effectively does: x = 10\,000\cdot 0.1/12\,000 = 0.0833 m and F = 2000 \cdot 0.0833 = 166.7 N. The same target gives about 17 times the force. As K grows without limit, the force tends to k_e x_d = 200 N: the wall, not the controller, sets the limit.

The transient is also worth a number. Start the robot at x = -0.2 m with zero speed and the default settings in the lab. The robot slides to the wall, and the peak contact force is about 30 N before it settles at the 9.5 N equilibrium.

The formal definition

M\ddot x + B\dot x + K(x - x_d) = -F_{\text{wall}},\qquad F_{\text{wall}} = k_e\,\max(0,\,x - x_w),\qquad \omega_n = \sqrt{K/M},\qquad \zeta = \frac{B}{2\sqrt{KM}}
x, ẋ, ẍposition (m), velocity (m/s), acceleration (m/s²) of the end effector
Meffective mass, kg: how the controller makes the robot feel heavy
Bdamping, N·s/m: resistance to speed
Kstiffness, N/m: how strongly the robot is pulled to x_d
x_ddesired position, m; may lie beyond a surface
k_e, x_wstiffness (N/m) and position (m) of the environment; the wall here is at 0
F_wallcontact force the wall exerts on the robot, N; zero when there is no contact
ω_n, ζnatural frequency (rad/s) and damping ratio (dimensionless) of the desired behaviour

The controller does not measure force in this basic form. It only makes the robot follow the equation above, which requires the motors to be torque-controlled. Adding a force sensor lets the controller know when it is in contact and what force it is actually applying.

Lab

Try this
  1. Keep the defaults. Note the peak contact force and the equilibrium 9.52 N in the readout. Then raise K to 400 N/m: the equilibrium force rises.
  2. Set B to 0. The robot rings against the wall and the force oscillates. Add damping back and watch ζ rise towards 1.
  3. Set M to 5 kg. With the same K and B the response is slower and the impact is harder: inertia carries momentum into the wall.
  4. Untick wall present. The robot now reaches xd with no force at all. The difference between the two runs is the contact.
Common traps
  • “Impedance control is compliance by itself.” The stiffness and damping are whatever you set in software. Real compliance needs torque-controlled actuators and good models, or a force sensor.
  • Too soft to work. A very low K lets the robot drift away from the target, so the task fails even though the contact is safe. Compliance is a trade-off with accuracy.
  • Ignoring inertia. A heavy effective mass carries large momentum into a contact, so the peak force can be high even with a soft controller.
  • Confusing impedance with admittance. Impedance control takes motion and produces force. Admittance control takes measured force and produces motion. They are dual views, and the choice depends on the hardware.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Assembly tasks such as peg-in-hole and connector insertion rely on compliant control so that a small misalignment does not break the part.
  • Robots that work beside people use impedance control to keep contact forces below safety limits, which connects to the standards of Module 9.
  • Learned manipulation policies for contact-rich tasks often run under a compliant low-level layer, so that the learned part never has to output large forces directly.

Check yourself

1. With M = 1 kg, B = 20 N·s/m and K = 100 N/m, what is the damping ratio, and what does it mean?
ζ = B/(2√(KM)) = 20/(2·10) = 1.0. The system is critically damped: it reaches the target as quickly as possible without overshooting.
2. With K = 100 N/m, k_e = 2000 N/m and x_d = 0.1 m beyond a wall at 0, what is the steady contact force?
x = K x_d/(K + k_e) = 10/2100 ≈ 0.00476 m, and F = k_e x ≈ 9.52 N.
3. Why does a position controller with very high stiffness push harder into a wall than an impedance controller with a modest stiffness, for the same target?
The steady force is F = k_e K x_d/(K + k_e). With large K this approaches k_e x_d, which is the largest force the wall can receive for that target. With a modest K the robot yields earlier, so the force is smaller. The controller's stiffness decides how hard the robot presses; the target sets the limit.
Pipeline viewStages: Sense (encoders and, where present, a force or torque sensor) and Control (the impedance law on the torques). Loop rate: typically 500 Hz to 1 kHz, because contact dynamics are fast. Stored: the impedance parameters M, B, K, the contact stiffness estimate and the logs of force and position.

Takeaways

  • Impedance control makes the robot behave like a mass-spring-damper towards a target, so contact forces are bounded by the chosen stiffness.
  • At a wall the steady force is k_e K x_d/(K + k_e), which is far smaller than the rigid-position force for a soft K.
  • Stiffness trades safety against accuracy; damping sets how the robot settles; mass sets how hard an impact is.
