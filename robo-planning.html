Module 5 · Planning & motion

A robot that knows where it is still has to decide where to go. Planning turns a goal into a motion that avoids obstacles and respects the robot's limits. You will see planning as a search through configurations, watch A* and RRT explore a space, shape a trajectory with velocity and acceleration limits, bend a path away from obstacles by optimisation, and finally plan a sequence of symbolic actions such as pick, move, place.

6 lessons≈ 3 hoursNeeds Module 0 (frames, configuration space) and Module 2 (kinematics)

Every lesson sits in the Plan stage of the sense–estimate–plan–control loop. Planners are usually run at a few hertz, or only when the goal or the map changes, while the controller that tracks the plan runs at hundreds of hertz. Each lesson says which of the two it belongs to. Angles are in radians unless a lesson says degrees, lengths are in metres, times in seconds.

5.1

Planning as search in configuration space

The questionA two-link arm must move its gripper from a cup on the left to a shelf on the right. Somewhere in the middle, the forearm would sweep through a post. Which joint angles are safe, which are not, and how does a program search for a route that only passes through the safe ones?

Intuition

Plan in the space the robot actually moves in. The arm has two joint angles, \theta_1 and \theta_2. Every pair of angles is one configuration, and the set of all pairs is the configuration space. Inside that space the arm is just a point. Moving the arm means moving the point.

The obstacle is what changes shape. In the workspace (the plane where the gripper actually goes) the post is a small disc, but the arm is a bent rod, so the set of forbidden configurations is a strange region with curved edges. In configuration space the picture reverses: the arm is a point and the forbidden set is simply a region you must avoid. Planning becomes: find a path for a point that stays out of the region.

Build it up: from one pose to a whole map

Step 1: a pose is a configuration. Take an arm with two links of length L_1 = L_2 = 1 m. Its joint angles q = (\theta_1, \theta_2) determine the elbow and the tip:

e(q) = \big(L_1\cos\theta_1,\; L_1\sin\theta_1\big), \qquad p(q) = e(q) + \big(L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2),\; L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\big)

Step 2: a configuration is valid if no link touches the obstacle. For one configuration we compute the two links as line segments: the first from the origin to the elbow, the second from the elbow to the tip. The configuration is in collision if either segment passes within the obstacle radius R of the obstacle centre C.

Step 3: a map of all configurations. If we test every pair of angles on a grid, say 90 values of \theta_1 times 90 values of \theta_2, each cell is either free (white) or blocked (dark). That grid is a picture of the configuration space, and the obstacle has become a blob in it. This is the "discretised configuration grid" that the rest of the module searches.

Step 4: a path is a chain of free cells. A plan is a sequence of configurations from the start to the goal in which every configuration is free and every step between neighbouring configurations is also free. In practice the step between two configurations is checked by testing a few intermediate configurations, because a path can jump over a thin obstacle between two samples.

Worked example: four configurations by hand

Let the obstacle be a disc with centre C=(0.5,\,1.2) m and radius R=0.3 m. The distance from the disc centre to a segment is the distance to the nearest point of that segment; a configuration is free if both links stay more than R away.

q = (θ₁, θ₂)elbow e(q)tip p(q)closest approach to Cresult
(0, 0)(1.000, 0.000)(2.000, 0.000)1.200 m (to link 1)free
(π/2, 0)(0.000, 1.000)(0.000, 2.000)0.500 m (to link 2)free, since 0.500 > 0.300
(π/4, 0)(0.707, 0.707)(1.414, 1.414)0.495 mfree
(π/3, π/6)(0.500, 0.866)(0.500, 1.866)0.000 m (the tip link passes through C)collision

Check the last row by hand. The second link runs from (0.5, 0.866) to (0.5, 1.866), a vertical segment at x=0.5. The centre (0.5, 1.2) lies on it, so the distance is zero, far less than R=0.3 m. The arm is in collision in that configuration even though its tip is a long way from the post.

The formal definition

Let \mathcal{Q} be the set of all configurations allowed by the joint limits, and let \mathcal{O} be the set of configurations in which some part of the robot touches an obstacle. The free space is \mathcal{Q}_{\text{free}} = \mathcal{Q} \setminus \mathcal{O}. A motion plan is a continuous path q:[0,1]\to\mathcal{Q}_{\text{free}} with q(0)=q_{\text{start}} and q(1)=q_{\text{goal}}.

q=(\theta_1,\theta_2)a configuration: the two joint angles in radians
L_1, L_2link lengths in metres (here both 1 m)
e(q), p(q)elbow and tip positions in the plane, in metres
C, Rcentre (metres) and radius (metres) of a disc obstacle
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{free}}the configurations in which no link is in collision
q(s)a path parametrised by s\in[0,1], not yet by time
Pipeline positionStage 3, Plan. Collision checking is called thousands of times inside a planner, so it must be cheap. Typical planning runs at 1–10 Hz or on demand, while the controller tracks the resulting path at hundreds of hertz.

Common traps
  • Checking only the tip. The worked example collided through a link whose tip was far away. Check every link, and the joints too.
  • Coarse grids that skip thin obstacles. A 10-degree grid can step over a 5 cm post. Either refine the grid or check the segment between neighbours.
  • Mixing degrees and radians. Most maths code expects radians. A joint at "90" in a degree field is a full extra turn in radians.
  • Forgetting joint limits. The free space is the allowed configurations and the collision-free ones. A valid path can still be impossible for the motor.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Motion planners in MoveIt and OMPL search configuration space for industrial and research arms. Their collision checkers test links against the scene exactly as the lab does, only for six or seven joints.
  • Learned policies (Module 7) act in the same space. A policy that outputs joint targets is only useful if the targets are collision-free, which is why planners and safety filters (Module 9) are often kept as a layer around the learner.

Check yourself

1. The arm has L₁ = L₂ = 1 m. At q = (0, π/2), where is the tip, and is the arm free against a disc at C = (2.0, 0.0) m with R = 0.3 m?
The elbow is (1, 0) and the tip is (1 + cos(π/2), sin(π/2)) = (1, 1). Link 1 runs from (0,0) to (1,0). The closest point of link 1 to C is (1,0), at distance 1.0 m. Link 2 runs from (1,0) to (1,1); its closest point to C is again (1,0), at 1.0 m. Both exceed 0.3 m, so the configuration is free.
2. Why is the configuration space usually easier to plan in than the workspace?
In configuration space the robot is a point, so planning is a search for a path of a point around forbidden regions. The awkward link geometry is absorbed into the shape of the forbidden region, which the collision checker computes once per configuration.
3. A planner tests a straight move between two free configurations only at its two endpoints. Why can the move still be unsafe?
The arm can sweep through an obstacle between the endpoints and come back out. Checking intermediate configurations along the move, at a spacing small enough that no obstacle fits between checks, is the standard fix.

Takeaways

  • A configuration is one vector of joint angles; the configuration space holds all of them, and the arm is a point inside it.
  • An obstacle is a forbidden region in configuration space. A valid plan is a path of free configurations from start to goal.
  • Planning is search, and the search needs a collision check for every configuration it touches.
5.2

Graph search: Dijkstra and A*

The questionA warehouse robot must drive from its charging dock to a pallet on a grid of aisles. Some cells are shelves, and turning or driving over a rough patch costs more time. How does it find the cheapest route, and how can it avoid exploring the whole warehouse to do so?

Intuition

Imagine dropping a stone in a pond. The ripples spread out evenly in every direction, and the first ring to reach a point is the shortest distance from the stone. That is Dijkstra's algorithm: it expands the cheapest known node first, so the set of explored nodes grows like ripples, correct but wide. It explores the whole pond before it reaches a far bank.

A* adds a sense of direction. If you can estimate how far each node still is from the goal, you can let the ripples lean toward the goal. A node that is cheap to reach but far from the goal is visited later. The estimate must never be too optimistic, or A* could ignore the true best route.

Build it up: costs, then estimates

Step 1: cost so far. Let g(n) be the cheapest cost found so far from the start to node n. Dijkstra always expands the open node with the smallest g.

Step 2: an estimate of cost to go. Let h(n) be a guess of the remaining cost from n to the goal. On a grid where a step costs 1 and moves are up, down, left or right, the Manhattan distance |x_n - x_G| + |y_n - y_G| is such a guess.

Step 3: rank by the sum. A* expands the open node with the smallest f(n)=g(n)+h(n), the estimated cost of the best path through n.

Step 4: when is the answer right? If h never overestimates the true remaining cost, A* finds a cheapest path when the goal is first taken from the open set. We state the condition precisely in the definition below.

Worked example: A* on a four-node graph

Nodes S, A, B, G with directed edges: S\to A cost 1, S\to B cost 4, A\to B cost 2, A\to G cost 5, B\to G cost 1. The true remaining costs are h^*(S)=4, h^*(A)=3, h^*(B)=1, h^*(G)=0. We use the estimates h(S)=4, h(A)=3, h(B)=1, h(G)=0, which never overestimate.

stepexpandgf = g + hnew or improved nodes
1S04A: g=1, f=4; B: g=4, f=5
2A14B improved: g=3, f=4 (through A, 1+2); G: g=6, f=6
3B34G improved: g=4, f=4 (through B, 3+1)
4G44goal reached: path S→A→B→G, cost 4

Notice what happened to G. It was first reached at cost 6 through A, but B still had f=4 in the open set, which is cheaper, so B was expanded next and gave G a cost of 4. A* keeps going until the goal is the cheapest open node; only then is its cost known to be optimal. Dijkstra visits the same four nodes here because these estimates happen to be exact. On a large grid, A* with a good estimate expands far fewer nodes.

The formal definition

Let c(n,m)\ge 0 be the cost of an edge from n to m, and h^*(n) the true cheapest cost from n to the goal. A heuristic h is admissible when 0\le h(n)\le h^*(n) for every node. It is consistent when h(n)\le c(n,m)+h(m) for every edge. Consistency implies admissibility. With an admissible heuristic, A* returns an optimal path.

f(n) = g(n) + h(n), \qquad \text{expand the open node with smallest } f(n)
\text{weighted A*: } f_w(n) = g(n) + w\,h(n), \quad w\ge 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{cost} \le w \cdot \text{optimal cost}
g(n)cheapest cost found so far from the start to n
h(n)heuristic estimate of the remaining cost from n to the goal
h^*(n)true cheapest remaining cost (unknown in practice; used only in the condition)
f(n)estimated cost of the best path through n
wweight on the heuristic; w=1 is A*, w>1 is faster but not always optimal

Dijkstra is the special case h(n)=0 for every node. It is optimal for any non-negative edge costs but expands the most nodes.

Pipeline positionStage 3, Plan. A grid search of a few thousand cells takes milliseconds, so it can run whenever the map changes. Recomputing a route at 1–5 Hz is typical for mobile robots.

  2. Switch to A* and play again at the same speed. The search now leans toward the goal in the top right. Count how many cells it expands compared with Dijkstra, and read the counter.

Common traps
  • An overestimating heuristic. Straight-line distance is admissible when diagonal moves cost \sqrt 2. If diagonal moves cost 1, the straight-line distance overestimates nothing only if you use the Chebyshev distance \max(|\Delta x|,|\Delta y|), and Manhattan distance then overestimates. A heuristic that overestimates can return a longer route with no warning.
  • Forgetting that g can improve. A node can be reached first by an expensive route. The search must update its g when a cheaper route appears, as in step 2 of the worked example.
  • Calling the search "optimal" when w > 1. Weighted A* trades optimality for speed on purpose. Say which one you use.
  • Using grids for everything. A grid is cheap for a 2-D floor but explodes in higher dimensions; the next lesson shows the alternative.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Route planners in mapping services and mobile robot stacks run A* or its variants on road or occupancy graphs, with the straight-line distance as the heuristic.
  • The same search reappears in AI as beam search and best-first search over token sequences, with a learned value estimate playing the role of h.

Check yourself

1. On a 4-connected grid with unit step costs, a robot is at (2, 3) and the goal is at (6, 1). What is the Manhattan heuristic, and is it admissible?
h = |2−6| + |3−1| = 4 + 2 = 6. It is admissible, because a 4-connected path must take at least 6 unit steps to change x by 4 and y by 2.
2. In the worked example, G is first reached at cost 6 (through A). Why does the search keep going instead of accepting it?
Because B still sits in the open set with f = 4, which is smaller than G's f = 6. A cheaper route through B might exist, and it does (cost 4). The goal is accepted only when it is the cheapest open node, at which point its cost is optimal.
3. Suppose h(A) were set to 5 in the worked example, while h*(A) is still 3. What could go wrong?
The heuristic would be inadmissible. A's f would be 1 + 5 = 6, so A would be expanded late. The search could then accept a more expensive route to G before the cheaper one through A is explored, so the path may not be optimal.

Takeaways

  • Dijkstra expands by cost so far; A* expands by cost so far plus an estimate of cost to go.
  • An admissible heuristic (never overestimating) keeps A* optimal; a weight above 1 makes it faster but only approximately optimal.
  • A search needs the graph's edge costs and a way to update the best cost of each node.
5.3

Sampling-based planning: PRM and RRT

The questionA six-joint arm has six configuration coordinates. If each joint is split into 100 values, the grid has 100^6 = 10^{12} cells. Checking each one for collision would take far too long, and the grid of the previous lesson no longer fits in memory. How can a planner find a path while looking at only a tiny fraction of the space?

Intuition

Imagine searching a dark house for a way from the front door to the kitchen. You do not map every cubic centimetre. You take a few steps in random directions, keep the spots you could reach without bumping into anything, and remember which spots connect to which. Sooner or later a chain of remembered spots reaches the kitchen.

Sampling planners do exactly that. They draw random configurations, test each one for collision, and connect the free ones. Two popular forms differ in how they connect. A PRM (probabilistic roadmap) builds a graph of free samples first and searches it afterwards. An RRT (rapidly exploring random tree) grows a single tree outward from the start, steered toward random targets, and stops when the tree reaches the goal.

Build it up: growing a tree

Step 1: sample a target. Draw a random configuration x_{\text{rand}}. Sometimes, say one time in twenty, use the goal itself as the target. This goal bias pulls the tree toward the goal.

Step 2: find the nearest node. Among the nodes already in the tree, find the one closest to x_{\text{rand}}. Call it x_{\text{near}}.

Step 3: take one limited step. Move from x_{\text{near}} toward x_{\text{rand}} by at most a step size \eta. The new node x_{\text{new}} is added only if the segment between the two is collision-free.

Step 4: repeat until the goal is reached. The tree spreads into the free space and eventually connects to the goal region. Following the parent links back from the goal gives the path.

Worked example: two extensions by hand

The tree starts with one node at the origin (0,0). Step size \eta = 1 m, and no obstacles lie nearby.

Extension 1. Sample x_{\text{rand}}=(3,4). The only node is (0,0), so it is the nearest. The distance is d=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5 m, which is larger than \eta. The unit direction is (3,4)/5 = (0.6,\,0.8), so

x_{\text{new}} = (0,0) + 1\cdot(0.6,\,0.8) = (0.6,\,0.8)

Extension 2. Sample x_{\text{rand}}=(1,0). The nearest node is now (0.6,\,0.8), at distance \sqrt{0.4^2+0.8^2}=\sqrt{0.8}\approx 0.894 m from the sample. That is less than \eta=1 m, so the step reaches the sample exactly and x_{\text{new}}=(1,0).

The tree now has three nodes, (0,0)\to(0.6,0.8)\to(1,0). The second node was added only because the target was closer than one step.

The formal definition

Let T=(V,E) be the tree, initialised with the start configuration. The RRT loop is:

\begin{aligned}&x_{\text{rand}} \leftarrow \text{sample}(\mathcal{Q}), \quad x_{\text{near}} \leftarrow \arg\min_{v\in V}\lVert v - x_{\text{rand}}\rVert\\ &x_{\text{new}} \leftarrow x_{\text{near}} + \min\!\big(\eta,\ \lVert x_{\text{rand}}-x_{\text{near}}\rVert\big)\,\frac{x_{\text{rand}}-x_{\text{near}}}{\lVert x_{\text{rand}}-x_{\text{near}}\rVert}\\ &\text{if the segment } [x_{\text{near}},x_{\text{new}}] \text{ is free: } V\leftarrow V\cup\{x_{\text{new}}\},\ E\leftarrow E\cup\{(x_{\text{near}},x_{\text{new}})\}\end{aligned}

A PRM instead samples N free configurations, connects each to its nearby neighbours when the connecting segment is free, and then runs graph search (Lesson 5.2) from start to goal.

\mathcal{Q}the configuration space (joint limits included)
x_{\text{rand}}, x_{\text{near}}, x_{\text{new}}a random sample, the nearest tree node, and the node added this step; each a vector of joint angles (radians)
\etastep size, the largest move per extension (in configuration units, here metres in the worked example)
\lVert\cdot\rVertEuclidean distance in configuration space
Nnumber of roadmap samples in a PRM

RRT is probabilistically complete: if a collision-free path exists, the chance that the planner finds one tends to 1 as the number of samples grows. It does not say how long that takes. Its paths are usually jagged. RRT* adds a rewiring step that reconnects nearby nodes through cheaper routes, which makes the path approach the optimum as samples grow (asymptotic optimality).

Pipeline positionStage 3, Plan. A sampling planner for a six-joint arm typically runs in tens of milliseconds to a few seconds, and its output is then smoothed (Lesson 5.5) before control.

Lab

Try this

  2. Drag the step size η up and down. A small η gives a fine tree that grows slowly; a large η grows fast but may fail to squeeze between obstacles.

Common traps
  • "RRT is optimal." Plain RRT returns the first path it finds, which is usually much longer than the best one. Use RRT* or smooth the path afterwards.
  • Forgetting the segment check. Adding a node is safe only if the segment to its parent is free. Testing just the new point lets the tree tunnel through thin walls.
  • A non-uniform sampler. If samples are only drawn in the easy part of the space, the tree never learns about the narrow passage. Narrow passages are the hard case for all sampling planners.
  • Reading a failure as proof. If RRT does not find a path after a fixed budget, the problem may still be solvable. It is not a certificate of no path.
Where it shows up in AI
  • OMPL, a widely used open library, implements RRT, RRT* and PRM, and MoveIt calls it to plan arm motions in factories and labs.
  • Sampled candidate motions are the same idea as sampling candidate action sequences in model-predictive and learned planners: generate many, score them, keep the best feasible one.

Check yourself

1. The tree holds (0,0) only, η = 0.5 m, and the sample is (3,4). What node is added?
The distance is 5 m, which exceeds η, so the step is 0.5 m along (0.6, 0.8): x_new = (0.3, 0.4). The tree does not jump to the sample.
2. Why does the extension in the worked example reach (1, 0) exactly, while the first did not?
The first sample was 5 m away, more than the step size of 1 m, so the node moved one step only. The second sample was about 0.894 m from the nearest node, which is less than the step size, so the new node can equal the sample.
3. What does probabilistic completeness promise, and what does it not promise?
It promises that, if a collision-free path exists, the probability of finding one tends to 1 as samples increase. It does not promise a short path, nor a bound on the running time.

Takeaways

  • Sampling planners avoid mapping a huge configuration space by testing only the random samples they visit.
  • RRT grows one tree toward random targets in limited steps; PRM builds a roadmap and then searches it.
  • Plain RRT is complete in the probabilistic sense but not optimal; smoothing or RRT* is needed for good paths.
5.4

Trajectory generation & time scaling

The questionThe planner gives a path: a list of joint configurations. The motors, however, need to know when to be where. If the arm jumps from one configuration to the next, the joints need infinite acceleration and the motors saturate. How do you turn a path into a motion that starts and stops smoothly and respects a maximum speed and a maximum acceleration?

Intuition

A path says where to go; a trajectory says where to be at each moment. Think of a train on a fixed track. The track is the path, and the timetable is the trajectory. The same track can be run slowly and gently or fast and hard. Changing the timetable changes how fast and how hard the train moves, but never where it goes.

A good timetable starts from rest, speeds up smoothly, and slows down smoothly to a stop. That means the velocity is zero at the ends and changes continuously in the middle. The simplest smooth curve that does this is a polynomial in time.

Build it up: from a rest-to-rest move to limits

Step 1: normalised time. Let the move last T seconds, and let s = t/T run from 0 to 1. Then a shape function \sigma(s) goes from 0 to 1, and the joint moves by \Delta q\,\sigma(s) with \Delta q = q_f - q_0.

Step 2: the cubic. The cubic \sigma(s)=3s^2-2s^3 starts and ends at rest, because its slope is zero at s=0 and s=1. But its acceleration jumps at the ends, so a motor is asked for a sudden torque change.

Step 3: the quintic. The quintic \sigma(s)=10s^3-15s^4+6s^5 is zero in velocity and acceleration at both ends. It is the smoothest polynomial that moves from rest to rest in a given time.

Step 4: derivatives give the limits. Velocity and acceleration are \dot q = \Delta q\,\sigma'(s)/T and \ddot q = \Delta q\,\sigma''(s)/T^2. The peak values must stay under the joint limits, and that fixes the smallest allowed T.

Worked example: a quintic move from 0 to 1 rad in 2 s

Take q_0=0, q_f=1 rad, T=2 s. At t=0.5 s, s=0.25.

Position: \sigma(0.25)=10(0.25)^3-15(0.25)^4+6(0.25)^5 = 0.15625-0.05859+0.00586 = 0.10352, so q = 0.1035 rad.

Velocity: \sigma'(s)=30s^2-60s^3+30s^4 gives \sigma'(0.25)=1.8750-0.9375+0.1172=1.0547, so \dot q = \Delta q\,\sigma'(s)/T = 1.0547/2 = 0.5273 rad/s.

Acceleration: \sigma''(s)=60s-180s^2+120s^3 gives \sigma''(0.25)=15-11.25+1.875=5.625, so \ddot q = 5.625/2^2 = 1.406 rad/s².

Peak values for this move: the velocity peaks at the midpoint, where \sigma'(0.5)=1.875, so \dot q_{\max}=1.875/2 = 0.9375 rad/s. The acceleration peaks at s\approx 0.211 with |\sigma''|\approx 5.774, so |\ddot q|_{\max}=5.774/4 = 1.443 rad/s².

The formal definition

q(t) = q_0 + \Delta q\,\sigma\!\left(\tfrac{t}{T}\right), \qquad \sigma_{\text{quintic}}(s)=10s^3-15s^4+6s^5, \qquad \sigma_{\text{cubic}}(s)=3s^2-2s^3
\dot q_{\max} = \kappa_v\,\frac{|\Delta q|}{T}, \qquad |\ddot q|_{\max} = \kappa_a\,\frac{|\Delta q|}{T^2}, \qquad (\kappa_v,\kappa_a)_{\text{quintic}} = \big(1.875,\ 5.774\big)
q_0, q_fstart and end joint angles (radians)
\Delta qtotal joint change, q_f-q_0 (radians)
Tduration of the move (seconds)
s=t/Tnormalised time, dimensionless, from 0 to 1
\sigma(s)the shape function, from 0 to 1, with zero slope and curvature at the ends for the quintic
\kappa_v,\kappa_apeak factors of the shape: 1.875 for velocity and 5.774 = 10/√3 for acceleration (quintic)

Time scaling. The shape is fixed, so halving T doubles the peak velocity and quadruples the peak acceleration. This is why the duration of a move is chosen from the limits, not from a wish.

Pipeline positionStage 3, Plan, feeding Stage 4, Control. A trajectory is computed once per move, in microseconds; the controller then samples it at the control rate, typically 100–1000 Hz.

Common traps
  • Confusing position limits with speed limits. A motor's rated speed and torque are separate constraints. Check the velocity and the acceleration (and, through the dynamics of Module 2, the torque).
  • Starting a move without a rest. A trajectory that begins at speed jumps the controller's setpoint and produces a step in the motor command.
  • Assuming the duration is free. Shorter moves look faster but scale peak values as 1/T and 1/T². Pick T from the limits.
  • Joining segments with mismatched velocity. Two moves in a row must agree on position and velocity at the join, or the arm kicks at each waypoint.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Industrial and research arms run time-optimal or jerk-limited trajectory generators (for example, trapezoidal or S-curve profiles) on the same principle: a fixed path, a timing law, and limits on speed, acceleration and jerk.
  • Learned controllers that output joint targets at each step are usually followed by a trajectory filter, so that a sudden network output does not command an impossible acceleration (Module 9).

Check yourself

1. A quintic move from 0 to 0.5 rad lasts 1 s. What is its peak velocity?
Peak velocity = 1.875 · |Δq| / T = 1.875 · 0.5 / 1 = 0.9375 rad/s.
2. You need the same quintic move with the peak velocity halved. What T do you use, and what happens to the peak acceleration?
Velocity scales as 1/T, so doubling T halves the peak velocity: T = 2 s for the move above. Acceleration scales as 1/T², so the peak acceleration falls to one quarter of its original value.
3. Why is the quintic preferred over the cubic for a rest-to-rest move, even though both start and end at rest?
The cubic has zero velocity at the ends but non-zero acceleration there, so the acceleration jumps when the move starts and stops. The quintic also has zero acceleration at both ends, so the commanded torque changes smoothly and the motors are not asked for a sudden change.

Takeaways

  • A trajectory is a path plus a timing law; the path fixes the shape, the timing fixes the speed and acceleration.
  • A quintic with zero velocity and acceleration at the ends gives a smooth rest-to-rest move.
  • Peak velocity scales as 1/T and peak acceleration as 1/T², so the duration is set by the limits.
5.5

Trajectory optimisation

The questionThe sampling planner returned a path that is collision-free but jagged, and it grazes a post by 2 cm. A sensor error of 3 cm would make the arm hit the post. How can a path be made smoother and kept comfortably away from obstacles, without throwing the whole planner away?

Intuition

Picture a rubber band stretched between the start and the goal, with a row of beads on it. The band wants to be short and straight, so it pulls every bead toward its neighbours. Now place an obstacle near the band. Each bead that comes too close is pushed away from the obstacle. The final shape is where the pulling and the pushing balance. Nothing in the process "plans" the shape; it settles.

This is trajectory optimisation: write down a cost that measures how bad a path is, then move the path downhill on that cost. The cost has two parts. A smoothness term punishes sharp bends, and a clearance term punishes coming within a safety margin of an obstacle.

Build it up: pulls and pushes

Step 1: a path is a list of points. Write the path as P_0, P_1, \dots, P_{N-1}, points in the plane (metres). The first and last points are fixed at the start and goal.

Step 2: a bend costs more when it is sharp. At an interior point, the second difference P_{i+1}-2P_i+P_{i-1} is zero when the three points lie on a straight line, and grows with the bend. Its squared length is the smoothness cost.

Step 3: a near miss costs more when it is closer. Let the clearance gap of a point be its distance to the obstacle surface, g_i = \lVert P_i - C\rVert - R. If g_i is below a safety margin d_{\text{safe}}, a penalty grows with the shortfall.

Step 4: move downhill. The gradient of the cost tells each point which way to move to lower the cost. Each iteration moves every interior point a small distance against its gradient, then checks again.

Worked example: one gradient step on three points

Let P_0=(0,0), P_1=(2,1), P_2=(4,0) (metres), with the first and last fixed. Obstacle centre C=(2,0.3), radius R=0.2 m, margin d_{\text{safe}}=0.4 m. Weights w_s=w_c=1 and step size \alpha=0.05.

Smoothness. The second difference at P_1 is P_0-2P_1+P_2=(0,0)-(4,2)+(4,0)=(0,-2). Its squared length is 4. The gradient of that term with respect to P_1 is -4(0,-2)=(0,8).

Clearance. The gap is g_1=\lVert(0,0.7)\rVert-0.2=0.5 m, which is larger than d_{\text{safe}}=0.4 m. No penalty, so no clearance gradient.

Step. P_1 \leftarrow P_1-\alpha(0,8) = (2,\,1-0.4) = (2,\,0.6). The bend has flattened and the point has dropped toward the obstacle.

Next iteration. The gap is now \lVert(0,0.3)\rVert-0.2=0.1 m, so the penalty is active: w_c(d_{\text{safe}}-g)^2=0.09. Its gradient is -2w_c(d_{\text{safe}}-g)\,(P_1-C)/\lVert P_1-C\rVert = -2(0.3)(0,1) = (0,-0.6), which pushes the point up. The smoothness gradient at (2,0.6) is -4(0,-1.2)=(0,4.8). The total gradient is (0,\,4.8-0.6)=(0,4.2), so the pull toward the straight line still wins at this weight. A larger clearance weight is needed to hold the point at the margin.

The formal definition

J(P)=w_s\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\big\lVert P_{i+1}-2P_i+P_{i-1}\big\rVert^2 \;+\; w_c\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\max\!\big(0,\ d_{\text{safe}}-g_i(P_i)\big)^2, \qquad g_i(P)=\lVert P-C\rVert-R
P \leftarrow P - \alpha\,\nabla J(P)
P_ithe i-th path point, in metres; P_0 and P_{N-1} are fixed
Nnumber of points on the path
w_s, w_cweights on smoothness and clearance; they trade one goal against the other
C, Robstacle centre and radius, in metres (a disc)
g_iclearance gap of point i, in metres: distance to the obstacle surface
d_{\text{safe}}required safety margin, in metres
\alphastep size of the gradient descent (dimensionless here, with points in metres)

The gradient \nabla J is the vector of partial derivatives of J with respect to every coordinate of every interior point. With many points, the descent converges to a local minimum: the answer depends on the starting path. A start that already avoids the obstacle usually gives a good answer.

Pipeline positionStage 3, Plan. Optimisation runs after a sampling planner, typically in tens to hundreds of milliseconds, before the trajectory of Lesson 5.4 is generated and tracked by the controller.

Lab

Try this
  1. Press play. The straight path starts through the obstacle and is pushed out while it straightens. The caption shows the iteration count and the cost, both in plain units.
  2. Set the clearance weight w_c to 0.2. Let the path settle and read the minimum clearance gap. It sits inside the safety margin. Now raise w_c to 4 and reset: the gap moves to the margin.
  3. Raise the smoothness weight w_s. The path becomes straighter and hugs the obstacle more closely. Find the setting that gives smoothness and clearance together.
  4. Set the step size α to its maximum and watch the backtracks counter. When a step would raise the cost, the lab halves α for that iteration and counts a backtrack. A large α therefore slows the descent through backtracks rather than making the path blow up.
Common traps
  • Starting inside the obstacle without checking. A gradient method can walk out of an obstacle, but a start deep inside one can settle into a bad local minimum.
  • Trusting the cost weights. Weights are not physical units. A margin met only "on average" by the cost is not a guarantee; check the minimum gap on the final path.
  • Too large a step. Gradient descent is stable only for a step small compared with the stiffness of the smoothness term. Shrink α when the cost rises.
  • Optimised paths are not dynamically feasible by default. A smooth geometric path can still violate speed or torque limits. Pass it to the trajectory generator of Lesson 5.4.
Where it shows up in AI
  • CHOMP and TrajOpt, two well-known trajectory optimisers, minimise a smoothness-plus-obstacle cost of this form, with gradients computed from the robot's geometry for arms and humanoids.
  • Diffusion and flow-based planners (Module 8) learn to generate paths directly; many systems still score their output with an optimisation-style cost to pick a safe one.

Check yourself

1. For P₀ = (0,0), P₁ = (1,1), P₂ = (2,0), what is the smoothness gradient with respect to P₁?
P₀ − 2P₁ + P₂ = (0,0) − (2,2) + (2,0) = (0,−2). The gradient of its squared length is −4 times that vector, which is (0, 8).
2. In the worked example, the point is 0.5 m from the obstacle surface with d_safe = 0.4 m. Why does the clearance term contribute nothing there?
The penalty is max(0, d_safe − g). Because g = 0.5 is larger than d_safe = 0.4, the maximum is zero, and both the penalty and its gradient are zero.
3. Why can a good smoothness weight still give a path that is unsafe?
Smoothness pulls the path toward the straight line, which may pass closer to an obstacle. If the clearance weight is too small to balance the pull, the equilibrium lies inside the safety margin. The minimum gap of the final path must be checked, not assumed.

Takeaways

  • Trajectory optimisation writes the quality of a path as a cost and moves the path downhill on it.
  • Smoothness and clearance pull in different directions; their weights decide where the path settles.
  • A local method needs a sensible start and a checked result: the minimum gap and the step size both matter.
5.6

Task and mission planning

The questionA household robot is told "put the cup on the shelf." That sentence involves no joint angles. The robot must decide the order of actions: which place to visit, when to pick the cup up, and when to put it down, so that each action is possible when it runs. How can a planner reason about actions and states, rather than about geometry?

Intuition

A motion planner answers "how do I get the arm there?" A task planner answers "what must I do, and in what order?" It works with facts that are either true or false, such as the robot is at the table or the hand is empty. An action can run only when its requirements hold, and it changes which facts are true.

Think of a recipe. "Add the eggs" needs the eggs to be out of the fridge and the bowl to be on the counter. After adding them, the eggs are in the bowl and no longer on the counter. A task planner works out the recipe steps that take you from the facts you have to the facts you want.

Build it up: states, actions and plans

Step 1: a state is a set of true facts. For the cup problem, the start state is \{\text{at\_robot(table)},\ \text{at\_cup(table)},\ \text{handempty}\}. Facts not in the set are false (the closed-world assumption).

Step 2: an action has three lists. Its preconditions must all be true before it runs. Its add list becomes true afterwards, and its delete list becomes false afterwards.

Step 3: a plan is a sequence that reaches the goal. Apply actions one at a time, checking the preconditions each time. The plan succeeds when every goal fact is true in the final state. Searching over sequences is a graph search (Lesson 5.2), where the nodes are states.

Worked example: a three-action plan, checked state by state

Start: s_0=\{\text{at\_robot(table)},\ \text{at\_cup(table)},\ \text{handempty}\}. Goal: \text{at\_cup(shelf)}.

actionpreconditions, all true?deletedaddedstate after
pick(cup, table)at_robot(table) ✓, at_cup(table) ✓, handempty ✓at_cup(table), handemptyholding_cup{at_robot(table), holding_cup}
move(table, shelf)at_robot(table) ✓at_robot(table)at_robot(shelf){at_robot(shelf), holding_cup}
place(cup, shelf)at_robot(shelf) ✓, holding_cup ✓holding_cupat_cup(shelf), handempty{at_robot(shelf), at_cup(shelf), handempty}

The goal fact at_cup(shelf) is true, so the plan is pick, move, place: three actions. A breadth-first search over all states, which is guaranteed to find the shortest plan, finds this same three-action plan. The start with the robot at the shelf and the cup in the sink needs four actions: move to the sink, pick the cup up, move to the table, and place it there.

The formal definition

A STRIPS-style planning problem is a tuple (F, A, s_0, G). An action a has preconditions \text{pre}(a)\subseteq F, add effects \text{add}(a)\subseteq F and delete effects \text{del}(a)\subseteq F. Action a is applicable in state s when \text{pre}(a)\subseteq s, and then

\text{apply}(s,a) = \big(s \setminus \text{del}(a)\big)\cup\text{add}(a)

A plan \pi=(a_1,\dots,a_n) solves the problem when each action is applicable in turn and G\subseteq s_n, where s_k=\text{apply}(s_{k-1},a_k).

Fthe set of all facts (propositions) the planner may use
s_0, s_kthe start state and the state after the k-th action; each is a subset of F
Gthe goal: a set of facts that must all hold at the end
\text{pre}, \text{add}, \text{del}the three lists of each action
\pia plan: an ordered sequence of actions

Breadth-first search over states finds a shortest plan when all actions cost the same. Costs can be added with the graph search of Lesson 5.2, and learned or heuristic task planners use the same definition with a smarter search.

Pipeline positionStage 3, Plan, at the top of the stack. A task plan is made once per mission, or again when a step fails. It runs in milliseconds for small domains and is followed by motion planners for each geometric step.

Common traps
  • Forgetting a precondition. "Place the cup" is not possible while the hand is empty. A plan that skips the pick is invalid, and the checker must refuse it.
  • Confusing the delete list with negation. Deleting a fact means it is no longer listed in the state. Under the closed-world assumption that means false. Facts that should stay true must not be deleted.
  • Assuming actions always succeed. The plan says move(table, shelf) makes the robot at the shelf. A real move can fail. A task planner needs execution monitoring and a re-plan from the actual state.
  • Planning without geometry. The symbolic plan does not know whether the shelf is reachable. Each symbolic action must be grounded by a motion planner of Lessons 5.1 to 5.5.
Where it shows up in AI
  • PDDL, the Planning Domain Definition Language, is the standard way to write domains of this kind, and classical planners solve them. Its STRIPS core is exactly the definition above.
  • Language models used as robot planners (Module 8) propose sequences of skills. Their output is checked against preconditions, the same way the lab does, before any motion is attempted.

Check yourself

1. The state is {at_robot(shelf), holding_cup}. Can place(cup, table) be applied? Why or why not?
No. Its precondition at_robot(table) is not in the state, because the robot is at the shelf. The planner would first need move(shelf, table).
2. Write the state after pick(cup, sink) from the state {at_robot(sink), at_cup(sink), handempty}.
Delete at_cup(sink) and handempty, add holding_cup. The state becomes {at_robot(sink), holding_cup}.
3. Why is a plan that is valid in the symbolic model not enough for a real robot?
The model assumes each action succeeds and changes only the facts listed. A real move can fail, a grasp can slip, and a shelf can be unreachable. The symbolic plan must be grounded in motion plans and checked against sensors during execution.

Takeaways

  • Task planning reasons over true and false facts; each action has preconditions, add effects and delete effects.
  • A plan is valid when each action's preconditions hold in turn and the goal facts hold at the end.
  • Symbolic plans are only as good as the model: execution monitoring and geometric planning close the gap.
