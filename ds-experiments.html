Module 5 · Experiments & causal inference

Does the change cause the improvement, or did something else move at the same time? This module shows how a coin flip earns the right to say "cause", how to analyse the result honestly, three ways the analysis goes wrong (peeking, many comparisons, confounding), and what is left when you cannot randomise.

5.1

A/B tests: randomisation and the difference in means

The questionA shopping app has built a "recommended for you" row. Product wants to know: does it make people spend more minutes in the app each week? Ten volunteers will try it. Whether to ship the row is the decision. What data would make that decision trustworthy, and why is "compare the people who switched it on with the people who did not" a bad way to get it?

Intuition: the coin flip as the only reason

Every user has a number we would love to see but never can: how many minutes they would spend with the row, and how many without it. For one person we only ever observe one of the two, because the person either sees the row or does not. This is the basic difficulty of causal questions: the comparison we want is between two worlds, and we live in one.

The way around it is to compare groups instead of individuals, and to make the groups interchangeable. If we split users by a fair coin flip, then nothing about a user (age, enthusiasm, device, how bored they are today) can influence which group they land in. The only systematic difference between the groups is the row. Any gap in average minutes beyond chance must be due to the row.

Compare this with letting users opt in. People who switch on a new feature tend to be the keen ones, who would spend more minutes anyway. The gap you measure mixes the effect of the row with the keenness of the people who chose it. Randomisation breaks that link, because the coin does not know who is keen.

Build it up

Name the two groups. Control (A) sees the app as it is; treatment (B) sees the new row. Write Y for the outcome, here weekly minutes. For user i, Y_i(0) is the outcome in the world with no row and Y_i(1) the outcome with it. The thing we want is the average treatment effect, the average over all users of Y_i(1)-Y_i(0).

We cannot see that difference for any one person, but we can estimate its average. Because assignment is random, the control group is a fair sample of the whole population, so its average estimates the average of Y(0); likewise the treatment group estimates the average of Y(1). Subtracting gives the estimator, the difference in means.

Worked example — ten users, one coin each

Ten volunteers were assigned by coin flip, five to each arm. Weekly minutes:

GroupUser 1User 2User 3User 4User 5MeanVariance s^2
A (control)4657352.5
B (treatment)6879572.5

Control mean: (4+6+5+7+3)/5=5. The deviations from 5 are -1,1,0,2,-2, their squares sum to 1+1+0+4+4=10, and dividing by n-1=4 gives the sample variance 2.5. The treatment arm has the same deviations around its mean 7, so its variance is also 2.5.

The estimated effect is 7-5=2 minutes per week. How much would that number wobble if the coin had fallen differently? Each arm's mean has variance s^2/n, and the two arms are independent, so the variances add:

\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{2.5/5+2.5/5}=\sqrt{1}=1. The difference is twice its standard error.

Is "twice" enough? With only five users per arm we compare to a t distribution with 8 degrees of freedom, whose two-sided 5% critical value is 2.306, so 2 does not clear it. With hundreds of users per arm the normal value 1.96 applies. Ten volunteers suggest an effect; they do not establish one. See the hypothesis-testing lesson for the logic of that comparison.

Why the standard error matters. The standard error shrinks like 1/\sqrt n. To halve it you need four times the users. Cutting the noise in each user's outcome (for example by measuring something less variable) helps just as much as collecting more users.

\hat\tau=\bar Y_B-\bar Y_A,\qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat\tau)=\sqrt{\frac{s_A^2}{n_A}+\frac{s_B^2}{n_B}}
Symbols. \hat\tau: the estimated average treatment effect. \bar Y_A,\bar Y_B: mean outcome in the control and treatment arms. n_A,n_B: users in each arm. s_A^2,s_B^2: sample variances of the outcome within each arm. \mathrm{SE}: standard error, the typical size of the random wobble of \hat\tau across repeated experiments. Under random assignment \hat\tau is an unbiased estimator of the average treatment effect: averaged over many possible coin flips it equals the truth.

What randomisation does and does not give you. It gives you an unbiased estimate of the effect on the people in the experiment, as the experiment was run. It does not give you the effect on a different population, a different season, or at a larger scale; and it assumes that one user's assignment does not change another user's outcome (no interference, which fails in marketplaces and social networks).

Try this
  1. Leave the assignment on "A coin flip" with effect 1 and watch the blue line. Early on it jumps and the band is wide; later it settles. Roughly how many users does it take before the band excludes zero?
  2. Double the noise (sd 8). Replay. Does the band take about four times as many users to reach the same width?
  3. Set the true effect to 0 and switch to "Keen users opt in to B". The difference settles at a positive number although the feature does nothing. Why does the coin flip not have this problem?
Common traps
  • Comparing adopters with non-adopters. That is an observational comparison, not an experiment; see lesson 5.5.
  • Randomising at the wrong unit. If users share a household, a team or a car, assigning by session lets the same person see both versions. Randomise at the unit that the outcome belongs to.
  • Reading a difference in means as the effect on every user. It is an average; some users may be helped and others harmed.
  • Checking balance after the fact and "fixing" it. Randomisation balances groups on average, not exactly. Check that the split looks right (a sample ratio check), but do not re-draw until you like the groups.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Model rollouts: a new ranking or recommendation model is shown to a random share of traffic, and click or revenue metrics are compared with the old model.
  • Online versus offline metrics: a model can win on a held-out set (classification metrics) and still lose in an A/B test, which is the only evidence of its effect on behaviour.
  • Language-model evaluation: human preference tests randomise which of two answers appears first, for the same reason.

Check yourself

1. Each arm has 100 users and the outcome has standard deviation 10 in both arms. What is the standard error of the difference in means?
\sqrt{10^2/100+10^2/100}=\sqrt{2}=1.414.
2. Why does random assignment let us read the difference in means as an effect of the treatment?
Because the coin is independent of everything about the user, the two groups have the same distribution of every user characteristic apart from chance. So the average outcome in the control arm estimates what the treated users would have done without treatment, and the gap is attributable to the treatment.
3. By what factor must the sample grow to halve the standard error?
Four. The standard error is proportional to 1/\sqrt n, so halving it needs n multiplied by 4.

Takeaways

  • A causal effect compares two worlds; randomisation makes two groups that differ, on average, only by the treatment.
  • The estimator is \bar Y_B-\bar Y_A with standard error \sqrt{s_A^2/n_A+s_B^2/n_B}. In the table: 7 − 5 = 2, SE = 1.
  • The error shrinks like 1/\sqrt n; randomisation removes bias, not noise.
5.2

Analysing an A/B test end to end

The questionA sign-up page test ran for two weeks. 4000 visitors saw the old page and 4000 the new one. 200 people signed up on the old page and 248 on the new. Should the team ship the new page, and what should the written result say?

Intuition: is the gap bigger than luck would make it?

New page: 6.2% sign up. Old page: 5.0%. The gap is 1.2 percentage points (pp). If the pages were truly identical, random assignment alone would still produce some gap, because which visitors landed where is chance. We ask: how large a gap would pure luck typically create, and is ours far beyond that?

This is the two-sample version of the test from hypothesis testing, specialised to conversion rates. The yardstick for "typical luck" is again a standard error, and the result is reported as a gap in standard errors (the z value) and then as an interval for the size of the effect.

Build it up

Step 1: the null story. Suppose both pages have the same true sign-up rate. Our best single estimate of that common rate pools all visitors: total sign-ups over total visitors. Under this story the variance of one arm's rate is \hat p(1-\hat p)/n, and the gap between two independent arms has the two variances added.

Worked example — the sign-up test
ArmVisitors nSign-ups xRate \hat p=x/n
Control (old page)40002005.0%
Treatment (new page)40002486.2%

Observed lift: 0.062-0.050=0.012 (1.2 pp), a relative lift of 0.012/0.050=24\%.

Pooled rate: \hat p=(200+248)/8000=0.056.

Standard error under the null: \sqrt{0.056\times0.944\times(1/4000+1/4000)}=\sqrt{0.052864\times0.0005}=0.005141.

Test statistic: z=0.012/0.005141=2.334. Two-sided p-value: 2(1-\Phi(2.334))=0.0196.

So a gap this large would arise about 2% of the time if the pages were identical. At \alpha=0.05 we reject "no difference". That is evidence that the pages differ, not yet a size.

Step 2: how big is the effect? A p-value says nothing about size. For that we build an interval for the true lift. Here we do not assume the arms share a rate; each arm keeps its own variance:

z=\frac{\hat p_B-\hat p_A}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)\left(\frac1{n_A}+\frac1{n_B}\right)}},\qquad (\hat p_B-\hat p_A)\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_A(1-\hat p_A)}{n_A}+\frac{\hat p_B(1-\hat p_B)}{n_B}}
Symbols. \hat p_A,\hat p_B: observed rates in the two arms. \hat p: the pooled rate, used only inside the test statistic because it is computed under the null. z_{1-\alpha/2}: the normal quantile, 1.96 for a 95% interval. n_A,n_B: visitors per arm. The normal approximation needs enough sign-ups and non-sign-ups in each arm (a common rule: at least about 10 of each).
Worked example — the interval

Unpooled standard error: \sqrt{0.05\times0.95/4000+0.062\times0.938/4000}=0.005139. Margin: 1.96\times0.005139=0.010073.

95% interval for the lift: 0.012\pm0.0101=[0.0019,\ 0.0221], that is 0.19 to 2.21 percentage points. As a relative lift on the 5.0% base this is about 3.9% to 44.1%.

A fair report. "The new page converted 6.2% versus 5.0% (4000 visitors per arm). The estimated lift is 1.2 pp, 95% interval 0.19 to 2.21 pp, p = 0.020. The data are compatible with a very small or a fairly large improvement." The interval, not the single number, is the finding. Here it excludes 0, matching the test, but it is wide.

Step 3: was the test big enough? Sample size should be chosen before the test, from the smallest lift worth detecting, the baseline rate, \alpha and the power you want (see errors and power). To detect 5.0% versus 6.0% with 80% power and \alpha=0.05, the usual formula gives n=\bigl(1.96\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)}+0.8416\sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)+p_2(1-p_2)}\bigr)^2/(p_2-p_1)^2\approx8158 visitors per arm, with \bar p=0.055. Our 4000 per arm were about half of that, so a true lift of 1 pp would usually be missed, and a lift that is declared significant at this size tends to be an overestimate of the truth (the "winner's curse").

Try this
  1. Keep the defaults and watch the interval. At what planned size does it first stay entirely above zero? Compare with the dotted "planned n" marker.
  2. Set the true lift to 0 and press "New draw" several times. How often does the interval at the end exclude zero? What fraction would you expect at α = 0.05?
  3. Set the lift to 0.5 pp and plan 4000 users per arm. Run a few draws. The interval is usually wide enough to include zero: a real effect that the test is too small to see.
Common traps
  • "p = 0.02, so a 98% chance the new page is better." The p-value is the probability of data this extreme if there were no difference. It is not the probability that the page is better.
  • Significant is not important. With a million visitors, a 0.01 pp lift can be significant yet worthless. Judge the interval against the smallest effect that matters.
  • Not significant is not "no effect". A wide interval that includes both 0 and a useful lift means the test was inconclusive.
  • Mixing up percent and percentage points. 5.0% to 6.2% is +1.2 pp, a 24% relative lift. Say which one.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Conversion and click-through testing for ranking models, ad systems and prompts reduces to exactly this two-proportion comparison.
  • Guardrail metrics: a new model must not make latency, error rates or complaints worse; each is tested with an interval and a pre-set tolerance.
  • Reporting model improvements: the same interval-first habit applies to a benchmark gain (see intervals and tests).

Check yourself

1. Control: 120 of 2000 convert; treatment: 150 of 2000. Compute the pooled z statistic and p-value.
Rates 6.0% and 7.5%, pooled \hat p=270/4000=0.0675. SE =\sqrt{0.0675\times0.9325\times(2/2000)}=0.007934. z=0.015/0.007934=1.891, two-sided p = 0.059: not significant at 5%.
2. For the same data, give the 95% interval for the lift. How does it agree with question 1?
Unpooled SE = 0.007930, margin 0.01554, so the interval is 0.015 ± 0.0155 = [−0.05 pp, 3.05 pp]. It just includes 0, agreeing with p = 0.059 being slightly above 0.05. The interval adds that effects from about −0.05 to 3 pp are all compatible with the data.
3. Why report the interval and not only the p-value?
The p-value only says whether zero is plausible. The interval shows the whole range of plausible effect sizes, which is what the ship decision needs, and it makes a small, imprecise result look small and imprecise.

Takeaways

  • Two-proportion test: pooled rate for the null SE, z=(\hat p_B-\hat p_A)/\mathrm{SE}. Example: z=2.334, p = 0.0196.
  • Report the lift with an interval: 1.2 pp, 95% interval 0.19 to 2.21 pp.
  • Plan the sample size in advance: 5% to 6% at 80% power needs about 8158 per arm; smaller tests overstate what they find.
5.3

Peeking, stopping early and sequential testing

The questionAn experiment is planned for four weeks. A dashboard updates every day. On day nine the test shows p = 0.04. Should the team stop and ship? What goes wrong if "stop when the p-value dips under 0.05" is the rule?

Intuition: a wandering line will touch any fence eventually

As users arrive, the test statistic z is not a steady climb toward the truth. If there is no effect at all, it is a random walk-like wander around zero. A single look at the planned end has a 5% chance of landing beyond ±1.96. But if you look many times and stop at the first look beyond ±1.96, you give the wandering line many chances to touch the fence. The more looks, the more likely some look succeeds by luck.

That is peeking (or optional stopping): the error rate of a test is guaranteed only for the procedure it was designed for. A fixed-sample test promises 5% false positives if you look once, at the planned sample size. Looking repeatedly and stopping at the first success is a different procedure with a different, larger, false-positive rate.

Build it up

How bad is it? Simulate a large number of experiments in which there is truly no effect, look at equally spaced moments, and count how many ever show |z|>1.96. From 40,000 simulated null experiments per row (illustrative, seed 7):

Number of equally spaced looks1251020
Share with a "significant" result at some look5.1%8.4%14.1%19.4%25.2%

With one look the rate is the promised 5% (the 0.1 is simulation noise). With five looks it is about 14%, nearly three times the advertised rate, and with twenty looks about one experiment in four "wins" with no effect at all. These match the classical figures for repeated significance testing.

Worked example — one invented run

A four-week test is checked at five equally spaced looks. The z statistic at each look is 0.9, 1.4, 2.1, 1.7, 1.5. A peeker who stops at the first |z|>1.96 stops at look 3 and ships, although at the planned end z is only 1.5 (p = 0.13). The "win" at look 3 was a wiggle.

A sequential design would have set stricter boundaries in advance. With an O'Brien–Fleming-type rule for five looks, the boundaries are about 4.56, 3.23, 2.63, 2.28 and 2.04. Look 3 needs |z|>2.63, and 2.1 does not qualify: the test continues, as it should. A Pocock rule uses one constant, about 2.41, at every look.

Alpha spending. The idea that makes this rigorous is to treat the 5% as a budget spent over the looks. A boundary at look j is set so that the total probability of ever crossing, under no effect, is at most 5%. An O'Brien–Fleming-style spending function spends almost nothing early (cumulative 0.00001 at 20% of the data, 0.0019 at 40%, 0.0114 at 60%, 0.0284 at 80%) and saves the rest for the end, so early stops need overwhelming evidence. A Pocock-style function spends faster (0.0148, 0.0262, 0.0354, 0.0432, 0.05 at 20, 40, 60, 80, 100%), which stops earlier more often but gives a lower power at the end. Both are spending 5% in total; they differ in when.

\alpha(t)=2-2\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt t}\right)\qquad(\text{O'Brien–Fleming type}),\qquad \Pr\bigl(\text{ever cross}\bigr)\le\alpha
Symbols. t: the information fraction, from 0 to 1, the share of the planned sample collected so far. \alpha(t): the cumulative false-positive probability allowed to be spent by time t. \Phi: the standard normal distribution function. z_{1-\alpha/2}: 1.96 for \alpha=0.05. The boundaries themselves come from solving for the cutoffs whose crossing probabilities add up to the spending; this is a preview, and the lab below finds them by simulation.

Stopping early also has a cost in accuracy: an experiment that is stopped because the estimate looks large will tend to overstate the effect, because it stopped at a lucky high. Sequential designs say the false-positive rate is controlled; they do not remove this bias in the point estimate.

Try this
  1. With effect 0, 5 looks and "Stop when |z| > 1.96", press Play. Watch the percentage of experiments that have crossed rise at each look. Does it reach about 14%?
  2. Switch to Pocock, then to O'Brien–Fleming (same looks). The final share crossing should fall back to about 5%. Which rule makes the first look almost impossible to cross, and why?
  3. Raise the true effect to 2.5 with O'Brien–Fleming. Compare the share crossing to the share you would get looking once at the end (the last line of the readout). You pay a price in power for being allowed to peek.
Common traps
  • "I only looked, I did not act." The error rate is about the decision rule. If you would have stopped given a good-looking result, you were peeking, even if this time you did not stop.
  • Sequential boundaries are not permission to look at any time. The looks and the rule are fixed in advance. Unplanned extra looks again inflate the rate.
  • Extending a test that "almost" worked. Adding more data after a disappointing result, and testing again, is peeking with one more look.
  • Always-valid p-values and Bayesian monitoring exist for continuous looking, but they are different procedures with their own assumptions, not a way to keep the fixed-sample p-value.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Continuous monitoring of model rollouts and online experiments: experimentation platforms use group-sequential or always-valid methods precisely so dashboards can be watched safely.
  • Training-run curves: stopping a run, or choosing a checkpoint, at the best validation score is a form of peeking: the chosen score is optimistic, so a separate test set is needed (cross-validation).
  • Early stopping of bad variants: bandit-style allocation (see the reinforcement-learning module on bandits) trades off learning and earning without a fixed horizon.

Check yourself

1. Why is the false-positive rate for five looks at |z| > 1.96 about 14% instead of 5%?
Each look has about a 5% chance of crossing, but the experiment is declared a success if any of the looks crosses. The looks are correlated (they share earlier data), so the rate is less than 5 × 5% = 25%, but it is still far above 5%: about 14% in the simulation.
2. In the five-look O'Brien–Fleming design above, what would the first look need to show to stop the test?
|z|>4.56. The rule is very strict early, because the experiment has seen only a fifth of its data and a large z can be produced by chance.
3. A team uses a sequential design with the right boundaries and stops at look 2 with a significant result. Is the effect estimate at stopping unbiased?
No. The false-positive rate is controlled, but an experiment that stops early because the estimate crossed a boundary tends to have a high estimate, so the effect is typically overstated. Adjusted estimators and confidence intervals exist for sequential designs.

Takeaways

  • Looking repeatedly and stopping at the first |z| > 1.96 inflates false positives: about 14% with 5 looks, 25% with 20.
  • Alpha spending treats 5% as a budget across looks; O'Brien–Fleming spends late (first boundary about 4.56 for 5 looks), Pocock spends evenly (about 2.41 each).
  • Plan the looks and the rule in advance; early stopping still tends to overstate the effect.
5.4

Multiple comparisons and the false discovery rate

The questionA test on the new checkout is run and the team inspects ten metrics: conversion, average basket, returns, speed, and so on. Six of them show p < 0.05. Six wins out of ten, surely the change works? And what if the dashboard had two hundred metrics and segments?

Intuition: buy enough lottery tickets

A p-value of 0.05 means a 1-in-20 fluke if nothing is going on. Run twenty tests on pure noise and you expect about one to light up. The fluke does not announce itself; it looks exactly like a real result. When you inspect many metrics, many segments (by country, device, new versus returning), or many model variants, the chance that something shows a significant result grows quickly. The question changes from "is this one result a fluke?" to "among all the results I am about to report, how many are flukes?"

Build it up

Family-wise error. If you run m independent tests, all with no real effect, each at level \alpha, the probability of at least one false positive is

\mathrm{FWER}=1-(1-\alpha)^m

For \alpha=0.05: m=10 gives 1-0.95^{10}=40.1\%, and m=20 gives 1-0.95^{20}=64.2\%.

Bonferroni. The simplest cure: demand p\le\alpha/m for each test. Then the chance of any false positive is at most \alpha, whatever the dependence between tests. The cost is power: for large m the threshold is tiny and real effects are missed.

False discovery rate. Often the goal is not "no false positives at all" but "of the things I declare discoveries, a small fraction are false". That fraction is the false discovery proportion, and its expected value is the false discovery rate (FDR). The Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure controls it: sort the p-values, compare the i-th smallest with the rising line i\alpha/m, find the largest i that sits at or below its line, and reject all hypotheses up to that rank.

Worked example — ten metrics

The ten p-values, sorted, with m=10 and \alpha=0.05:

Uncorrected (p < 0.05): ranks 1 to 6, six "discoveries". Bonferroni threshold 0.05/10=0.005: only rank 1 survives (0.001). BH finds two more than Bonferroni, and rejects three fewer than the uncorrected rule.

Reading it: the six uncorrected wins would be expected to contain about 10\times0.05=0.5 flukes if all ten were null, and likely more flukes than that suggests when some are real; BH says that among its three discoveries the expected fraction of false ones is at most 5% (under independence or positive dependence of the tests).

Symbols. m: number of tests. p_{(i)}: the i-th smallest p-value. \alpha: the target level (family-wise for Bonferroni, false discovery for BH). The BH rule is: reject ranks 1,\dots,k^\ast where k^\ast=\max\{i: p_{(i)}\le i\alpha/m\}. If no rank qualifies, reject nothing.

Both methods answer a different question from the single-test p-value. Bonferroni controls the probability of making any mistake (strict, good when each false positive is costly). BH controls the proportion of mistakes among your discoveries (more powerful, good for screening many candidates).

Try this
  1. Set the share of real effects to 0 and press "New draw" a few times, then "Average over 500 draws". The uncorrected rule makes about m·α false positives per draw; Bonferroni and BH make almost none. What does that cost when real effects are present?
  2. Set m = 40 and the share of real effects to 25%. Compare how many real effects each rule finds. Which rule misses the most?
  3. Make the real effects weak (z about 2). Does BH still beat Bonferroni? What do both do to the weak ones?
Common traps
  • The garden of forking paths. Even if you ran only one test in the end, if you chose it after looking at many cuts, the effective m is the number of cuts you could have run. Decide the primary metric and the segments before the test.
  • Correcting only the "interesting" results. The family is everything you looked at and would have reported, not the few that stand out.
  • Using BH to say "this specific discovery is true with 95% chance". FDR is an average property of the whole list; any single item may still be false.
  • Treating a failed correction as "no effect". A metric that does not survive Bonferroni may still be real; the test just cannot tell it from noise.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Metric dashboards with hundreds of cuts: an experimentation platform that slices every test by country, device and cohort must correct for the cuts or it will "find" segment effects constantly.
  • Model comparison and hyper-parameter search: trying 200 configurations and reporting the best test score is a multiple-comparison problem: the best is optimistic (hyper-parameter tuning).
  • Genomics and feature screening: selecting thousands of candidate features or genes uses BH so that the shortlist has a controlled share of false leads.

Check yourself

1. For 20 tests with no real effect, each at α = 0.05, what is the chance of at least one false positive, and what is the Bonferroni threshold?
1-0.95^{20}=64.2\%. Bonferroni threshold 0.05/20=0.0025; with it the chance of any false positive (independent tests) is 1-(1-0.0025)^{20}=4.88\%.
2. Four p-values: 0.003, 0.011, 0.020, 0.040. Which are rejected by Bonferroni and by BH at 0.05?
Bonferroni threshold 0.05/4 = 0.0125: rejects 0.003 and 0.011 (two). BH lines are 0.0125, 0.025, 0.0375, 0.05; 0.040 ≤ 0.05 at rank 4, so the largest qualifying rank is 4 and BH rejects all four.
3. Why is BH more powerful than Bonferroni, and what do you give up?
BH's threshold rises with rank (i\alpha/m instead of a flat \alpha/m), so it rejects more. You give up the guarantee of no false positives at all: it controls only the expected share of false discoveries among the rejections.

Takeaways

  • With m null tests at α, P(at least one false positive) = 1-(1-\alpha)^m: 40.1% for 10, 64.2% for 20.
  • Bonferroni (p\le\alpha/m) controls the family-wise error; BH (largest i with p_{(i)}\le i\alpha/m) controls the false discovery rate. Ten-metric table: uncorrected 6, Bonferroni 1, BH 3.
  • Decide the metrics and segments in advance; the family includes everything you could have reported.
5.5

Confounding and why correlation is not causation

The questionAn app's analyst notices that users who use the "smart reply" feature have 75% retention, and those who do not have 35%. Management wants to push the feature on everyone. Is smart reply worth 40 points of retention? No experiment was run, only logs. What else could explain the gap?

Intuition: a third thing pulling both strings

Suppose the people who adopt smart reply are mostly the heavy, engaged "power users". Power users would have stayed anyway. The feature gets credit for what the kind of user did. The kind of user is a confounder: a variable that influences both who gets the treatment and the outcome, creating a statistical link between treatment and outcome even if the treatment does nothing.

We can draw this as a picture of causes: a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Each node is a variable; an arrow means "directly influences". Here, power user points to uses feature and to retained; uses feature points to retained. The route treatment ← power user → outcome is a "back door": information flows between treatment and outcome along it even if the direct arrow is removed. Correlation measures all the routes together; only the direct arrow is the effect we want.

Build it up

Adjust by stratifying. If we know the confounder, we can compare within its levels: power users who adopted with power users who did not, and the same for casual users. Within a level the confounder is held fixed, so it cannot explain a difference. Then average the within-level differences, weighting each level by its share of the population.

Worked example — the smart-reply logs

Retained users out of all users, by group (counts are invented for the lesson):

Power users (400)270 of 300 = 90%85 of 100 = 85%+5 pp

The naive comparison is 75% − 35% = 40 pp. Inside each type of user the gap is only 5 pp. Stratified estimate: 0.4\times5+0.6\times5=5 pp. Most of the 40 points came from who adopted: 75% of the adopters are power users (300 of 400) against only 17% of the non-adopters (100 of 600).

The adjusted figure of 5 pp is a better estimate of the effect, if the assumptions below hold. It is not guaranteed to be right.

\hat\tau_{\text{adj}}=\sum_{z}\frac{n_z}{n}\Bigl(\bar Y_{z,T=1}-\bar Y_{z,T=0}\Bigr)
Symbols. z: a level of the confounder (power or casual). n_z: users at that level, n: all users. \bar Y_{z,T=t}: mean outcome (here retention rate) among users at level z with treatment status t. The weights n_z/n make the estimate describe the whole population.

What adjustment assumes. (1) No unmeasured confounding: every common cause of treatment and outcome is in your adjustment set. If a hidden driver (motivation, income) remains, the estimate is still biased. (2) Overlap: within each level, both treated and untreated users exist, or there is nothing to compare. (3) Right variables: adjust for things that happen before the treatment. Adjusting for a result of the treatment (a "mediator", for example "number of replies sent") can remove part of the effect you want; adjusting for something caused by both treatment and outcome (a "collider") can even create a spurious link. The DAG is how you decide, which is why it is worth drawing.

Randomisation (lesson 5.1) is the strongest fix: a coin flip has no arrow from the confounder into the treatment, so the back door is closed for every confounder, measured or not. Observational adjustment is a weaker substitute that works only as well as the confounder list.

  2. Slide the confounding strength s down toward 0 (random assignment). Watch the red line come down to meet the green one. What does that say about randomisation?

Common traps
  • Simpson's paradox: the sign can flip. Suppose treated and untreated users are split across two groups. Group A: treated 8 of 10 succeed (80%) against 70 of 100 untreated (70%). Group B: treated 30 of 100 (30%) against 2 of 10 untreated (20%). Treatment helps in both groups, yet overall treated users succeed 38 of 110 = 34.5% against 72 of 110 = 65.5% for untreated, because the treated mostly sit in the hard group B. Looking only at the pooled table gives the wrong answer; the right answer depends on the causal story, which is why the DAG, not the arithmetic, decides whether to pool or split.
  • "Controlling for everything." More variables are not always better; the wrong ones (mediators, colliders) introduce bias.
  • Believing adjustment fixes unmeasured confounding. It cannot. Say which confounders you did not measure.
  • Correlation tells you nothing? No: correlation is evidence that a causal link or a common cause exists. It just cannot tell you which.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Observational studies on logged data: measuring a feature's or model's impact from usage logs alone, where users chose or were targeted, needs adjustment and often propensity-score methods built from this idea.
  • Spurious features in models: a classifier that predicts disease from the hospital's scanner tag, or a wolf from snow in the background, is exploiting a confounder that will not hold in deployment.
  • Off-policy evaluation: estimating how a new recommendation policy would have done from data logged under an old policy corrects for the old policy's targeting, the same confounding problem.

Check yourself

1. In the smart-reply table, what share of adopters are power users, and of non-adopters?
Adopters: 300 of 400 = 75%. Non-adopters: 100 of 600 = 16.7%. The groups are very different in kind, so their retention gap is mostly about who they are.
2. Name two assumptions the stratified estimate of 5 pp relies on.
No unmeasured confounding beyond power versus casual (for example, motivation within a group), and overlap: both adopters and non-adopters exist in each stratum. Also that the adjustment variable was determined before the adoption, not as a result of it.
3. Why does randomising the treatment remove confounding without knowing what the confounders are?
Because the coin flip is independent of every characteristic of the user, there is no arrow from any confounder into the treatment. The back door is closed for all variables at once, measured or not, so the groups differ only by chance and by the treatment.

Takeaways

  • A confounder influences both treatment and outcome and creates a link even when the treatment does nothing. DAGs draw the causal story.
  • Stratify on the confounder and average the within-group differences: naive +40 pp became +5 pp.
  • Adjustment needs no unmeasured confounding, overlap, and pre-treatment variables; randomisation removes the problem outright.
5.6

Quasi-experiments: difference-in-differences and natural experiments

The questionA delivery service launches free delivery in one city, but not in a neighbouring one, and nobody randomised which. Weekly orders in the launch city rose from 100 to 130 (in hundreds). Did free delivery cause an increase of 30? And what, if anything, can we conclude when randomising was never possible?

Intuition: subtract what would have happened anyway

Two easy comparisons are both wrong. Before versus after in the launch city (130 − 100 = 30) includes everything that changed with time: a holiday season, a general growth trend, weather. Treated versus untreated after the launch (130 versus the neighbour's 95) includes every permanent difference between the cities: size, wealth, habits.

The difference-in-differences design uses both comparisons together. The neighbouring city, which did not launch, shows what time alone did. Subtract that change from the launch city's change. What is left is the part that the launch city changed more than the comparison city, which we attribute to the launch under one important assumption.

Build it up

Worked example — free delivery
Mean weekly orders (hundreds)BeforeAfterChange
Launch city (treated)100130+30
Neighbouring city (control)8095+15
Difference between cities2035+15

The control city grew by 15 with no launch, so growth explains 15 of the treated city's 30. The difference-in-differences estimate is (130-100)-(95-80)=30-15=\mathbf{15} (hundred orders a week). Read it row-wise (change in the treated minus change in the control) or column-wise (the gap between cities after, 35, minus the gap before, 20): the answer is the same, 15. Both naive numbers were wrong, 30 and 35.

\hat\tau_{\mathrm{DiD}}=\bigl(\bar Y_{T,\text{post}}-\bar Y_{T,\text{pre}}\bigr)-\bigl(\bar Y_{C,\text{post}}-\bar Y_{C,\text{pre}}\bigr)
Symbols. T and C: treated and control units. \bar Y_{g,\text{pre}},\bar Y_{g,\text{post}}: mean outcome of group g before and after the intervention date. The estimate is the extra change in the treated group.

The assumption that does all the work: parallel trends. DiD is valid if, in the absence of the intervention, the treated group's outcome would have changed by the same amount as the control's. The gap between the groups may be large (20 here) but it must stay constant over time. This is untestable after the fact, because we never see the treated city without the launch. What we can do is look at several periods before the launch: if the two lines moved together then, parallel trends is plausible; if they were already diverging, DiD is suspect. The lab draws the dashed "counterfactual" line the assumption implies.

A second assumption is no anticipation and no spillover: people do not change behaviour before the launch, and the launch does not change the control city (for example by customers crossing the border). The groups should also be stable in composition across the two periods.

Other designs when you cannot randomise.

  • Interrupted time series. There is only one group, observed for many periods before and after an intervention. Fit the pre-trend, project it forward as the counterfactual, and read the effect as the jump or slope change. The assumption is that the pre-trend would have continued and that nothing else changed on the same date. With no control group, any other event at that moment is mixed into the effect.
  • Natural experiments. Something outside anyone's control, such as a lottery, a policy cut-off date, a server outage or a rule that applies from a threshold, assigns treatment in a way that is "as good as random". They are valuable but rare, and the case that it is as good as random must be argued from how the assignment worked, not assumed.

All of these are weaker than a randomised experiment. They identify a causal effect only given assumptions about what would have happened otherwise. State them, show the checks you can (pre-period trends, other controls), and say what would have to be true for the conclusion to fail.

Common traps
  • Treating DiD as a randomised result. It is valid only under parallel trends; a confident-looking number can still be biased.
  • Choosing a control group that was picked because it looks similar after the fact. That can leak the outcome into the design. Choose controls from what was known before.
  • Regression to the mean. If the treated unit was chosen because it was doing unusually badly, it would have improved anyway, which DiD attributes to the policy. Check how units were selected.
  • Ignoring noise. A DiD with few units and weeks has wide uncertainty; report an interval, not just 15.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Rollouts without randomisation: a feature, a model or a policy launched in one region or on one platform first, evaluated against regions not yet launched.
  • Policy evaluation: the effect of a content-moderation rule, a pricing change or a regulation (for example on how an AI product is used) is often estimated by DiD or interrupted time series.
  • Staggered launches let a platform compare early and late adopters, an idea that modern DiD estimators extend carefully.

Check yourself

1. Treated group: 50 before, 58 after. Control group: 40 before, 44 after. Compute the DiD estimate.
(58-50)-(44-40)=8-4=4.
2. If the treated group's outcome was already growing 2 units per period faster than the control's, would DiD over- or under-state the effect?
It would overstate it, because the extra growth that would have happened anyway is counted as the intervention's effect. The estimate is biased upward by about 2 units per period of elapsed time. This is a violation of parallel trends.
3. What can you check in the data to judge whether parallel trends is plausible, and what can you not check?
You can plot the two groups over several periods before the intervention and see whether they moved together. You cannot check the assumption for the post period, because the treated group's no-intervention outcome is never observed.

Takeaways

  • DiD = (treated change) − (control change): 30 − 15 = 15 in the table. Before/after (30) and after-only (35) were both wrong.
  • It needs parallel trends, no anticipation and no spillover; check pre-period trends, but the post-period counterfactual cannot be checked.
  • Interrupted time series and natural experiments help when randomising is impossible, but they identify an effect only under stated assumptions.
