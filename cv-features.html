Module 2 · Features & geometry

Before deep networks, vision was geometry plus careful measurement. You will learn to find distinctive points, describe and match them across photos, fit models despite wrong matches with RANSAC, understand how a 3-D point becomes a pixel (pinhole camera), recover depth from two eyes, and measure motion between frames. These ideas still run SLAM, AR, stitching and robotics, and they explain why modern networks are built the way they are.

2.1

Corners & keypoints

The questionYou photograph a building from the left, then from a few steps to the right. A computer must say "this spot in photo 1 is that spot in photo 2". Where should it even look? A patch of blue sky matches a thousand other patches of blue sky. Which points in an image are worth tracking at all?

Intuition

Imagine you are handed a small square window cut out of an image, and you are allowed to slide the window a tiny bit in any direction and compare what you see before and after. Three things can happen.

  • Flat region (clear sky, a white wall): slide in any direction and the content looks identical. The window could be anywhere; it tells you nothing about where you are.
  • Edge (the side of a building): slide across the edge and the content changes a lot; slide along the edge and nothing changes. You know your position in one direction only. This is the aperture problem, and it returns in the optical-flow lesson.
  • Corner (the corner of a window frame): slide in any direction and the content changes. You can pin down your position in both directions.

A good keypoint (also called an interest point or feature) is therefore a place where the image changes in every direction. Corners, blob centres and the tips of thin structures all qualify. That is the entire idea; the rest of the lesson is making it measurable.

Build it up

Step 1: measure change. Take a window W around pixel (x,y). Shift it by (u,v) and add up the squared brightness differences:

E(u,v)=\sum_{(x,y)\in W}\big[I(x+u,\,y+v)-I(x,y)\big]^2

A corner is a point where E is large for every small shift (u,v)\neq 0.

Step 2: replace the image by its slope. For a tiny shift, brightness changes like a straight line: I(x+u,y+v)\approx I(x,y)+I_x u+I_y v, where I_x,I_y are the horizontal and vertical gradients (how fast brightness rises per pixel; you met them as Sobel kernels in module 1). Substituting, the difference is I_xu+I_yv, so

E(u,v)\approx\begin{pmatrix}u&v\end{pmatrix}M\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=\sum_{W}\begin{pmatrix}I_x^2&I_xI_y\\ I_xI_y&I_y^2\end{pmatrix}

The 2×2 matrix M is the structure tensor (or second-moment matrix). It summarises the gradients inside the window. Its two eigenvalues \lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge 0 are exactly the amount of change along the best and the worst shift direction (that is what an eigenvalue means: the stretch factor along a special direction). So:

  • both λ tiny → flat;
  • one λ large, one tiny → edge;
  • both λ large → corner.

Step 3: avoid computing eigenvalues. Harris and Stephens (1988) noticed that \det M=\lambda_1\lambda_2 and \operatorname{tr}M=\lambda_1+\lambda_2 carry the same information and are cheap. The Harris response is

R=\det M-k\,(\operatorname{tr}M)^2=\lambda_1\lambda_2-k(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^2

with k\approx0.04\text{–}0.06. Large positive R means corner; negative R means edge (one λ dominates, so the trace term wins); near zero means flat.

Worked example: three windows, three verdicts

Take k=0.05 and three structure tensors written as (\sum I_x^2,\ \sum I_xI_y,\ \sum I_y^2).

Corner: M=\begin{pmatrix}9&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}. \det=9\cdot5-3\cdot3=36, \operatorname{tr}=14, so R=36-0.05\cdot14^2=36-9.8=\mathbf{26.2}. Eigenvalues: 7\pm\sqrt{4+9}=10.61,\ 3.39. Both are large: corner.

Edge: M=\begin{pmatrix}16&0\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix} (all gradients point in x: a vertical edge). \det=8, \operatorname{tr}=16.5, R=8-0.05\cdot272.25=\mathbf{-5.6}. λ = 16 and 0.5: negative R, edge.

Flat: M=\begin{pmatrix}0.4&0.1\\0.1&0.3\end{pmatrix}. \det=0.11, \operatorname{tr}=0.7, R=0.11-0.0245=\mathbf{0.0855}: close to zero compared with 26.2, so flat.

Scale: how big is the corner?

A fixed 5×5 window sees a corner at one zoom level only. Zoom into a rounded corner and it becomes an edge; zoom out and a whole building's outline becomes a "corner". If photo 2 is taken closer than photo 1, the same point looks bigger. The fix is scale space: blur the image with Gaussians of increasing width \sigma (like looking from further away) and search for keypoints in (x, y, σ). SIFT subtracts neighbouring blur levels (a difference of Gaussians, DoG, which approximates the Laplacian of Gaussian) and keeps points that are extreme among their 26 neighbours in space and scale. The winning σ is the keypoint's scale, and it later sets the size of the patch to describe.

The formal definition

R(x,y)=\det M-k\,\operatorname{tr}(M)^2,\qquad M(x,y)=\sum_{(i,j)\in W}w_{ij}\begin{pmatrix}I_x^2&I_xI_y\\ I_xI_y&I_y^2\end{pmatrix}
I(x,y)image brightness at a pixel
I_x,I_yhorizontal / vertical brightness gradient (finite differences or Sobel)
W,\ w_{ij}the window around the pixel and optional weights (a Gaussian puts more weight on the centre)
Mstructure tensor: sums of gradient products over the window
\lambda_1,\lambda_2eigenvalues of M: change along the strongest / weakest direction
\det M,\ \operatorname{tr}M\lambda_1\lambda_2 and \lambda_1+\lambda_2
ksmall constant (0.04–0.06) that penalises edges
Rcorner response: threshold it and keep local maxima (non-maximum suppression)

The practical recipe: compute I_x,I_y; form the three products; blur them (that is the window sum); compute R everywhere; keep pixels where R is above a threshold and a local maximum.

Try this
  1. Let the tour run. Watch the three panels as the window visits sky, an edge and a corner. In the right panel the shape of E(\theta) is a dot, a two-lobed dumbbell, or a fat round blob.
  2. Drag the window onto the straight wall edge. Why is R negative there? (One eigenvalue dominates, so the squared-trace term is larger than the determinant.)
  3. Raise k from 0.03 to 0.12. Corners that are almost edges disappear first.
  4. Increase the window size. Detections become fewer and blurrier: bigger windows see coarser structure, the scale idea in miniature.
Common traps
  • A corner response is not invariant to scale. Harris alone fails if photo 2 is zoomed; SIFT's scale space solves that.
  • Pixel gradients are noisy. Without the window sum (or Gaussian weights) the tensor has rank 1 everywhere and every pixel looks like an edge.
  • Thresholds depend on the image's contrast. Normalise (e.g. a fraction of the maximum R) instead of a fixed number.
  • A strong response in a textured patch (grass, noise) is not a stable landmark: it moves if lighting changes. Distinctive and repeatable are two different requirements.
Where it shows up in AI
  • SLAM and visual odometry (ORB-SLAM, phone AR) track hundreds of corner keypoints per frame (FAST/Harris/ORB) to estimate where the camera is.
  • Trackers such as KLT pick "good features to track" with the same \lambda_{\min} test.
  • Learned keypoints (SuperPoint, R2D2, DISK) are CNNs trained to output a heatmap like this R; the idea of a response map plus non-maximum suppression is identical.
  • The edge/corner/flat reasoning explains what the first convolutional layers of every CNN learn (oriented edges, corner-like detectors).

Check yourself

1. A window has M = [[4,0],[0,4]]. With k = 0.05, what is R, and what is the verdict?
det = 16, tr = 8, R = 16 − 0.05·64 = 12.8 > 0, and λ₁ = λ₂ = 4: a corner (gradients in every direction).
2. Why does a perfectly straight edge give a negative R?
Gradients are all parallel, so λ₂ ≈ 0 and det ≈ 0, while tr = λ₁ is large. R = λ₁λ₂ − k(λ₁+λ₂)² ≈ −kλ₁² < 0.
3. You zoom the camera in 2× between two photos. Which part of SIFT handles that and how?
Scale space: keypoints are detected as extrema over (x, y, σ) of a difference-of-Gaussians pyramid. The same physical point is found at σ in one photo and about 2σ in the other, so patch sizes scale together.

Takeaways

  • Distinctive = the window changes when shifted in every direction.
  • Structure tensor M summarises gradients; its eigenvalues classify flat / edge / corner.
  • Harris R = det − k·tr² avoids eigen-decomposition; threshold + non-maximum suppression gives keypoints.
  • Scale space (DoG) makes detection work across zoom levels.
2.2

Descriptors & matching

The questionYou found 500 corners in photo 1 and 480 in photo 2. A corner is just a coordinate: (212,87) says nothing about what the surroundings look like. How can the computer decide which corner in photo 2 is the same physical point, even if the camera has rotated, zoomed or the light has changed?

Intuition

Think of describing a person to a friend who must pick them out of a crowd. You do not give coordinates; you give a short description ("tall, red coat, glasses"). A descriptor is that description for an image patch: a short list of numbers computed from the pixels around a keypoint, designed so that the same physical point gives similar numbers in both photos, and different points give different numbers.

Raw pixel values make poor descriptors: brighten the photo and every number changes. So descriptors use quantities that survive such changes: gradient directions (insensitive to overall brightness), histograms (insensitive to tiny shifts) and comparisons ("is pixel A brighter than pixel B?").

Build it up

SIFT (Lowe, 1999/2004) in four moves:

  1. Orientation. Find the dominant gradient direction around the keypoint (a histogram of gradient angles; take the peak). Rotate the patch so that direction points "up". Now a rotated photo gives the same patch.
  2. Scale. Cut the patch with a size proportional to the keypoint's scale σ, so zoom changes do not matter.
  3. Grid of histograms. Split the 16×16 patch into 4×4 cells. In each cell, make an 8-bin histogram of gradient orientations weighted by gradient strength.
  4. Normalise. 4×4 cells × 8 bins = 128 numbers; scale the vector to unit length (and clip large entries) to cancel contrast changes.

ORB is the fast, free, binary cousin: take 256 pairs of pixels in a (rotated) patch, record 1 if the first is brighter than the second, else 0. The descriptor is 256 bits; comparing two uses the Hamming distance (the number of differing bits), computed with one XOR and a bit-count, hundreds of times faster than floating-point distances.

Matching. Two descriptors \mathbf p,\mathbf q are "similar" when their distance is small:

d(\mathbf p,\mathbf q)=\lVert\mathbf p-\mathbf q\rVert_2=\sqrt{\sum_{i}(p_i-q_i)^2}

For each descriptor in photo 1, the nearest neighbour in photo 2 is the candidate match. Brute-force search checks all of them; large databases use approximate search (k-d trees, FLANN, HNSW, product quantisation).

But is the nearest neighbour correct? If the keypoint's true partner was occluded or missed by the detector, the nearest neighbour is some unrelated point, and its distance can still be small. A fixed distance threshold cannot distinguish a good match from a lucky wrong one, because "small" depends on how crowded that region of descriptor space is. Lowe's insight: compare the best match with the second best. A true match is clearly better than every other candidate; a wrong or repetitive match has several equally plausible competitors. Accept only if

\frac{d_1}{d_2}<\rho\qquad(\rho\approx0.7\text{–}0.8)

where d_1 is the distance to the nearest and d_2 to the second-nearest descriptor. Lowe reported that \rho=0.8 removes about 90% of the false matches while losing under 5% of the correct ones.

Worked example: distances and the ratio test

Use tiny 4-number descriptors. Query \mathbf q=(4,1,0,3). Candidates in photo 2:

Case A (distinctive point). \mathbf a=(3,1,1,3): \mathbf q-\mathbf a=(1,0,-1,0), d_1=\sqrt{1+0+1+0}=1.414. Next best \mathbf b=(0,5,4,1): (4,-4,-4,2), d_2=\sqrt{16+16+16+4}=\sqrt{52}=7.211. Ratio 1.414/7.211=0.196<0.8: accept.

Case B (repetitive texture, e.g. two identical windows). Nearest \mathbf c=(4,1,2,3): (0,0,-2,0), d_1=2.000. Runner-up \mathbf d=(3,3,0,3): (1,-2,0,0), d_2=\sqrt5=2.236. Ratio 2.000/2.236=0.894\ge0.8: reject. The match might be right, but with an almost-as-good rival it is too risky.

Note that both nearest distances (1.41 and 2.0) are small. A distance threshold alone could not have told these cases apart.

The formal definition

\mathcal M=\Big\{(i,j_1(i)):\ d\big(\mathbf p_i,\mathbf q_{j_1}\big)<\tau\ \ \text{and}\ \ \frac{d(\mathbf p_i,\mathbf q_{j_1})}{d(\mathbf p_i,\mathbf q_{j_2})}<\rho\Big\}
\mathbf p_idescriptor of keypoint i in image 1 (e.g. 128 numbers for SIFT, 256 bits for ORB)
\mathbf q_jdescriptor of keypoint j in image 2
j_1,j_2indices of the nearest and second-nearest descriptors in image 2
dEuclidean (float descriptors) or Hamming (binary) distance
\tauoptional absolute distance threshold
\rhoLowe ratio threshold, typically 0.7–0.8

A further filter is the cross-check (mutual nearest neighbours): keep the pair only if i is also the nearest match of j when searching in the opposite direction. The surviving matches still contain wrong ones, which is why the next lesson (RANSAC) exists.

Try this
  1. Press play and follow the first keypoints. Each step shows the nearest (solid) and second-nearest (thin) candidate, the three descriptor bar-charts and the ratio.
  2. Set the ratio slider to 1.0 (or switch the ratio test off). Red lines appear: wrong matches, mostly between the two identical-looking windows.
  3. Lower the ratio to 0.6. The wrong lines vanish, but so do some correct ones. That is the precision / recall trade-off.
  4. Raise the descriptor noise (a darker photo, blur, a different camera). Correct matches get harder and the ratio test has to work harder.
Common traps
  • A smaller distance is not the same as a correct match. Repetitive scenes (windows, tiles, fences) produce confident wrong matches; the ratio test is the cure.
  • Never apply Euclidean distance to binary descriptors or Hamming to floats.
  • The ratio test needs two candidates; with one candidate you cannot compute it.
  • Descriptors are invariant only to the transformations they were designed for (SIFT: rotation, scale, brightness; not large viewpoint changes or day/night).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Image retrieval and place recognition: bag-of-visual-words clusters millions of SIFT descriptors; Google Lens-style search and loop closing in SLAM match descriptors against a database with approximate nearest-neighbour search.
  • AR and 3-D reconstruction (COLMAP, photogrammetry) start by matching SIFT or learned features across thousands of photos.
  • Learned descriptors: SuperPoint+SuperGlue/LightGlue replace hand-built histograms with CNN features and replace the ratio test with an attention network that reasons about all matches jointly.
  • The same "embed, then nearest neighbour" idea is how face recognition, CLIP image search and retrieval-augmented generation work.

Check yourself

1. Two ORB descriptors of 256 bits differ in 20 positions. What is their distance, and why use it instead of Euclidean?
Hamming distance = 20. It is one XOR plus a popcount, extremely fast, and for binary vectors it is the natural count of disagreements.
2. d₁ = 0.30 and d₂ = 0.33. Accept at ρ = 0.8? What does that say about the point?
Ratio = 0.909, so reject. Two descriptors are almost equally close: the point is ambiguous (repeated texture or a crowded part of descriptor space).
3. Why does SIFT rotate each patch to its dominant orientation before building histograms?
So the descriptor describes the patch relative to its own orientation. If the camera rolls, the dominant direction rolls with it and the rotated patch (and therefore the 128 numbers) stays the same.

Takeaways

  • A descriptor is a short vector computed from a keypoint's neighbourhood that stays stable under rotation, scale and brightness.
  • SIFT: 4×4 cells × 8 orientation bins = 128 numbers. ORB: 256 comparison bits, Hamming distance.
  • Match by nearest neighbour, accept with the Lowe ratio d₁/d₂ < ~0.8, optionally cross-check.
  • Some wrong matches always survive; robust model fitting (RANSAC) removes them.
2.3

RANSAC & homography

The questionYou have 200 matches between two photos of a street, and 40% of them are wrong. You want the geometric relationship between the photos (so you can stitch them into a panorama). Ordinary least squares uses every point, so a few wildly wrong matches drag the answer far from the truth. How can you fit a model when you do not know which points to trust?

Intuition

Suppose you want the line through a cloud of points, and a third of the points are garbage. Least squares averages the garbage in. Here is a cleverer idea: guess, then check. Pick the smallest set of points that defines a line (two). Draw the line through them. Count how many other points agree with it. If you were unlucky and picked a garbage point, almost nobody agrees. If you got two good points, most of the good points agree. Repeat many times and keep the line that gathered the most support. Random sample consensus (RANSAC, Fischler and Bolles 1981) is exactly this vote.

Why does it work? Garbage points do not agree with each other, but good points all agree with the same model. Votes pile up only for the truth.

Build it up

The loop. Repeat N times:

  1. Pick a minimal sample of s data points at random (s = 2 for a line, 4 for a homography).
  2. Fit the model to that sample exactly.
  3. Count inliers: points whose error to the model is below a threshold t.
  4. If this model has more inliers than the best so far, keep it.

At the end, refit the model by least squares using only the best model's inliers (now clean, so least squares is exactly right).

How many iterations? Let w be the fraction of inliers (so 1-w are outliers). One random point is an inlier with probability w; s points are all inliers with probability w^s (if you ignore tiny corrections for sampling without replacement). So one iteration fails with probability 1-w^s, and N iterations all fail with probability (1-w^s)^N. The probability that at least one sample was clean is

P_{\text{success}}=1-\big(1-w^{s}\big)^{N}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N=\left\lceil\frac{\ln(1-P)}{\ln(1-w^{s})}\right\rceil

Notice how brutal the exponent s is: needing 4 good points instead of 2 squares the pain.

Worked example: how many iterations?

Line, w = 0.5, s = 2. w^s=0.25, so one try fails with probability 0.75. After 10 tries: 1-0.75^{10}=0.944. For 99%: N=\ln0.01/\ln0.75=16.01, so 17 iterations.

Homography, w = 0.5, s = 4. w^s=0.0625. After 10 tries: 1-0.9375^{10}=0.476. For 99%: N=\ln0.01/\ln0.9375=71.36 so 72 iterations.

Homography, w = 0.3 (70% outliers). w^4=0.0081, N=\ln0.01/\ln0.9919=566.2 so 567 iterations. Still cheap per iteration (one 8×8 solve), which is why RANSAC is practical. In real code w is unknown, so it is estimated from the best inlier ratio found so far and N is shrunk adaptively.

The homography

When two photos see a flat surface (a wall, a poster, the ground), or when the camera only rotates about its centre (a panorama sweep), the mapping from a pixel in photo 1 to photo 2 is a homography: a 3×3 matrix H acting on homogeneous coordinates.

\begin{pmatrix}x'\\y'\\w\end{pmatrix}\sim H\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad u=\frac{x'}{w},\ \ v=\frac{y'}{w}

Homogeneous coordinates add a third coordinate "1" so that the perspective division (dividing by w) can be written as a matrix product. H is defined only up to scale (multiplying all nine numbers by 3 gives the same mapping), so it has 8 degrees of freedom. Each point correspondence (x,y)\to(u,v) gives two equations (one for u, one for v), hence 4 correspondences are the minimal sample: s = 4. Fixing h_{33}=1 turns the 8 equations into an 8×8 linear system (the Direct Linear Transform).

Panorama pipeline: detect keypoints, describe, match with ratio test, RANSAC-fit H (inlier test: reprojection error \lVert H\mathbf x-\mathbf x'\rVert<t, usually 1–3 px), refit on inliers, warp photo 1 into photo 2's frame and blend the overlap.

Try this
  1. Mode line, outliers 40%. Press play and read the four captions per iteration: pick, fit, count, keep or discard. Tick "least squares on all points" and see how far it is from the truth.
  2. Raise the outlier ratio to 70% and watch the "iterations needed" number in the readout explode. Use Run 50 fast and see if the best model still finds the truth.
  3. Make the threshold tiny: even the best model has few inliers. Make it huge: everything is an inlier and the vote means nothing.
  4. Switch to homography. Four points in the left photo define a warped quadrilateral in the right one. Press Refit on inliers at the end.
Common traps
  • Degenerate samples: three collinear points cannot define a homography. Good implementations reject such samples.
  • The threshold t is in pixels of your geometry, not a magic number; set it from the expected noise (about 2σ–3σ of a good match).
  • RANSAC is random: the same data gives slightly different answers. Fix a seed if you need repeatability.
  • A homography is exact only for a plane or a pure rotation. For general 3-D scenes use the fundamental matrix (stereo lesson), s = 7 or 8, the same loop.
  • Counting inliers is not always the best score; MSAC/MAGSAC use soft scores and fewer parameters to tune.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Panorama stitching (phone cameras, OpenCV Stitcher) and document scanning (rectifying a photographed page).
  • Structure from motion and SLAM (COLMAP, ORB-SLAM) run RANSAC for every image pair to estimate relative pose from matches.
  • Learned matchers (SuperGlue, LoFTR) still pass their matches to RANSAC (usually MAGSAC++) for the final geometry.
  • The idea, "fit on random minimal subsets and vote", is general: line, plane and cylinder fitting in LiDAR point clouds, 3-D object registration and robust regression.

Check yourself

1. With 50% inliers, how many RANSAC iterations give 99% confidence for a line (s = 2)?
w^s = 0.25; N = ln 0.01 / ln 0.75 = 16.01, so 17 iterations.
2. Why are four point correspondences exactly enough to determine a homography?
H has 9 numbers but only 8 degrees of freedom (scale is irrelevant). Each correspondence gives 2 independent equations (x' and y'), so 4 correspondences give 8 equations for 8 unknowns.
3. You have 90% outliers (w = 0.1) and want a homography. Roughly what happens to N?
w^4 = 0.0001; N = ln 0.01 / ln 0.9999 ≈ 46,000 iterations. Better to improve the matcher (tighter ratio test) or use a smaller model (e.g. rotation only, s = 2 or 3).

Takeaways

  • RANSAC: sample a minimal set, fit, count inliers within a threshold, keep the best, then refit on the inliers.
  • P(success) = 1 − (1 − w^s)^N; fewer parameters (smaller s) and more inliers need far fewer iterations.
  • A homography (8 DOF, s = 4) relates two views of a plane or a rotating camera: the basis of panoramas.
  • Least squares is only safe after outliers have been removed.
2.4

The pinhole camera

The questionA robot sees a red cone at pixel (412,300). Where is the cone in the room, in metres? And the other way round: if you know a cone stands 5 m in front of the camera, 0.5 m to the right, at which pixel will it appear? To answer either question you need the exact rule that turns a 3-D point into a 2-D pixel.

Intuition

The simplest camera is a dark box with a tiny hole in one face. Each point of the world sends light in all directions, but only the ray that passes through the hole reaches the back wall, so each world point lights exactly one spot on the wall. The image is upside-down. We usually draw the "virtual" image plane in front of the hole so the picture is upright, with the same geometry.

Two facts follow. First, everything on one ray through the hole lands on the same pixel, so one image cannot tell you depth (the next lesson fixes this with two cameras). Second, far things look smaller: the same 1 m object subtends a smaller angle at 10 m than at 2 m. The size on the sensor is object size × f / distance, where f is the distance from the hole to the sensor, the focal length.

Build it up

Step 1: camera coordinates. Put the origin at the hole (the optical centre). The z-axis points forward along the viewing direction, x to the right, y down (the convention of OpenCV and of pixel rows). A point (X_c,Y_c,Z_c) with Z_c>0 is in front of the camera. Similar triangles (a ray at slope X_c/Z_c hits the plane at distance f at height fX_c/Z_c) give the perspective division:

x=f\,\frac{X_c}{Z_c},\qquad y=f\,\frac{Y_c}{Z_c}

Step 2: to pixels. The sensor is a grid of pixels with the origin at a corner, not at the centre. If f is expressed in pixels (focal length in mm divided by pixel size in mm) and (c_x,c_y) is the principal point (where the optical axis hits the sensor, usually near the image centre):

u=f_x\frac{X_c}{Z_c}+c_x,\qquad v=f_y\frac{Y_c}{Z_c}+c_y

In matrix form with homogeneous coordinates, the intrinsic matrix K packs these numbers, and the division by Z_c happens at the end:

\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{pmatrix}\sim K\begin{pmatrix}X_c\\Y_c\\Z_c\end{pmatrix},\qquad K=\begin{pmatrix}f_x&s&c_x\\0&f_y&c_y\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

Step 3: where is the camera? The world has its own coordinates \mathbf X_w (a room, a map). The camera sits somewhere and points somewhere: a rotation R (3×3, orthonormal) and translation t (3-vector), the extrinsics, move world coordinates into camera coordinates:

\mathbf X_c=R\,\mathbf X_w+\mathbf t,\qquad \lambda\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{pmatrix}=K\,[R\,|\,\mathbf t]\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf X_w\\1\end{pmatrix}

(If the camera centre is at \mathbf C in world coordinates, then \mathbf t=-R\mathbf C.) The 3×4 matrix P=K[R|\mathbf t] is the complete camera.

Step 4: real lenses bend straight lines. A lens adds radial distortion: with normalised coordinates x_n=X_c/Z_c, y_n=Y_c/Z_c and r^2=x_n^2+y_n^2, the lens moves points to (x_n,y_n)(1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4) before K is applied. k_1<0 gives barrel distortion (fish-eye bulge), k_1>0 pincushion. Calibration estimates K and the k's from photos of a chessboard (Zhang's method), and then images can be undistorted.

Worked example: projecting a point

Let f_x=f_y=800 px, (c_x,c_y)=(320,240) (a 640×480 image), no skew.

(a) Camera at the origin, R = I, t = 0. Point \mathbf X=(0.5,\,-0.25,\,5): u=800\cdot0.5/5+320=400, v=800\cdot(-0.25)/5+240=200. The point is 0.5 m right of the axis and 0.25 m above it (y is down), so it lands right of and above the centre: pixel (400, 200).

(b) With extrinsics. Rotate 30° about the vertical axis: R=\begin{pmatrix}0.866&0&-0.5\\0&1&0\\0.5&0&0.866\end{pmatrix}, \mathbf t=(0.2,0,1), world point \mathbf X_w=(1,\,0.5,\,4).

Rotate: R\mathbf X_w=(0.866-2,\ 0.5,\ 0.5+3.464)=(-1.134,\ 0.5,\ 3.964). Add t: \mathbf X_c=(-0.934,\ 0.5,\ 4.964). Now project: u=800\cdot(-0.934)/4.964+320=-150.5+320=\mathbf{169.5}, v=800\cdot0.5/4.964+240=80.6+240=\mathbf{320.6}.

Depth is 4.964 > 0, so the point is in front of the camera. Pixel (169.5, 320.6) is inside the 640×480 image.

(c) Field of view. \text{FoV}_x=2\arctan\frac{W/2}{f}=2\arctan\frac{320}{800}=2\cdot21.8^\circ=43.6^\circ. Doubling f to 1600 gives 22.6°: zooming in.

The formal definition

\tilde{\mathbf u}\sim K\,[R\,|\,\mathbf t]\,\tilde{\mathbf X}_w,\qquad u=\frac{p_1\cdot\tilde{\mathbf X}_w}{p_3\cdot\tilde{\mathbf X}_w},\ \ v=\frac{p_2\cdot\tilde{\mathbf X}_w}{p_3\cdot\tilde{\mathbf X}_w}
\mathbf X_w=(X,Y,Z)a 3-D point in world coordinates (metres)
\tilde{\mathbf X}_wthe same point in homogeneous form (X,Y,Z,1)
R,\ \mathbf textrinsics: rotation (3 DOF) and translation (3 DOF) from world to camera frame
Kintrinsics: f_x,f_y focal lengths in pixels, c_x,c_y principal point, s skew (almost always 0)
P=K[R|\mathbf t]3×4 projection matrix; rows p_1,p_2,p_3
(u,v)pixel coordinates (u to the right, v down)
k_1,k_2radial distortion coefficients applied to normalised coordinates

Intrinsics: 4 numbers (5 with skew). Extrinsics: 6 numbers. A calibrated camera therefore has just a handful of unknowns, which is why a few chessboard photos are enough.

Try this
  1. Watch the orange pulses travel along the rays from the cube, through the image plane, into the pinhole. The dot where each ray crosses the plane is that vertex's pixel.
  2. Increase the focal length f: the field-of-view wedge narrows, the plane in the top view shrinks and the cube fills more of the image (zoom).
  3. Move the cube nearer (distance slider) and see the cube grow: size ∝ 1/Z. Move the camera sideways or yaw it and watch the image slide.
  4. Set k₁ to −0.25: straight cube edges bend outward (barrel distortion). Choose a vertex and read the full arithmetic in the readout.
Common traps
  • Coordinate conventions differ: OpenCV uses y down and z forward; OpenGL uses y up and z backward; robotics often uses x forward. Always check before copying formulas.
  • Points with Z_c\le0 are behind the camera: the formula still gives numbers but they are meaningless. Test depth first.
  • The extrinsic \mathbf t is not the camera position: \mathbf C=-R^{\top}\mathbf t.
  • Focal length in mm and in pixels differ by the pixel size; a zoom changes f, a crop changes (c_x,c_y), a resize scales f and c by the same factor.
  • Depth is lost in projection: (u,v) alone gives a ray, not a point.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Calibration is the first step of any robotics or driving stack; every multi-camera and camera+LiDAR fusion pipeline uses K, R, t to project 3-D points into images.
  • AR (ARKit, ARCore) estimates the camera's R, t every frame so virtual objects are drawn with the same projection.
  • 3-D vision networks: monocular depth, NeRF and 3-D Gaussian splatting render by projecting rays through pixels with exactly this model; detection in 3-D (BEVFormer, DETR3D) uses K to lift image features into the world.
  • Data augmentation must respect it: random crops and zooms change K, so geometry-aware models need the adjusted K.

Check yourself

1. K has f = 500, c = (320, 240). Where does the camera-frame point (1, 0, 10) project?
u = 500·1/10 + 320 = 370, v = 500·0/10 + 240 = 240. Pixel (370, 240).
2. Why can a single image not tell you the depth of a point?
Every point on the ray (kX, kY, kZ), for any k > 0, projects to the same pixel because the division by Z cancels k. A second view (or a known size, or a learned prior) is needed.
3. The camera centre is C = (2, 0, 0) with R = I. What is t, and where is the world origin in camera coordinates?
t = −R C = (−2, 0, 0). The world origin maps to X_c = R·0 + t = (−2, 0, 0): two metres to the left of the camera.

Takeaways

  • u = f·X/Z + c: perspective division by depth makes far things small.
  • K (intrinsics) turns camera coordinates into pixels; [R|t] (extrinsics) turns world coordinates into camera coordinates.
  • One pixel is a ray; depth needs another view or extra knowledge.
  • Real lenses add radial distortion that calibration estimates and removes.
2.5

Stereo & depth

The questionA self-driving car must know how far the car ahead is, using only cameras. One image gives a ray, not a distance. Humans manage with two eyes. How exactly does a small difference between the left and right pictures reveal metres?

Intuition

Hold a finger at arm's length and close one eye, then the other: the finger jumps sideways against the background. Now bring the finger close to your nose: it jumps much more. The size of the jump is the disparity, and it is bigger for near objects. Distant mountains hardly jump at all. So disparity measures closeness; the job is to turn it into a number of metres.

Where do you look for the match? Not anywhere in the other image. Because both cameras sit at the same height, a point seen on row 200 in the left image must be on row 200 in the right image: it can only have moved horizontally. The search shrinks from a 2-D area to a 1-D line (the epipolar line).

Build it up

Setup. Two identical cameras, focal length f (pixels), looking the same way, separated by the baseline B (metres) along x. A point is at depth Z and lateral position X (measured from the midpoint). From the pinhole model of the last lesson:

x_L=f\,\frac{X+B/2}{Z},\qquad x_R=f\,\frac{X-B/2}{Z}

(measured from each image's principal point). Subtract:

d=x_L-x_R=\frac{f\,B}{Z}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Z=\frac{f\,B}{d}

Three consequences to remember: depth is inversely proportional to disparity; a longer baseline or longer focal length gives more disparity (more resolution in depth); and a disparity of 0 means infinitely far.

Epipolar geometry. For general camera poses, a pixel in image 1 constrains its match in image 2 to lie on a line. This is encoded in the 3×3 fundamental matrix F: the match \mathbf x' of \mathbf x satisfies \mathbf x'^{\top}F\,\mathbf x=0. With calibrated cameras it becomes the essential matrix E=[\mathbf t]_\times R. Rectification warps both images so the epipolar lines become horizontal image rows, giving the simple setup above.

Finding disparity. For each pixel in the left image, slide a small window along the same row in the right image, compute a matching cost (sum of absolute differences, or a learned cost), and take the shift with the lowest cost. Doing this for all pixels gives a disparity map. Semi-global matching (SGM) and modern networks (RAFT-Stereo, PSMNet) add smoothness so that textureless regions get sensible values.

Worked example: disparity to depth (and its error)

A stereo rig has f=700 px and B=0.12 m, so fB=84 px·m. A pixel has disparity d=21 px.

Z=fB/d=84/21=\mathbf{4.0} m.

Sensitivity. If the disparity estimate is off by one pixel: d=20\Rightarrow Z=4.2 m (+0.2); d=22\Rightarrow Z=3.82 m (−0.18). For small errors, \Delta Z\approx\frac{Z^2}{fB}\Delta d=\frac{16}{84}\cdot1=0.19 m.

Now put the same object at 20 m: d=84/20=4.2 px, and the same one-pixel error gives \Delta Z\approx400/84=4.8 m. Depth error grows with the square of distance: stereo is precise near the camera and weak far away. To see 100 m reliably you need a long baseline (trucks use 0.3–1 m) or high resolution.

The formal definition

Z=\frac{f\,B}{d},\qquad \Delta Z\approx\frac{Z^{2}}{f\,B}\,\Delta d
Zdepth: distance along the optical axis (not the straight-line range)
ffocal length in pixels
Bbaseline: distance between the two camera centres, in metres
d=x_L-x_Rdisparity in pixels (positive for points in front of the rig)
\Delta ddisparity error (matching accuracy, often 0.1–1 px)
F,\ Efundamental / essential matrix describing epipolar geometry

  2. Double the baseline. Every disparity doubles; the curve steepens and the grey one-pixel error band narrows: more depth precision.

Common traps
  • Disparity is measured in pixels, depth in metres: f must be in pixels and B in the unit you want the answer in.
  • Texture-less walls, repeating patterns, reflections and occlusions (a point visible to one camera only) break matching: those pixels need filling in.
  • Rectification quality matters: if rows do not align (a 2-pixel vertical error), matching fails silently.
  • Depth resolution is not uniform: it is fine near and coarse far; do not average depths without thinking about this.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Self-driving and ADAS: Subaru EyeSight, Tesla-style multi-camera rigs and Mobileye use stereo or multi-view depth next to deep networks.
  • Depth cameras: Intel RealSense (active stereo), many robot vacuums and drones.
  • Deep stereo networks (PSMNet, GwcNet, RAFT-Stereo) build a 3-D cost volume over (x, y, disparity) and process it with 3-D convolutions, exactly the sliding comparison above, but learned.
  • Monocular depth models (MiDaS, Depth Anything) are trained with stereo or LiDAR depth as targets so one image can approximate the same output.

Check yourself

1. f = 1000 px, B = 0.5 m, d = 25 px. What is Z?
Z = 1000·0.5/25 = 20 m.
2. Which gives more precise depth at 30 m: B = 0.1 m or B = 0.4 m (same f, same Δd)? By what factor?
B = 0.4 m. ΔZ ∝ 1/B, so the error is 4× smaller.
3. Why is the correspondence search 1-D after rectification?
Both cameras have the same orientation and are displaced along x only, so the viewing ray of a left pixel projects onto the same row of the right image: the epipolar lines are the image rows.

Takeaways

  • Disparity d = x_L − x_R; depth Z = f·B/d.
  • Matching is searched along the epipolar line (a row after rectification).
  • Depth error grows with Z²; longer baselines and higher resolution help.
  • Occlusions and textureless areas need smoothness priors, which networks now learn.
2.6

Motion & optical flow

The questionTwo consecutive video frames: a pedestrian has moved a little. For every pixel, how far and in which direction did the content move? That field of arrows is the optical flow, and it drives video stabilisation, action recognition and collision warnings.

Intuition

Assume that a small patch of the world keeps its brightness as it moves: a red sweater stays red from one frame to the next, it just shifts a few pixels. This is the brightness constancy assumption. If a pixel at (x,y) in frame 1 reappears at (x+u,y+v) in frame 2, then I(x+u,y+v,t+1)=I(x,y,t).

The trouble is that this is one equation for two unknowns (u and v). Look through a small round hole at a long straight edge sliding to the right-and-up: you can only see the part of the motion perpendicular to the edge, because sliding along the edge changes nothing you can see. This is the aperture problem, the same flat/edge/corner story of lesson 2.1. Corners and texture, where gradients point in several directions, fix it.

Build it up

Linearise. For small motion, expand the shifted image to first order: I(x+u,y+v,t+1)\approx I(x,y,t)+I_xu+I_yv+I_t, where I_t is the change at a fixed pixel between frames; with brightness constancy this means I_xu+I_yv+I_t=0, the optical-flow constraint

I_x\,u+I_y\,v+I_t=0,\qquad I_t=I(\cdot,t+1)-I(\cdot,t)

One equation per pixel. Lucas and Kanade (1981) add the assumption that all pixels in a small window move together. A 5×5 window gives 25 equations for 2 unknowns, solved by least squares: minimise \sum_W(I_xu+I_yv+I_t)^2. Setting the derivatives to zero gives the 2×2 normal equations

\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\sum I_x^2&\sum I_xI_y\\ \sum I_xI_y&\sum I_y^2\end{pmatrix}}_{M}\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}\sum I_xI_t\\ \sum I_yI_t\end{pmatrix}

The matrix M is the same structure tensor as Harris! It is solvable exactly when M is invertible, i.e. when the window is a corner or texture (\lambda_{\min} large). On an edge M is nearly singular (aperture problem); on a flat patch it is almost zero. That is why "good features to track" are Harris corners.

Worked example: a Lucas-Kanade solve

A tiny window with three pixels, with measured (I_x,I_y,I_t):

pixelIxIyIt
A11−3
B10−1
C01−2

Sums: \sum I_x^2=1+1+0=2, \sum I_xI_y=1+0+0=1, \sum I_y^2=1+0+1=2, \sum I_xI_t=-3-1+0=-4, \sum I_yI_t=-3+0-2=-5.

Solve \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\5\end{pmatrix}. \det M=4-1=3. u=\frac{2\cdot4-1\cdot5}{3}=1, v=\frac{2\cdot5-1\cdot4}{3}=2.

Check each pixel: I_xu+I_yv=1+2=3=-I_t for A; 1=-(-1) for B; 2=-(-2) for C. The flow is (u, v) = (1, 2) pixels per frame. The eigenvalues of M are 3 and 1: well conditioned. If all three pixels had I_x=I_y (a diagonal edge), M would be singular and only u+v could be found.

Beyond small motions: dense flow

Linearisation holds only for shifts of about a pixel or less. Fixes: (1) image pyramids: estimate motion on a blurred, quarter-size image where a 8-pixel motion is 2 pixels, then refine at each finer level (coarse to fine); (2) iterate: warp frame 2 back by the current estimate and solve again. Dense flow gives an arrow at every pixel. Horn-Schunck (1981) adds a smoothness penalty over the whole image so textureless areas inherit motion from neighbours; Farnebäck fits polynomials. Deep networks (FlowNet, PWC-Net, RAFT) learn the whole thing: RAFT builds a 4-D correlation volume of all pixel pairs and iteratively updates the flow with a recurrent unit.

The formal definition

\min_{u,v}\ \sum_{(x,y)\in W}\big(I_x u+I_y v+I_t\big)^2\ \Longrightarrow\ \begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}=-M^{-1}\mathbf b,\quad \mathbf b=\begin{pmatrix}\sum I_xI_t\\ \sum I_yI_t\end{pmatrix}
(u,v)flow vector: pixels moved horizontally and vertically between frames
I_x,I_yspatial gradients of frame 1
I_ttemporal difference: frame 2 minus frame 1
Wwindow (e.g. 5×5) assumed to move rigidly together
Mstructure tensor (same as Harris); invertible only for corners/texture
\mathbf bright-hand side from the temporal change
Try this
  1. Watch the object slide between frame t and frame t+1 (loop). Then look at the flow field: green arrows are full Lucas-Kanade solutions; orange arrows are only the motion perpendicular to an edge.
  2. Tick peek through an aperture and drag the circle onto a straight edge: you cannot tell which way the object really moves. Drag it to a corner and the ambiguity disappears.
  3. Untick texture inside: the interior becomes flat and has no arrows; only edges and corners carry information.
  4. Raise the true motion above 2.5 px: the linearisation fails and the estimate shrinks. This is why pyramids exist.
Common traps
  • Brightness constancy fails with lighting changes, shadows, specular highlights and auto-exposure; flow gets noisy.
  • Flow is the motion of apparent brightness patterns, not necessarily the real 3-D motion (a rotating barber pole shows flow up while the pole turns sideways).
  • Large motions need pyramids; fast cameras or low frame rates break single-scale methods.
  • Flow is undefined at occlusions: pixels that appear or disappear have no partner.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Video models: two-stream action recognition feeds optical flow into a second CNN; optical flow supervises or warps features in video object segmentation, frame interpolation and video super-resolution.
  • Stabilisation and tracking: phone video stabilisers and KLT trackers use sparse Lucas-Kanade on corner points.
  • Autonomy: flow gives time-to-collision and ego-motion; drones and optical mouse sensors are flow sensors.
  • RAFT and its successors are standard building blocks in diffusion-based video generation and scene-flow models.

Check yourself

1. With ΣIx²=4, ΣIxIy=0, ΣIy²=1, ΣIxIt=−8, ΣIyIt=−3, what is (u, v)?
M is diagonal: u = 8/4 = 2, v = 3/1 = 3. The flow is (2, 3) px.
2. Why does Lucas-Kanade fail on a blank white wall and on a long straight edge?
Wall: gradients are zero, so M ≈ 0 (no information). Edge: all gradients are parallel, so M has one tiny eigenvalue and the motion along the edge is undetermined (aperture problem).
3. A car moves 12 px between frames. How does a pyramid help?
At 1/4 resolution the motion is 3 px, and at 1/8 it is 1.5 px, small enough for the linear approximation. Estimate there, upscale and refine at each finer level.

Takeaways

  • Brightness constancy gives I_x·u + I_y·v + I_t = 0: one equation, two unknowns.
  • Lucas-Kanade assumes a window moves together and solves a 2×2 system with the Harris structure tensor.
  • Edges give only normal flow (aperture problem); corners and texture give full flow.
  • Large motion: pyramids and iteration; deep nets (RAFT) learn dense flow end to end.
