Module 8 · Evaluation, accuracy & ground truth

A map is a claim about the ground. This module shows how to test that claim with a sample of points, how to turn the sample into an honest area estimate, how to score individual buildings or ships, how far the reference labels themselves can be trusted, where the errors cluster, and how to ask a map which of its pixels it is unsure about.

Basic classification metrics (precision, recall, F1, confusion matrices) are taught in Machine Learning · Evaluation; IoU and average precision for boxes in Computer Vision · Detection. This module adds the geography: sampling design, area weights, pixel footprints and maps of error.

8.1

Sample-based accuracy assessment

The questionA national land-cover product labels every 10 m pixel as Forest, Crop or Urban. The country has about 40 million pixels. Visiting them all is impossible, so how many points should you check, where should they be, and how do you turn a handful of checks into a number you can defend in a report?

Intuition

Think of the map as a very large exam. You cannot mark every answer, so you pick a few questions at random, look up the true answer (the reference, from a field visit or a high-resolution photograph), and count how often the map got it right. Each checked point is one question. The map's answer is the map label; the true answer is the reference label.

The tally of all these questions is called a confusion matrix (also an error matrix). Rows are what the map said, columns are what the reference says, and the diagonal holds the agreements.

Build it up

Step 1: one point. A point at 12.3 km east, 4.8 km north is inside a pixel the map calls Crop. The reference photograph shows a field of wheat. One agreement. Nothing more can be said from one point.

Step 2: many points. Sample 30 points. Put every pair (map label, reference label) into a table. The table is the whole story of the map's errors.

Worked example: 30 points, 3 classes

Rows are map classes, columns are reference classes. Each row was sampled separately (a stratified design: the strata are the map classes), so the row totals are the number of points taken in each stratum.

Map ↓ / Reference →ForestCropUrbanRow total n_{i\cdot}
Forest102113
Crop18110
Urban0167
Column total n_{\cdot j}1111830

Overall accuracy is the share of all points on the diagonal: (10+8+6)/30 = 24/30 = 0.800. The map is right on 80% of the sampled points.

User's accuracy asks the question a user of the map cares about: "If the map says Urban here, how often is it really Urban?" For each map class, divide its diagonal cell by its row total. Forest: 10/13 = 0.769. Crop: 8/10 = 0.800. Urban: 6/7 = 0.857.

Producer's accuracy asks the question the producer of the map cares about: "Of the places that really are Urban, how many did I catch?" For each reference class, divide its diagonal cell by its column total. Forest: 10/11 = 0.909. Crop: 8/11 = 0.727. Urban: 6/8 = 0.750.

Read together: a user who sees Urban on the map is right 86% of the time, but a real Urban pixel has only a 75% chance of being mapped as Urban. The overall 80% hides both facts.

Step 3: why stratify? Simple random sampling of 30 points from a map where Urban covers 5% of the area would give about 1.5 Urban points, which is useless for judging Urban. Stratified sampling takes a fixed number of points inside each map class, so every class gets enough checks. The price is that the sample no longer mirrors the map's area; the next lesson repairs that.

The formal definition

\text{OA}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{K} n_{ii}\qquad \text{UA}_i=\frac{n_{ii}}{n_{i\cdot}}\qquad \text{PA}_j=\frac{n_{jj}}{n_{\cdot j}}
Knumber of classes (here 3)
n_{ij}number of sample points whose map label is i and whose reference label is j
n_{i\cdot}=\sum_j n_{ij}row total: all points the map labelled i (the points taken in stratum i)
n_{\cdot j}=\sum_i n_{ij}column total: all points the reference says are j
Ntotal number of sample points, \sum_{i,j} n_{ij}
OAoverall accuracy: fraction of points where map and reference agree
UAiuser's accuracy of class i (reliability of the map's label)
PAjproducer's accuracy of class j (completeness: how much of the real j was caught)
How this relates to ML metrics. A confusion matrix of a classifier on a test set is the same object. UA is the precision of class i and PA is the recall of class j, computed per class. What is new in GeoAI is the sampling design: the points are chosen by a probability design, so the numbers can later be turned into area estimates.

Lab: sample the map point by point

Try this
  1. Press Play and watch the points arrive one by one. Each point is compared with the reference, and the matching cell of the matrix fills in.
  2. Switch from stratified to random and run to 30 points. Count how few points land on Urban in the random design. Stratified gives Urban a fair share.
  3. Raise the error rate of the product. Watch OA fall, and watch the off-diagonal cells grow. Which cell grows fastest?
  4. Press New map. The same error rate produces a different pattern of errors. Overall accuracy changes from map to map even though the rate is fixed: sampling noise is real.
Common traps
  • Overall accuracy alone is not a report. A map can reach 95% overall by being excellent on Forest and useless on a 3%-area class. Always report per-class UA and PA.
  • User's and producer's accuracy answer different questions. Users care about trust in a label (UA); producers and planners care about omission (PA). Mixing them up reverses the advice.
  • Sample rows are not map areas. In the worked table the rows total 13, 10 and 7, not 70%, 25% and 5%. Summing unweighted counts answers a question about the sample, not about the map. Area adjustment (8.2) is needed for area claims.
  • The reference is not free truth. A reference photograph from a different season can disagree with the map for reasons that have nothing to do with the map (8.4).
Pipeline position. This step sits in post-process and publish & monitor: the map is finished, and the sample of checked points is stored as a table next to it (points, map label, reference label, date of reference, interpreter), so that the accuracy can be recomputed later.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Validation reports of global and national land-cover products (for example ESA WorldCover and Dynamic World) use sample points checked against reference interpretations, reported as confusion matrices.
  • Every classifier evaluation in Machine Learning is the same table on a test set; GeoAI adds the spatial sampling design on top.

Check yourself

2. A user asks: "If I see Urban on this map, how often is it really Urban?" Which accuracy answers that, and what is its value?
The user's accuracy of Urban, UAUrban = 6/7 = 0.857. It is a row-wise quantity: it looks at what the map said.
3. Why does a stratified design make Urban accuracy estimable, and what does it cost?
Taking a fixed number of points inside each map class guarantees enough Urban points (7 here) to estimate its accuracy, whereas a random design of 30 points on a 5%-area class would give about 1.5 points on average. The cost is that the unweighted counts no longer reflect areas; the next lesson applies area weights to undo that.

Takeaways

  • A map is validated by checking sample points against a reference; the tally is a confusion matrix (rows = map, columns = reference).
  • Overall accuracy is the diagonal share; user's accuracy is a row ratio (reliability), producer's accuracy is a column ratio (completeness).
  • Stratify by map class so that small classes get enough checks, and remember the design when you later estimate areas.
8.2

Area-adjusted accuracy

The questionThe map says Urban covers 5% of the study area. The raw sample says Urban producer's accuracy is 75%. A ministry wants the area of urban growth and the accuracy of that area. Do the raw counts give the right answer, or do the 7 Urban sample points each represent the same amount of land?

Intuition

Not every sample point represents the same amount of land. In the stratified design of 8.1, the 13 Forest points were drawn from a stratum covering 70% of the map, and the 7 Urban points from a stratum covering 5%. A Forest point stands for many more pixels than an Urban point. If we count every point equally we give Urban far too much say in the overall accuracy, or too little, depending on the question.

The fix is a weight per stratum: how much of the map that stratum covers. A point from a stratum with a big weight counts for more.

Build it up

Step 1: area weights. Let A_i be the area of map class i. The weight is its share of the total: W_i=A_i/\sum_k A_k. Take areas of 700 km² Forest, 250 km² Crop and 50 km² Urban (total 1000 km²). Then W=(0.70,\,0.25,\,0.05).

Step 2: the estimated map area in each cell. A cell (i,j) is the set of pixels the map calls i and the reference calls j. Its estimated share of the whole area is the stratum weight times the fraction of that stratum's points that fell in the cell: \hat p_{ij}=W_i\,n_{ij}/n_{i\cdot}.

Worked example: area-weighted matrix

Using the matrix of 8.1 with n_{i\cdot}=13,10,7:

Map ↓ / Reference →ForestCropUrbanRow sum (= W_i)
Forest (W = 0.70)0.700·10/13 = 0.5380.700·2/13 = 0.1080.700·1/13 = 0.0540.700
Crop (W = 0.25)0.250·1/10 = 0.0250.250·8/10 = 0.2000.250·1/10 = 0.0250.250
Urban (W = 0.05)0.050·0/7 = 0.0000.050·1/7 = 0.0070.050·6/7 = 0.0430.050
Column sum0.5630.3150.1221.000

Step 3: area-adjusted overall accuracy is the sum of the diagonal: \widehat{\text{OA}}=0.538+0.200+0.043=0.781, compared with the raw 0.800. The estimate falls because area weighting moves weight away from Urban (UA 0.857, the highest) and towards Forest (UA 0.769, the lowest) once the true area shares are used.

Step 4: area-adjusted producer's accuracy is the diagonal divided by the column sum, \widehat{\text{PA}}_j=\hat p_{jj}/\sum_i \hat p_{ij}. Forest: 0.538/0.563 = 0.956. Crop: 0.200/0.315 = 0.635. Urban: 0.043/0.122 = 0.352.

Urban fell from 0.750 to 0.352. Look at the Urban column. Six correct Urban points carry a total weight of 0.043. The one point the Forest stratum called Urban carries weight 0.70/13=0.054 on its own, more than all six correct Urban points together. A rare class with few samples is very sensitive to a single mistake in a large stratum. This is why the area-adjusted estimate is the number to publish.

User's accuracy does not change. UAi = \hat p_{ii}/W_i = n_{ii}/n_{i\cdot}: the weights cancel within a row. UA answers "is this label reliable?" and that does not depend on how big the class is.

Step 5: how sure are we? Thirty points are a small sample, so the estimate has an uncertainty. For a stratified design the standard error of the overall accuracy is

\operatorname{SE}(\widehat{\text{OA}})=\sqrt{\sum_{i}W_i^{2}\,\frac{\text{UA}_i\,(1-\text{UA}_i)}{n_{i\cdot}-1}}
Worked example: standard error

Forest: 0.70^2\cdot\frac{0.769\cdot0.231}{12}=0.00725. Crop: 0.25^2\cdot\frac{0.800\cdot0.200}{9}=0.00111. Urban: 0.05^2\cdot\frac{0.857\cdot0.143}{6}=0.00005. The sum is 0.00841 and its square root is SE = 0.092. The Forest stratum contributes 86% of the variance.

An approximate 95% interval is 0.781\pm1.96\times0.092, that is 0.60 to 0.96. Honest reporting with 30 points says "between 60% and 96%". The sample must be much larger before the claim becomes useful, which is exactly the planning question a sampling design answers.

For comparison, the naive standard error of an unweighted proportion, \sqrt{0.8\cdot0.2/30}=0.073, ignores the stratification and the area weights, and is therefore the wrong formula for this design.

The formal definition

W_i=\frac{A_i}{\sum_k A_k}\qquad \hat p_{ij}=W_i\,\frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\cdot}}\qquad \widehat{\text{OA}}=\sum_i \hat p_{ii}\qquad \widehat{\text{PA}}_j=\frac{\hat p_{jj}}{\sum_i \hat p_{ij}}
Aimapped area of class i (any unit; only ratios matter)
Wiarea weight of stratum i, i.e. the proportion of the map it covers; the weights sum to 1
\hat p_{ij}estimated proportion of the whole area in cell (map i, reference j)
n_{ij}, n_{i\cdot}counts as in 8.1: points in a cell, and points in stratum i
\operatorname{SE}standard error: the typical size of the estimate's error from sampling alone

The estimator is valid when every stratum is sampled with a known, non-zero probability and the points are placed independently within each stratum (conditions of the stratified design). If the strata are wrong (for example, the map is updated after sampling), the weights are wrong too.

  2. Drag Urban down to 5 km². The raw numbers do not move at all, but the area-adjusted Urban producer's accuracy depends on it. Explain why in one sentence.

Common traps
  • Raw counts answer a sample question; area-weighted estimates answer a map question. Area statistics (hectares of urban growth) must use weights.
  • Weights come from the map, not from the sample. If you compute W from the sample rows (13/30, 10/30, 7/30) you have reproduced the sample design, not the map.
  • Small strata are fragile. One mistake in a small stratum that is mapped to a rare class can dominate that class's estimate (see the Urban column above).
  • Units must match. A table in hectares and a map in pixels must be converted to the same unit before the weights are formed.
Pipeline position. Post-process and publish: the stratum areas and the sample table are stored with the product so that an official area and its uncertainty can be recomputed when the map or the sample changes.
Where it shows up in AI
  • National land-change reporting, where the area of each class and of each class change must be estimated with a stated uncertainty and a stratified sample (the good-practice approach of Olofsson and colleagues, 2014).
  • Importance weighting in machine learning: reweighting a sample so that its class mix matches the target population, the same idea as using W_i instead of counts.

Check yourself

1. Why does the user's accuracy stay the same when you change the class areas?
Because UAi = nii/ni· only compares points inside one map stratum. The weight Wi multiplies every cell of row i and cancels when the row is divided by its own total. UA is a property of the map's labels, not of the land area each label covers.
2. With areas 700/250/50 the Urban producer's accuracy is 0.352, yet the raw value is 0.750. Which single point is the largest reason, and why?
The Forest-map point that the reference calls Urban (n13=1). Its weight is WForest/nForest· = 0.70/13 = 0.054, while each correct Urban point has weight 0.05/7 = 0.007. The one error in a large stratum outweighs six correct points in a small one.
3. Using SE = 0.092, the 95% interval is about 0.60 to 0.96. What would you tell a stakeholder, and what is the remedy?
The sample of 30 cannot tell apart a map that is 62% right from one that is 95% right. Report the interval, not only the point value, and enlarge the sample, particularly in the strata whose W_i^2\,UA(1-UA)/(n-1) terms dominate (here Forest).

Takeaways

  • Area weights W_i=A_i/\sum A turn sample counts into estimates of area.
  • Overall and producer's accuracies must be area-weighted; user's accuracy is unchanged by weights.
  • Always report a standard error or interval; with small samples it is wide, and the rare class is the most fragile.
8.3

Object-level metrics

The questionA model maps building footprints. Most of the area is background, so a model that says "no building" everywhere still gets 95% pixel accuracy when buildings cover 5% of the scene. What should we score: pixels, or buildings? And when is a predicted building "the same" as a reference building?

Intuition

Pixel accuracy counts the background as heavily as the buildings. Object metrics count things: each reference building should be matched with at most one predicted building, and each match should be judged by how much the two shapes overlap. The overlap score most used in GeoAI is the Intersection over Union: the shared area divided by the total area covered by either shape. Two identical shapes score 1; shapes that do not touch score 0.

A threshold then decides what counts as a match (commonly 0.5). Once matches are decided, the familiar counts of true positives, false positives and false negatives give precision, recall and F1 for the whole scene.

Build it up

Step 1: rasterise the shapes. Work on a grid of 10 m pixels in a projected CRS (for example UTM, so that pixel areas are equal and in m²). Each pixel is 100 m². The scene below is 10 × 8 pixels. Reference objects are A, B, C; predicted objects are 1, 2, 3.

.AAA......           .1111.....
..........           ..........
.....BB...           .....22...
.....BB.CC           .....22...
.....BB.CC           ......3333
........CC           ......3333

Step 2: count pixels. Intersection |P\cap R| is the number of pixels both shapes cover; union |P\cup R|=|P|+|R|-|P\cap R| is the number covered by either.

Check A vs 1 by hand: the intersection is the 2×3 block of A (6 px); prediction 1 has 8 px; union 6+8-6=8; IoU =6/8=0.750. Dice is 2\cdot6/(6+8)=0.857, and indeed \text{Dice}=\dfrac{2\,\text{IoU}}{1+\text{IoU}}=\dfrac{1.5}{1.75}=0.857.

Note prediction 3 also touches B (one pixel, IoU with B only 1/13 = 0.077). Matching is done per reference object, by the best IoU, so this pixel does not make 3 a match for B.

Step 3: match at a threshold. With threshold 0.5: A–1 (0.75) and B–2 (0.667) are matches; C–3 (0.400) is not. The result is TP = 2, FP = 1, FN = 1. Precision =2/(2+1)=0.667, recall =2/(2+1)=0.667, and F_1=\dfrac{2PR}{P+R}=0.667. Lower the threshold to 0.35 and C–3 becomes a match: TP = 3, F1 = 1.

Step 4: quality of matches. The panoptic-quality idea splits the score into how well matched objects fit (segmentation quality, the mean IoU of the TP pairs) and how many objects were found (recognition quality). Here \text{SQ}=(0.750+0.667)/2=0.708 and \text{RQ}=2/(2+\tfrac12+\tfrac12)=0.667, so \text{PQ}=\text{SQ}\times\text{RQ}=0.472.

Areas in square metres come directly from the pixel counts: A is 600 m², prediction 1 is 800 m². The same counts convert to any unit, but only inside one projected CRS; a geodesic area of a polygon can differ slightly from the sum of pixel areas because the pixels are not exactly equal on the ground in a geographic CRS.

The formal definition

\text{IoU}(P,R)=\frac{|P\cap R|}{|P\cup R|}\qquad \text{Dice}=\frac{2|P\cap R|}{|P|+|R|}=\frac{2\,\text{IoU}}{1+\text{IoU}}
\text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}\qquad \text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}\qquad F_1=\frac{2\,\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}
\text{PQ}=\underbrace{\frac{\sum_{(P,R)\in TP}\text{IoU}(P,R)}{|TP|}}_{\text{SQ}}\times\underbrace{\frac{|TP|}{|TP|+\tfrac12|FP|+\tfrac12|FN|}}_{\text{RQ}}
P, Ra predicted and a reference object, each as a set of pixels
|·|number of pixels (or area) in a set
∩, ∪intersection (in both) and union (in either)
TP, FP, FNmatched pairs with IoU ≥ threshold; unmatched predictions; unmatched reference objects
F1harmonic mean of precision and recall, between 0 and 1
SQ, RQ, PQsegmentation quality, recognition quality and their product (panoptic quality)

Matching is a one-to-one assignment: each reference object can be matched at most once. Greedy matching (take the highest-IoU pair first, remove both, repeat) is what most evaluation scripts do.

Common traps
  • The threshold is a choice. 0.5 is a convention, not a law. Report it with the score, and compare methods only at the same threshold.
  • Small objects are unforgiving. One pixel of boundary error changes the IoU of a 6-pixel object far more than of a 600-pixel one. Object metrics for small ships or cars need smaller thresholds or a boundary tolerance.
  • Split and merge. One reference building predicted as two halves gives one FN (or one TP with a poor IoU) and an extra FP. Check the counts, not only the score.
  • Pixel F1 is not object F1. The Dice of a pixel mask and the F1 of matched objects answer different questions; say which one you report.
Pipeline position. Post-process (polygons are vectorised from the mask) and evaluate. The matched pairs and their IoU should be stored per object so that a failure can be traced to a single building.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Building-footprint challenges (for example SpaceNet) score predicted polygons by matching them to reference polygons at an IoU threshold and reporting F1.
  • Detection and instance segmentation in Computer Vision · Detection use the same IoU; COCO-style average precision sweeps the threshold, and panoptic quality is used for combined stuff-and-thing maps.

Check yourself

1. Compute the IoU of prediction 2 with reference B. Give the counts.
|B| = 6 px, |2| = 4 px, both cover the 2×2 block, so |B∩2| = 4. |B∪2| = 6+4−4 = 6. IoU = 4/6 = 0.667 (area: 400 m² shared, 600 m² union).
2. At threshold 0.5, the scene has TP = 2, FP = 1, FN = 1. What are precision, recall and F1, and what happens to F1 if the threshold drops to 0.35?
Precision = recall = 2/3 = 0.667, so F1 = 0.667. At threshold 0.35 the pair C–3 (IoU 0.40) becomes a match: TP = 3, FP = 0, FN = 0, and F1 = 1.
3. Why is the mean pixel accuracy a poor score for buildings covering 5% of a scene?
A model that predicts background everywhere gets 95% pixel accuracy and finds no building. Object metrics (TP/FP/FN with IoU matching) and pixel F1 on the building class both give 0 for that model, so they expose the failure.

Takeaways

  • IoU = shared area / total area covered; Dice = 2·IoU/(1+IoU).
  • Match objects one-to-one at a stated IoU threshold, then count TP, FP and FN, and report precision, recall and F1.
  • Pixel counts give exact areas in a projected CRS; small objects are very sensitive to one-pixel boundary errors.
8.4

Collecting ground truth

The questionTwo careful interpreters look at the same 20 points in a high-resolution photograph and disagree on 4 of them. Reference labels are supposed to be the truth against which the map is judged. If the reference itself is wrong one time in five, what does "the map is 80% accurate" mean, and how much of the map's apparent error is really the reference's?

Intuition

Every reference label is a measurement with an error. A field survey has GPS offsets of a few metres, which at 10 m pixels can put you in the neighbouring class. A photograph interpreted in 2026 can be compared with a map from 2024 in which the crop has since been harvested. A crowd of volunteers may be excellent on roads and poor on wetlands. The reference is a map too, and it has its own uncertainty.

SourceTypical errorWhat to record
Field survey (GNSS)position offsets of metres; access limits (sample near roads)receiver accuracy, date, positions of the sample
Very-high-resolution photo interpretationdate mismatch; shadows; mixed pixels at edgesimage date, scale, interpreter, confidence
Crowdsourced labelsuneven skill; systematic confusionsnumber of votes, agreement, quality checks
Existing maps used as referenceinherit the old map's errorssource map, its own accuracy

Build it up

Step 1: measure agreement between labellers. Raw agreement can be high by chance: if most of the scene is forest, two labellers who guess will often agree. The statistic Cohen's kappa subtracts the chance agreement.

Worked example: two interpreters, 20 points
A ↓ / B →ForestNon-forestTotal
Forest10212
Non-forest268
Total12820

Observed agreement: p_o=(10+6)/20=0.800.

Chance agreement, from each labeller's own class shares: p_e=\tfrac{12}{20}\cdot\tfrac{12}{20}+\tfrac{8}{20}\cdot\tfrac{8}{20}=0.36+0.16=0.520.

Kappa: \kappa=\dfrac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e}=\dfrac{0.800-0.520}{0.480}=0.583. The interpreters agree 80% of the time, but once chance is removed the agreement is "moderate" (0.41–0.60 on the Landis and Koch scale). A reference with that agreement cannot be treated as perfect.

Step 2: what label noise does to a measured accuracy. Let the model be correct on a fraction a of points against the true labels. Let the reference be wrong (flipped, in a binary problem) on a fraction \eta of points. A point is counted as wrong when the model and the reference disagree about it. Measured accuracy is therefore 1-\dfrac{|W\,\triangle\,F|}{N}, where W is the set of points the model gets wrong, F the set the reference gets flipped, and \triangle is the symmetric difference (points in exactly one of the two sets).

Worked example: ten points

The model is right on 9 of 10 points against the truth, so a=0.9. Its single error is point 4 (W = {4}). The reference is flipped at two points, both where the model is right: F = {2, 7}. Then W\triangle F=\{4,2,7\}, which has 3 points, and measured accuracy is 1-3/10=0.70. The model is still right on 9 of 10 points against the truth, yet it looks like 70%.

If the flips fall independently with rate \eta=0.2, the expected measured accuracy is (1-\eta)\,a+\eta\,(1-a)=0.8\cdot0.9+0.2\cdot0.1=0.74. Two models with true accuracies 0.90 and 0.85 would measure 0.74 and 0.71: the gap shrinks from 0.05 to 0.03, a factor 1-2\eta=0.6. Noise makes good models look worse and compresses the differences between them.

The formal definition

\kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e},\qquad p_e=\sum_k \pi_{k}^{(A)}\,\pi_{k}^{(B)}\qquad\qquad \mathbb E[\text{measured accuracy}]=(1-\eta)\,a+\eta\,(1-a)\quad\text{(binary, independent flips)}
poobserved agreement: share of points labelled identically
peagreement expected by chance from each labeller's class shares \pi_k
κkappa: 1 = perfect agreement, 0 = chance level
atrue accuracy of the model against the unobservable truth
ηreference flip rate (label noise)
W, F, △set of model errors, set of flipped labels, symmetric difference (in exactly one set)

The expectation assumes that flips are independent of the model's mistakes. If the reference is systematically wrong where the model is also wrong (both fooled by the same shadow), the effect is different and no formula of this kind holds.

Common traps
  • "Ground truth" is a claim. Record who labelled what, when, and how sure they were. A map validated against a noisy reference is validated against that noise.
  • Kappa is not accuracy. A kappa of 0.58 with 80% agreement is a statement about labeller consistency, not about the map.
  • Sampling only easy places. Points near roads are easy to visit and biased towards the simpler classes. Random or stratified sampling must include the hard places.
  • Temporal mismatch. A 2024 map checked against 2026 imagery counts land-cover change as error. Match the reference date to the map date.
Pipeline position. Acquire (the reference) and evaluate. Each reference point should carry its source, date, positional accuracy and labeller ID, so that the noise can be estimated and reported with the score.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Crowdsourced and volunteer labelling (OpenStreetMap edits, citizen-science surveys) produces label noise that must be measured before models are trained or evaluated on it.
  • Machine learning in general: noisy labels bias training and evaluation alike; see the label-quality discussion in Machine Learning.

Check yourself

1. Two interpreters agree on 16 of 20 points and each labels 12 as forest. What is kappa, and why is it lower than 0.80?
Chance agreement pe = (12/20)² + (8/20)² = 0.52, so κ = (0.80 − 0.52)/(1 − 0.52) = 0.583. It is lower because the labellers' class shares already make some agreement likely by chance; kappa removes that part.
2. A model is 90% accurate against the truth. Its reference is flipped at 20% of points independently of the model. What does the reference-based measurement report on average?
(1 − 0.2)·0.9 + 0.2·0.1 = 0.74. The model looks 16 points worse than it is.
3. Your model beats a baseline by 0.05 on a clean reference. Roughly how big is the difference after 20% symmetric label noise?
About 0.6 × 0.05 = 0.03, because the expected measured difference is (1 − 2η) times the true difference. The noise shrinks the gap, so a small real improvement may become invisible.

Takeaways

  • Reference labels are measurements; record their source, date and agreement, and use kappa to measure labeller agreement beyond chance.
  • Label noise lowers measured accuracy and compresses differences between models; the true accuracy is higher than it looks.
  • Match the reference date to the map date, and sample the hard places as well as the easy ones.
8.5

Error analysis & visual checks

The questionOverall accuracy is 81%. Nineteen per cent of the pixels are wrong. Are those errors scattered like noise, which you can accept, or clustered in one corner of the scene, which points to a cause you can fix: cloud shadows, a sensor stripe, a wetland that is mapped as water in the wet season?

Intuition

Scores summarise; maps explain. An error map paints each pixel by what happened to it: correct, or wrong in a particular way. A pattern in the picture is a hypothesis about the cause. Random speckle suggests noise. A block suggests a region with a problem. A thin line along boundaries suggests mixed pixels, where one 10 m pixel straddles two fields.

Two error types matter for a single class. Commission (false positive for the class): the map says Crop, the reference says Forest. Omission (false negative for the class): the reference says Crop, the map says something else. They are the two ways a class can go wrong, and they have different remedies.

Build it up

Step 1: a tiny map pair. Here is a 4 × 4 reference (left) and a product (right). F = Forest, C = Crop, U = Urban.

Worked example: a 4 × 4 error map

Overall accuracy: 13 of 16 correct, 13/16=0.8125. Three errors: (row 1, col 1) reference F, map C; (row 2, col 2) reference C, map F; (row 3, col 3) reference C, map U.

Step 2: where are the errors? Split the scene into four quadrants of 2 × 2 pixels. Accuracy: north-west 3/4 = 0.75 (one error); north-east 4/4 = 1.00; south-west 4/4 = 1.00; south-east 2/4 = 0.50 (two errors). The errors are not spread evenly: half of the south-east quadrant is wrong.

Step 3: are the errors clustered? The three error pixels touch each other only diagonally: (1,1) touches (2,2), which touches (3,3). With 4-neighbour connectivity (sharing an edge) they are three separate errors; with 8-neighbour connectivity (sharing a corner too) they form one cluster. Which connectivity you use changes the story, so state it. A 3-pixel diagonal chain along a field boundary is the signature of a mixed-pixel effect, not of a random failure.

Step 4: the usual checks. Stratify the error by everything that might matter: region (tile, district), season or acquisition date, sensor or sensor geometry (view angle), terrain (slope and aspect), and distance to the nearest reference point. The question is always the same: does the accuracy change with the factor, and is the change large enough to matter?

The formal definition

\text{Omission}_j=n_{\cdot j}-n_{jj}\qquad \text{Commission}_i=n_{i\cdot}-n_{ii}\qquad \text{acc}(g)=\frac{\sum_{x\in g}\mathbf 1[\hat y(x)=y(x)]}{|g|}

\hat y(x), y(x)the map label and the reference label at pixel x
\mathbf 1[\cdot]indicator: 1 if the statement is true, 0 otherwise

Report the accuracy of each group with its count. A group with 4 pixels can show 50% by chance alone; large groups are needed before a difference is believed.

Lab: scan the map for errors

Common traps
  • Small groups fool the eye. A region with 9 pixels and 4 errors looks dreadful; with 9,000 pixels it may be typical. Always show the count.
  • Looking only at errors. Compare with the correct pixels too; a class that is always correct gives no errors to see.
  • Confusing the error type. Omission and commission lead to different fixes (lower the threshold versus raise it). Never use a single "error" colour without the type.
  • Boundaries are where most errors live. A thin band of errors along field edges is mixed pixels; it may be acceptable for the use case, but it must be stated.
Pipeline position. Inference and post-process feed the error maps; they are stored as rasters (a COG with an error code per pixel) so that a reviewer can open them next to the imagery.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Debugging segmentation models: a per-region mIoU table and an error overlay on the tile reveal failure modes such as cloud shadows read as water or snow read as cloud.
  • Fairness and robustness checks in any model: error rates by group are the same idea as error rates by region or sensor.

Check yourself

1. In the 4 × 4 example, what are Crop's omission and commission counts, and what is its producer's accuracy?
Omission: reference Crop but map not Crop: 2 (positions (2,2) and (3,3)). Commission: map Crop but reference not Crop: 1 (position (1,1)). Producer's accuracy = 5/7 = 0.71 (Crop was correct on 5 of its 7 reference pixels, the table rounds to 0.71).
2. The south-east quadrant has accuracy 0.50, the rest 0.75–1.00. Is that evidence of a regional problem?
Not yet. With 4 pixels per quadrant, two errors is within what chance can produce. The pattern is a reason to look and to collect more samples in that quadrant, not a conclusion.
3. Why state the connectivity (4- or 8-neighbour) when you say errors are clustered?
Diagonal neighbours can connect errors into one cluster under 8-connectivity but not under 4-connectivity. The count of clusters, and hence the apparent clustering, depends on this choice; the same map can look scattered or clustered.

Takeaways

  • An error map turns a single accuracy into a picture; clustered errors point to causes you can name.
  • Separate omission (missed the class) from commission (false alarm), per class, with counts.
  • Stratify accuracy by region, season, sensor and terrain, and always show the number of pixels behind each figure.
8.6

Uncertainty & confidence in maps

The questionA pixel is labelled Crop. Two pixels can both get that label with very different confidence: one with probabilities 0.98 / 0.01 / 0.01, another with 0.40 / 0.35 / 0.25. A field team can visit only 200 pixels this season. Which ones should they visit, and how big is the area you would be asking them to check?

Intuition

A deep-learning segmentation model outputs, for every pixel, a probability for each class (usually with a softmax). The map keeps only the most probable class (the argmax), but the probabilities carry more information. A pixel whose probability mass sits on one class is confident. A pixel whose mass is spread across classes is uncertain, even when its argmax is the same.

The standard measure of spread is entropy: high when the distribution is flat, low when it is peaked. An uncertainty map is simply an entropy map, and a flagging rule is a threshold on it.

Build it up

Step 1: entropy of a distribution. For probabilities p_1,\dots,p_K that sum to 1, the entropy in bits is H=-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k (with 0\log 0=0). The logarithm base sets the unit: base 2 gives bits.

Worked example: three pixels, three classes
PixelProbabilities (Forest, Crop, Urban)ArgmaxH (bits)H / log₂3
a0.98, 0.01, 0.01Forest0.1610.10
b0.70, 0.20, 0.10Forest1.1570.73
c0.333, 0.333, 0.333tie1.5851.00

Pixel b: H=-(0.7\log_2 0.7+0.2\log_2 0.2+0.1\log_2 0.1)=0.360+0.464+0.332=1.157 bits. Pixel a is close to zero because one outcome is almost certain. Pixel c reaches the maximum possible for three classes, \log_2 3=1.585 bits: a flat distribution is the most uncertain. For two equally likely classes the entropy is exactly 1 bit.

Step 2: flag the uncertain pixels. Take a threshold \tau=1.0 bit. Pixels b and c are flagged for review (1.157 and 1.585 > 1.0); a is not. Pixel b is the interesting case: its argmax is Forest with 70%, yet it goes to the review queue because a 30% chance of something else is not small enough for this use.

Step 3: count the flagged area. Suppose a 10 × 10 pixel tile (10 m pixels, 100 m² each) has 6 flagged pixels. Flagged area = 6 × 100 m² = 600 m², which is 0.0006 km², or 6% of the 100-pixel tile. Team workload follows from the count of flagged areas, not from the number of uncertain-looking colours on screen.

Step 4: calibration. Entropy is only useful if the probabilities mean what they say. A map is calibrated when, among pixels that are given probability about 0.7 for their class, about 70% are really that class. Deep networks are often over-confident, so their raw probabilities need checking against a reference sample (a reliability diagram groups pixels by stated confidence and compares with the observed fraction correct). Calibration is a property of the model and its validation sample, not of the entropy formula.

The formal definition

H(\mathbf p)=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_k\log_2 p_k\in\left[0,\ \log_2 K\right]\qquad\qquad \text{flag}(x)=\mathbf 1\!\left[H(\mathbf p(x))>\tau\right]\qquad \text{Area}=\#\{x:\text{flag}(x)\}\cdot s^2
\mathbf p(x)the vector of class probabilities at pixel x (sums to 1)
Knumber of classes
Hentropy in bits; 0 for a certain pixel, \log_2 K for a flat one
τthreshold chosen for the use case (set on a validation sample, not on the test sample)
spixel size in metres (10 for Sentinel-2 bands in this example); s^2 is the pixel area

Using natural logarithms gives the entropy in nats; dividing by \ln K gives a value between 0 and 1 that is comparable across models with the same number of classes.

Lab: from probability maps to a review queue

Try this
  1. Press Play. A cursor moves over the map and reads the three probabilities and the entropy of the pixel under it. Check one reading against the formula by hand.
  2. Lower sharpness (the model becomes less sure) and watch the entropy map brighten. The argmax map barely changes. Confidence and label are different things.
  3. Move the flag threshold. Note how the flagged area (in km²) grows and shrinks. Pick the threshold that flags about 10% of the map.
  4. Choose a class in the probability panel. Find a boundary between two classes and check that the entropy is high there.
Common traps
  • Probabilities are not automatically calibrated. A softmax of 0.99 can be wrong far more often than 1 time in 100. Check calibration on held-out labelled data.
  • Uncertainty is not error. Flagged pixels are where the model is unsure; some wrong pixels are confident, and some flagged pixels are right.
  • Tune on validation, report on test. A threshold chosen to maximise the score on the test sample is a leak.
  • Area needs the pixel size and CRS. Quote the flagged area with its units and projection; a pixel of 10 m and one of 0.0001 degrees are not the same thing.
Pipeline position. Inference outputs the probability stack; post-process computes entropy and the flag; publish & monitor stores both the argmax map and the uncertainty layer, with the threshold recorded in the product metadata.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Global land-cover products that publish per-class probabilities for each pixel, so that users can threshold them for their own needs.
  • Active learning and human review queues: the most uncertain pixels are labelled first, which makes the next training round more informative.

Check yourself

1. Compute the entropy in bits of (0.5, 0.5, 0) and of (0.5, 0.25, 0.25).
(0.5, 0.5, 0): −2·0.5·log₂0.5 = 1 bit. (0.5, 0.25, 0.25): 0.5·1 + 2·0.25·2 = 0.5 + 1 = 1.5 bits. The second is more uncertain, because the mass is spread over three outcomes instead of two.
2. A pixel has probabilities (0.70, 0.20, 0.10) and the team's flag threshold is 1.0 bit. Is it flagged, and why might the team want that?
Yes: H = 1.157 > 1.0. Although Forest is the most likely class (70%), a 30% chance of another class is more than the team accepts for this product, so the pixel is sent for review.
3. Why is a per-pixel probability of 0.8 not automatically "80% reliable"?
Only if the model is calibrated. Calibration means that among pixels given 0.8, about 80% are truly that class. Deep models are often over-confident, so the claim must be tested on a labelled sample (reliability diagram) before it is quoted.

Takeaways

  • Entropy H = −Σ p log₂ p measures how spread a pixel's class probabilities are; the maximum is log₂K.
  • A flagging threshold turns an uncertainty map into a review queue; quote its area with pixel size and CRS.
  • Uncertainty is useful only when probabilities are calibrated, and thresholds must be chosen on validation data.
