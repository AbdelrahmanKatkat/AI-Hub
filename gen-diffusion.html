Module 8 · Diffusion & flow matching

Destroying data with noise is easy, and so is knowing exactly how it was destroyed. A diffusion model learns to undo one small piece of that destruction, and generation is then a long walk from pure noise back to data. Flow matching reaches a similar goal with straight lines. This module sits in the generate stage of the pipeline (collect and tokenise, pretrain, align, adapt and compress, generate, evaluate and guard), with its training loop in the pretrain stage. It gives the general theory; the image-specific overview, with the U-Net and a pixel-level lab, is in Computer Vision, lesson 8.3.

How this module works. This is the most mathematical module of the topic, so every idea is first met on the smallest case where everything can be computed exactly: one number instead of an image, drawn from a two-bump distribution, p_{\text{data}}(x)=\tfrac12\mathcal N(x;-1,s^2)+\tfrac12\mathcal N(x;+1,s^2) with a default width s=0.3. For this distribution the best possible denoiser, the score and the flow-matching velocity all have closed forms, so the arrows and paths in the labs are exact, not hand-drawn, and no network has to be trained. A real model replaces these exact formulas by a trained network that only approximates them. All labs are illustrative simulations with seeded random numbers, so a run can be repeated, and none of them says anything about the speed or quality of any particular commercial system.
Two conventions to keep apart. In lessons 8.1 to 8.7 (diffusion) x_0 is the data, x_T is noise and the formula is x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon. In lesson 8.8 (flow matching) the roles flip: x_0 is the noise, x_1 is the data and x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\,x_1. Both conventions are common in the literature, so it is worth noticing which one a paper uses.
8.1

The forward noising process

The questionTo teach a model to generate data, we first need a way to turn data into noise in a precise, controlled manner, so that the model can later learn to run it backwards. If the noising takes 1 000 small steps, do we really have to simulate all 1 000 to make one training example, or is there a shortcut that jumps straight to step 500?

Intuition: shake the sand picture a little at a time

Imagine a drawing made of sand on a tray. Shake the tray gently: the drawing blurs a little. Shake it again and again: eventually nothing is left but a flat, random-looking layer of sand. Diffusion models use exactly this idea on numbers. At every step the data are shrunk slightly toward zero and a small amount of random noise is added. After enough steps the original data are gone and only noise remains, whatever the data looked like. The path from data to noise is cheap and needs no learning. The hard part, the part a network learns, is the way back.

The shortcut exists because of a pleasant property of Gaussian noise: adding independent normal noise several times gives the same result as adding one larger dose of normal noise. So 500 small shake steps are equivalent to a single big one, with a formula we can write down.

Build it up

One step. Pick a tiny number \beta_t between 0 and 1 for each step t=1,2,\dots,T. Write \alpha_t=1-\beta_t. The forward step is

x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,z_t,\qquad z_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\ \text{independent for every }t

The weights satisfy (\sqrt{\alpha_t})^2+(\sqrt{\beta_t})^2=1, so the step shrinks the signal and adds noise in a balanced way, and the total variance does not blow up. Define the running product \bar\alpha_t=\alpha_1\alpha_2\cdots\alpha_t ("alpha-bar"), the fraction of the signal power still left after t steps.

Worked example: two steps by hand

Take \alpha_1=0.9,\ \alpha_2=0.8 (so \beta_1=0.1,\ \beta_2=0.2), one data value x_0=1, and noise draws z_1=0.5,\ z_2=-1.

Step by step. x_1=\sqrt{0.9}\cdot1+\sqrt{0.1}\cdot0.5=0.94868+0.15811=1.1068. Then x_2=\sqrt{0.8}\cdot1.1068+\sqrt{0.2}\cdot(-1)=0.89443\cdot1.1068-0.44721=0.5427.

Unrolled. Substituting x_1 into the second line gives x_2=\sqrt{\alpha_1\alpha_2}\,x_0+\sqrt{\alpha_2\beta_1}\,z_1+\sqrt{\beta_2}\,z_2=0.84853\,x_0+0.28284\,z_1+0.44721\,z_2. The two noise coefficients combine in variance: 0.28284^2+0.44721^2=0.08+0.20=0.28, which is exactly 1-\bar\alpha_2=1-0.72.

Closed form. So x_2=\sqrt{0.72}\,x_0+\sqrt{0.28}\,\epsilon where \epsilon is one standard normal variable. For these particular draws \epsilon=(0.28284\cdot0.5+0.44721\cdot(-1))/\sqrt{0.28}=-0.57789, and 0.84853\cdot1+0.52915\cdot(-0.57789)=0.5427, the same number as the two-step computation.

This is the general rule, proved by induction: if x_{t-1}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\epsilon', then one more step adds noise of variance \alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})+\beta_t=\alpha_t-\bar\alpha_t+1-\alpha_t=1-\bar\alpha_t.

x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad q(x_t\mid x_0)=\mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\big)
Symbols. x_0: a clean data value (in this module: a number; for an image: all its pixels, with the same formula applied to each). x_t: the noisy version after t steps. \beta_t\in(0,1): the noise amount at step t, chosen by us (the schedule). \alpha_t=1-\beta_t. \bar\alpha_t=\prod_{i\le t}\alpha_i: how much signal power survives, from 1 at t=0 down toward 0. \epsilon: a single standard normal draw that stands for all the noise added so far. \mathcal N(m,v): a normal distribution with mean m and variance v. The signal-to-noise ratio is \mathrm{SNR}(t)=\bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t): signal power over noise power.

Schedules. The numbers \beta_t are a design choice. A common original choice is a linear schedule with T=1000, \beta rising from 10^{-4} to 0.02. A cosine schedule defines \bar\alpha_t\propto\cos^2\!\big(\tfrac{t/T+0.008}{1.008}\cdot\tfrac\pi2\big) directly, and destroys the signal more slowly at the start. Computed values:

The two-bump data. If the data are the mixture above, each bump stays a bump and just moves and widens: the marginal of x_t is p_t(x)=\sum_k\tfrac12\mathcal N\big(x;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,m_k,\ \bar\alpha_t s^2+1-\bar\alpha_t\big) with centres m_k=\mp1. The centres shrink toward 0 like \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} while the bumps widen from s toward 1 until they overlap into one bell.

The differences are within the sampling error of 200 000 runs. The lab below repeats this comparison live with 1 000 points.

Common traps
  • "T is a physical time." It is just the number of steps we chose. What matters is the profile of \bar\alpha_t, which says how much signal is left at each noise level.
  • The closed form is not an approximation. It is exact for Gaussian noise (the check above), which is why training never simulates the chain.
  • A schedule that stops too early. If \bar\alpha_T is not close to 0, some signal remains in x_T, while sampling starts from pure noise. That mismatch is a known failure mode of badly chosen schedules.
  • Mixing up conventions. Here x_0 is data and t grows toward noise. In flow matching (lesson 8.8) it is the other way round.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Every diffusion model is trained by taking a clean example, drawing a random t and a random \epsilon, and building x_t with the closed form. For an image the same formula is applied to every pixel (or every latent value, lesson 8.7), with independent noise per value.
  • The schedule decides how much of the model's effort goes to coarse structure (high noise) and to fine detail (low noise). Choosing and re-weighting schedules is an active design topic in image, audio and video generators.
  • The SNR is the natural "dial" that later lessons use: samplers move from low SNR to high SNR.

Takeaways

  • Each forward step is x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}z_t; the whole chain collapses to x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon (verified step by step: 0.5427 both ways).
  • The schedule \bar\alpha_t sets how fast the signal is destroyed; SNR = \bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t) measures what is left.
  • For the two-bump toy, p_t is again a two-component mixture with centres \pm\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} and variance \bar\alpha_t s^2+1-\bar\alpha_t, which is what makes the exact labs possible.
8.2

Learning to predict the noise

The questionWe can make a noisy x_t from any clean x_0. What should a network be trained to output when it sees x_t and the noise level t, and what loss should it minimise? And since several different clean values could have produced the same x_t, what is the best possible answer?

Intuition: the answer key is something we put in ourselves

Training data for the denoiser come for free: take a clean example, add noise \epsilon that we draw, and keep a note of it. Now we have a question (the noisy value) and its answer key (the noise we added). A network is trained to reproduce the answer key. That is plain regression, with no game and no hidden target.

There is a catch that makes the problem interesting. The network sees only x_t, but the same x_t could have arisen from different (x_0,\epsilon) pairs: a value near 0 might be the left bump plus a positive push or the right bump plus a negative push. The network cannot know which, so the best it can do is to output the average noise over all pairs that fit. Learning that average, for every x_t and every noise level, is the entire task.

Build it up

Predicting the noise is equivalent to predicting the clean data, because the formula x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon can be solved either way: \hat x_0=(x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\epsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}. Many systems use the noise target; others predict x_0 or a related combination, and each is a rearrangement of the same information.

Worked example: one training step, with the exact answer for two-point data

Let the data be the two numbers -1 and +1, equally likely (the case s\to0 of our toy). Take \bar\alpha=0.5, so \sqrt{\bar\alpha}=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}=0.7071. Draw x_0=+1 and \epsilon=-0.8:

x_t=0.7071\cdot1+0.7071\cdot(-0.8)=0.7071-0.5657=0.1414. The training question is "here is 0.1414 at this noise level, what noise was added?" and the answer key says -0.8.

Best possible answer. By Bayes' rule the probability that this x_t came from the +1 data point is \sigma\big(2\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,x_t/(1-\bar\alpha)\big)=\sigma(0.4)=0.599, so the expected clean value is \mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]=\tanh\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,x_t/(1-\bar\alpha)\big)=\tanh(0.2)=0.1974. The corresponding expected noise is \epsilon^*=(x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,0.1974)/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}=(0.1414-0.1396)/0.7071=0.0026. A value near 0 carries almost no clue about which bump it came from, so the best guess for the noise is about 0, not -0.8.

Loss at the best answer. (-0.8-0.0026)^2=0.644. Even a perfect model pays this on this particular sample: the loss cannot reach zero, only its average over all samples is minimised.

One gradient step of a toy model. Let the model be \epsilon_\theta(x)=a\,x with a=0.3. It predicts 0.3\cdot0.1414=0.0424, loss (-0.8-0.0424)^2=0.7097. The gradient is \partial L/\partial a=-2(\epsilon-ax)\,x=-2(-0.8424)(0.1414)=+0.2383. With learning rate 0.5: a\leftarrow0.3-0.5\cdot0.2383=0.1809; the new prediction is 0.0256 and the new loss 0.6816, lower than 0.7097.

Averaging over many samples and random noise levels gives the training objective, often called the simple loss:

L(\theta)=\mathbb E_{t,\;x_0,\;\epsilon}\Big[\big\lVert\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\ t\big)\big\rVert^2\Big],\qquad \epsilon^*(x,t)=\mathbb E\big[\epsilon\mid x_t=x\big]
Symbols. \theta: the network's parameters. \epsilon_\theta(x_t,t): the network's guess of the noise, given the noisy input and the step t. \mathbb E_{t,x_0,\epsilon}: the average over a random step t (typically uniform in 1..T), a random data point and fresh noise. \lVert\cdot\rVert^2: the sum of squares over all components (all pixels). \epsilon^*: the exact minimiser of the loss, the conditional mean of the noise given the noisy value. For a two-point or Gaussian-mixture data distribution it has a closed form, which is what the labs draw as the green curve.

The link to the score. The slope of the log-density of the noisy data is minus the expected noise, scaled:

\nabla_x\log p_t(x)=-\frac{\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t=x]}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \epsilon^*(x,t)=-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\nabla_x\log p_t(x)

Check with data x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1): then x_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1) for every t, the score is -x, and the best noise guess is the regression slope \mathrm{Cov}(\epsilon,x_t)/\mathrm{Var}(x_t)\cdot x=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x, which equals -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\cdot(-x). So a network that predicts the noise is secretly learning the score of the noised data. Lesson 8.5 uses this.

In the lab the model is not a neural network but a piecewise-linear curve with 41 knots, trained by stochastic gradient descent, which is enough to show the mechanics.

Common traps
  • "The loss should go to zero." It cannot, for the reason in the worked example; the floor depends on the noise level. Never compare loss values across different t as if they measured the same thing.
  • Not conditioning on t. The best noise guess for the same x_t is different at different noise levels. The network must receive t (typically as an embedding) or it averages over levels.
  • Confusing the target with the prediction. The target \epsilon is the noise actually drawn; the prediction is an average over everything that could have been drawn. They differ, and that is fine.
  • One noisy sample at a time. A single sample's loss may go up after an update; only the average over many samples falls.
Where it shows up in AI
  • This regression is the whole training loop of a diffusion model: sample a clean example, a step t and noise, form x_t, and take a gradient step on the squared error. Pretraining sees an enormous number of such steps, but each one is as simple as the worked example.
  • The network is conditioned on t and, for text-to-image models, on the prompt (lessons 8.6 and 8.7). Its backbone is a U-Net or a transformer.
  • The same pretraining-stage view as language models applies: a large dataset, a simple self-supervised loss (no human labels), and a compute budget (scaling laws gives the general accounting).

Check yourself

1. With ᾱ = 0.36, x₀ = 2 and ε = −0.5: compute x_t. If the network answers ε̂ = 0, what clean value x̂₀ does that imply? And if it answers ε̂ = −0.5?
√0.36 = 0.6, √0.64 = 0.8, x_t = 0.6·2 + 0.8·(−0.5) = 0.8. With ε̂ = 0: x̂₀ = 0.8/0.6 = 1.333. With ε̂ = −0.5: x̂₀ = (0.8 + 0.4)/0.6 = 2.0, the true value. A perfect noise guess gives a perfect clean guess.
2. The true noise was ε = −0.5 and the model said 0.2. What is the squared error for this sample?
(−0.5 − 0.2)² = 0.49.
3. Why can the average training loss not reach 0 even for a perfect network?
The network sees only x_t. Different pairs (x₀, ε) give the same x_t, so it can only output their average noise. The leftover variance of ε around that average is irreducible. It is large when the data stand out little from the noise at low noise levels (floor about 0.95 at step 20 in the lab) and nearly zero when the noise dominates (floor about 0 at step 950).

Takeaways

  • Training data are made by us: noise \epsilon is the answer key and x_t is the question. The loss is \mathbb E\lVert\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\rVert^2.
  • The best possible predictor is the conditional mean \epsilon^*=\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t], and it equals -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t} times the score of the noised data.
  • The loss has a floor (here 0.644 on the example sample, and about 0.51 on average at step 250) because one noisy value cannot reveal its own noise exactly.
8.3

Sampling with DDPM

The questionWe have a noise predictor. To generate, we start from pure noise and want to walk back to data. What exactly is one backward step, why does each step add fresh noise when the goal is to remove it, and what happens if we use far fewer than 1 000 steps?

Intuition: undo the shrink, then admit what you do not know

A forward step shrank the data by \sqrt{\alpha_t} and added a little noise. A backward step should undo the shrink and take out the noise. But the noise predictor only gives its best average guess: the true noise could differ. So the backward step moves the particle to where the guess says it should be, and then adds back a small random kick equal to the amount of uncertainty that remains. This keeps the cloud of particles as spread out as the real distribution at that noise level. If you removed the kick, every particle would drift toward the middle of the data and the variety would collapse.

Build it up

For a small forward step, the exact backward distribution is close to a normal distribution, p(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\approx\mathcal N(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\ \sigma_t^2). The DDPM (denoising diffusion probabilistic model) update, with \hat\epsilon=\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t), is

x_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\hat\epsilon\Big)+\sigma_t z,\qquad z\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\qquad \sigma_t^2=\tilde\beta_t=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t

Repeating it from t=T (with x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,1)) down to t=1 (where no noise is added) is ancestral sampling: each step is a draw conditioned on the previous one. (Another common choice is \sigma_t^2=\beta_t, which is slightly noisier.)

Worked example: one reverse step on a 4-step schedule

Use the tiny schedule \alpha=(0.9,\,0.8,\,0.7,\,0.6), so \bar\alpha=(0.9,\,0.72,\,0.504,\,0.3024) and \beta_4=0.4. Data: the two-point set \{-1,+1\}. A particle sits at x_4=0.8.

Denoiser. \sqrt{\bar\alpha_4}=0.54991, \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_4}=0.83522. The expected clean value is \hat x_0=\tanh\big(0.54991\cdot0.8/0.6976\big)=\tanh(0.6306)=0.5585, so the best noise guess is \hat\epsilon=(0.8-0.54991\cdot0.5585)/0.83522=0.5901.

Mean. \mu=\big(0.8-\tfrac{0.4}{0.83522}\cdot0.5901\big)/\sqrt{0.6}=(0.8-0.2826)/0.7746=0.6679.

Spread. \tilde\beta_4=\frac{1-0.504}{1-0.3024}\cdot0.4=0.2844, so \sigma_4=0.5333.

Result. With a draw z=0.5: x_3=0.6679+0.5333\cdot0.5=0.9346. A different z would give a different x_3: one particle, many possible next positions.

Same step in the equivalent form that the next lesson uses: x_3=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_3}\,\hat x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_3-\sigma^2}\,\hat\epsilon+\sigma z=0.70993\cdot0.5585+\sqrt{0.2116}\cdot0.5901+0.5333\cdot0.5=0.3965+0.2715+0.2667=0.9346. (I also checked in code that the two forms agree to six digits.)

Symbols. \hat\epsilon: the noise predictor's output at (x_t,t); in the labs it is the exact conditional mean \epsilon^* from lesson 8.2. \hat x_0: the implied guess of the clean data. \mu_\theta: the mean of the backward step, the bracket above. \sigma_t: the standard deviation of the fresh noise; \tilde\beta_t is the variance of the exact posterior q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0) and does not depend on x_0. z: fresh standard normal noise, drawn at every step.

Number of steps. The Gaussian approximation of the backward step is good only when each step is small, which is why the original schedules use T=1000. To sample with S<T steps, pick S of the T noise levels and treat consecutive picks as one bigger step (the formulas keep the same shape with \bar\alpha_t\to\bar\alpha_s). Each step costs one network evaluation, so S is the cost of generating one sample.

Measured on the toy (exact denoiser, so all error comes from big steps; 2 000 particles, mean absolute gap to the data quantiles, the one-dimensional Wasserstein-1 distance): S=100: 0.025, S=50: 0.038, S=20: 0.053, S=10: 0.132, S=5: 0.303, S=3: 0.543, S=2: 0.768. Values below about 0.05 are within finite-sample noise.

Removing the noise breaks it. With S = 40 and 600 particles, a sampler that keeps the DDPM mean but drops the random z ends with a standard deviation of 0.357, against 1.044 for the data. The correct sampler gives 1.025.

Common traps
  • "Sampling removes noise; so adding noise is a bug." The added z is what keeps the distribution correct. A "denoise as much as possible" loop produces blurry averages, the same collapse you see in the lab.
  • Final step. At the last step (t=1) no noise is added, otherwise the output would carry leftover noise.
  • Few steps with the same recipe. Using only 10 of 1 000 levels does not mean "the same method, faster": the Gaussian step assumption breaks, and the error in the table is large. The next lesson shows how to use fewer steps properly.
  • Mixing up the two \sigma choices. \tilde\beta_t and \beta_t give slightly different samples; a trained model works with the one it was evaluated with.
Where it shows up in AI
  • DDPM and its descendants established that this simple regression plus a long ancestral chain generates high-quality images; the cost, hundreds of network evaluations per sample, motivated everything in lesson 8.4.
  • The same sampling loop appears for audio, video and molecules whenever a diffusion model is used; only the denoiser network changes.
  • The fresh noise at each step is where the diversity of a stochastic sampler comes from; deterministic variants take it only from the starting noise.

Check yourself

1. A step goes from ᾱ_t = 0.5 to ᾱ_{t−1} = 0.6. Find β_t and the DDPM standard deviation σ_t = √β̃_t.
α_t = ᾱ_t/ᾱ_{t−1} = 0.5/0.6 = 0.8333, so β_t = 0.1667. β̃_t = (1 − 0.6)/(1 − 0.5) · 0.1667 = 0.1333, so σ_t = 0.3651.
2. Why does the reverse step add noise when the goal is to remove it?
The predictor gives only the average of the possible noises. The true next state is uncertain, with spread σ_t. Adding z with that spread keeps the particles distributed like the true noisy data at the previous level. Without it the cloud shrinks: in the lab the spread falls from 1.04 to 0.36.
3. Sampling takes 1 000 steps and each network call takes 30 ms. How long is one sample, and what would 50 steps cost?
1 000 · 30 ms = 30 s, and 50 · 30 ms = 1.5 s, 20 times faster, but only if the sampler still works well with 50 steps; the plain DDPM sampler in the table is already at an error of 0.038 for S = 50 on the toy, a small but visible increase.

Takeaways

  • One DDPM step: move to the predicted mean \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}(x_t-\tfrac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\hat\epsilon) and add noise of standard deviation \sigma_t (worked: 0.6679 + 0.5333·z).
  • The added noise keeps the diversity; leaving it out collapses the samples.
  • The number of steps is the cost, and with this recipe too few steps give clearly wrong samples (error 0.025 at 100 steps, 0.768 at 2).
8.4

Fast samplers

The questionA thousand network calls per sample is slow. Is there a way to use 20 or 50 calls without retraining the network? What changes in the update, what is lost, and how can a model be taught to do the job in even fewer?

Intuition: a straight walk instead of a drunk walk

DDPM's path from noise to data is a random walk: at every step it is kicked by fresh noise, so the path zigzags and many small steps are needed to follow it. But a particle's final position depends on where it started and on all the kicks. There exists a different, smooth path from each starting noise to a data point that produces exactly the same distribution of results. A smooth path can be followed with a few long strides, the way a straight road needs fewer waypoints than a winding one. DDIM (denoising diffusion implicit models) follows this smooth path by using no fresh noise at all.

Build it up

One reverse update from noise level \bar\alpha_t to a less noisy level \bar\alpha_s (s<t, not necessarily adjacent) has three parts: first guess the clean data from the current noisy value, then re-noise that guess to the new level, reusing the same noise estimate.

\hat x_0=\frac{x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\epsilon}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}},\qquad x_s=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_s}\,\hat x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_s-\sigma^2}\,\hat\epsilon+\sigma z,\qquad \sigma=\eta\sqrt{\tfrac{1-\bar\alpha_s}{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\sqrt{1-\tfrac{\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_s}}
Symbols. \hat\epsilon=\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t) and \hat x_0 as before. \bar\alpha_s>\bar\alpha_t: the target level, s steps further toward clean data (adjacent in DDPM, a jump of several in a fast sampler). \eta\in[0,1]: how much of the DDPM noise to use. \eta=1 gives exactly the DDPM step of lesson 8.3 when s=t-1; \eta=0 gives \sigma=0, the deterministic DDIM step, with no z. The square root \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_s-\sigma^2} keeps the total noise variance at the right level.
Worked example: the same particle, two samplers

Same 4-step schedule and particle as lesson 8.3: x_4=0.8, \hat x_0=0.5585, \hat\epsilon=0.5901, \bar\alpha_4=0.3024.

DDIM to the next level (\bar\alpha_3=0.504, \sigma=0): x_3=\sqrt{0.504}\cdot0.5585+\sqrt{0.496}\cdot0.5901=0.70993\cdot0.5585+0.70427\cdot0.5901=0.3965+0.4156=0.8121. DDPM with z=0.5 gave 0.9346; DDPM with z=0 gives its mean 0.6679. DDIM lands somewhere else again, because it keeps the noise estimate \hat\epsilon at full strength instead of splitting the variance between \hat\epsilon and z.

DDIM skipping a level (jump straight to \bar\alpha_2=0.72, using the same \hat\epsilon): x_2=\sqrt{0.72}\cdot0.5585+\sqrt{0.28}\cdot0.5901=0.84853\cdot0.5585+0.52915\cdot0.5901=0.4739+0.3123=0.7862. A single network call has taken a double-length stride.

Check of the formula for σ (\eta=1, \bar\alpha_t=0.5\to\bar\alpha_s=0.6): \sqrt{0.4/0.5}\sqrt{1-0.5/0.6}=0.89443\cdot0.40825=0.3651, the same \sigma_t as the check question of lesson 8.3.

Why it works. With \eta=0 each starting noise is mapped by a fixed deterministic function to one output, and the collection of outputs has the same distribution as DDPM's. The smooth path is the solution of an ordinary differential equation (the "probability-flow ODE" of lesson 8.5), and the DDIM update is one step of a numerical solver for it. So faster sampling is a numerical-analysis question: how big a step can a solver take before its error shows?

Solver trade-offs. Higher-order solvers (Runge–Kutta or multistep methods that reuse earlier network outputs) take larger accurate strides for the same number of evaluations, at the price of extra bookkeeping or more evaluations per step. Beyond solvers, distillation trains a student network to reproduce in one or a few steps what a teacher does in many (progressive distillation halves the step count repeatedly; consistency-style models learn to map any point on a path directly to its endpoint). Those require extra training, and few-step students typically give up some quality or diversity.

Common traps
  • "DDIM is a different model." It uses exactly the same trained noise predictor; only the update rule differs.
  • "Fewer steps is free." It is not: every column of the table to the left of 20 shows a visible error. The lab's exact denoiser makes this error purely a step-size error; a real network adds its own.
  • Deterministic means no diversity? No: the diversity comes from the starting noise, and every starting point gives a different output. It does mean you cannot "resample" an answer from the same start.
  • Comparing at equal S is not comparing at equal cost if one method needs two evaluations per step.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Fast image generation: a sampler that needs tens of steps, or a distilled model that needs a handful, rather than a thousand. Which solver or distilled model a given product uses is not something this module claims.
  • Deterministic samplers allow inversion (running the path backwards from an image to its noise) and smooth interpolation between two noise inputs, which image editing methods use.
  • The same speed-up reasoning applies to flow-matching models (lesson 8.8): both are ODE solvers.

Takeaways

  • The general update is x_s=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_s}\hat x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_s-\sigma^2}\hat\epsilon+\sigma z; \eta=1 is DDPM, \eta=0 is DDIM.
  • DDIM is a numerical solver for a deterministic ODE with the same distribution of results, so fewer, larger steps are possible without retraining.
  • On the toy, 10 steps cost an error of 0.11 for DDIM against 0.13 for DDPM, and 3 steps are still poor (0.53 and 0.54). Distillation trades extra training for fewer steps.
8.5

The score and SDE view

The questionSo far we have a collection of recipes (a noising formula, a noise predictor, two ways of stepping). Is there a single idea underneath them? What is the network really learning, and why do the noisy random walk and the smooth deterministic path give the same answers?

Intuition: an arrow that always points uphill in probability

Picture the density of the data as a landscape of hills, where height means "how likely". At every point, the score is an arrow that points in the direction in which the log-density rises fastest, and is long where the slope is steep. If you drop a ball into the landscape and let it follow the arrows, it climbs to the nearest peak. If you also shake it randomly all the time, it wanders around the peaks in proportion to how high they are, and visits the hills with the right frequencies. This is Langevin dynamics: following the score plus noise produces samples from the distribution.

For raw data this has a problem: far from the data the density is almost zero and its slope is unknown. Noising the data fills the empty space and gives a score at every point, and at many noise levels. A diffusion model learns the score at all levels (lesson 8.2: predicting the noise is learning the score), and sampling means following the arrows from the high-noise level, where the landscape is one smooth hill, down to the low-noise level, where it has the fine peaks of the data.

Build it up

The score. For a density p(x) the score is s(x)=\nabla_x\log p(x)=p'(x)/p(x). It needs no normalising constant: if p=e^{f}/Z then s=f'.

Worked example: scores and one Langevin step

A Gaussian. For p=\mathcal N(1,0.25), \log p=-(x-1)^2/0.5+\text{const}, so s(x)=-2(x-1)/0.5=(1-x)/0.25. At x=0.5: s=0.5/0.25=2, pointing right, toward the peak at 1.

The two-bump data (s=0.3, bumps at \pm1). Computed scores: s(-0.2)=-8.631, s(0.5)=5.555, s(0.9)=1.111, s(1.5)=-5.556. At 0.5 the arrow points right toward the bump at +1, at 1.5 it points left toward it, and near the bump centre it is small.

One Langevin step with step size \varepsilon=0.1 from x=0.5 and a noise draw z=0.5: x'=x+\tfrac\varepsilon2s(x)+\sqrt\varepsilon\,z=0.5+0.05\cdot5.555+0.3162\cdot0.5=0.5+0.2778+0.1581=0.9359. Drift (0.2778) is the pull uphill; the kick (0.1581) is the shake.

Link to the noise predictor. At \bar\alpha=0.5 and x_t=0.2 for the same data, the score of the noised distribution is -0.0377, and the best noise guess is -\sqrt{0.5}\cdot(-0.0377)=+0.0266, the value the formula of lesson 8.2 gives.

x_{k+1}=x_k+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\nabla_x\log p(x_k)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,z_k,\quad z_k\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\qquad(\text{Langevin; exact as }\varepsilon\to0,\ k\to\infty)

The SDE view. Let the step size of the forward process go to zero. The forward noising becomes a stochastic differential equation (SDE) in continuous time \tau\in[0,1]:

dx=-\tfrac12\beta(\tau)\,x\,d\tau+\sqrt{\beta(\tau)}\,dW,\qquad \bar\alpha(\tau)=\exp\Big(-\!\int_0^\tau\beta\Big)

Here dW is the continuous limit of the noise draws (Brownian motion). Discretising it with step \Delta\tau gives x_{t}=\sqrt{1-\beta\Delta\tau}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta\Delta\tau}\,z to first order, the forward step of lesson 8.1. In the labs \beta(\tau)=0.1+19.9\tau, which is the continuous counterpart of the linear schedule: \bar\alpha(1)=4.3\cdot10^{-5}. The same equation can be run backwards in time (a classical result for diffusions), and the only unknown it needs is the score s_\tau(x)=\nabla_x\log p_\tau(x):

\text{reverse SDE:}\ \ dx=\Big[-\tfrac12\beta x-\tfrac{1+\lambda^2}{2}\beta\,s_\tau(x)\Big]d\tau+\lambda\sqrt{\beta}\,d\bar W\quad(\lambda=1)\qquad\text{probability-flow ODE:}\ \ \frac{dx}{d\tau}=-\tfrac12\beta x-\tfrac12\beta\,s_\tau(x)\quad(\lambda=0)
Symbols. \tau: continuous noise time, 1 = pure noise, 0 = data; the reverse equation runs from 1 down to 0. \beta(\tau): the noise rate. s_\tau(x): the score of the noised distribution at time \tau, which the network supplies as -\epsilon_\theta/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}. d\bar W: Brownian motion running backwards. \lambda: how much noise the reverse process injects; \lambda=1 is the reverse SDE, \lambda=0 removes the randomness completely. \varepsilon: the Langevin step size (not the noise \epsilon).

Unification. For every \lambda\ge0 the family of reverse processes carries the distribution p_1 to p_0 and has the same marginal p_\tau at every time. So the random walk and the smooth path are two ways to move the same cloud of probability. DDPM can be viewed as a discretisation of the reverse SDE and DDIM as a discretisation of the ODE; the \eta of lesson 8.4 is the discrete analogue of \lambda.

Checked numerically (exact score, 3 000 starting points, 500 Euler or Euler–Maruyama steps from \tau=1 to 0.002): \lambda=0: standard deviation 1.039, distance to the data 0.049; \lambda=0.5: 1.050, 0.019; \lambda=1: 1.047, 0.021; \lambda=2: 1.048, 0.018. The data standard deviation is 1.044, so all reproduce it, up to the 0.05 finite-sample floor explained in lesson 8.4.

Common traps
  • Two meanings of "score". In classical statistics the score is the derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to parameters. Here it is the derivative with respect to the data point x.
  • Langevin is slow to cross valleys. Particles between two far-apart bumps need many steps to move from one to the other; this is why diffusion starts at a high noise level where the landscape is a single hill.
  • "The ODE is a different model." It has the same score and the same marginals; it differs in what it does to individual particles (no jitter).
  • Large steps. Langevin and the SDE discretisations are exact only for small steps; that is the same error you saw in lessons 8.3 and 8.4.
Where it shows up in AI
  • The score and SDE view is the theory behind samplers: it explains why a noise predictor suffices, justifies deterministic samplers, and lets numerical-analysis tools (adaptive and higher-order solvers) be applied to generation.
  • Score estimation at many noise levels is the same training objective as lesson 8.2 with a different weighting over t.
  • Related general reading: the diffusion maths lesson and the ELBO view from the latent-variable module.

Takeaways

  • The score \nabla_x\log p points uphill in probability; a noise predictor learns it: \epsilon^*=-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,s_t.
  • Langevin dynamics (drift along the score plus noise) samples a distribution; diffusion applies it across a whole ladder of noise levels.
  • The reverse SDE (λ = 1) and the probability-flow ODE (λ = 0) share all marginals; DDPM and DDIM are their discretisations.
8.6

Guidance

The questionA model trained on all the data produces anything in it. How do we make it produce what a prompt asks for, and, since a model that only loosely follows the prompt is disappointing, how do we make it follow the prompt more strongly than its training alone does? And what does that cost?

Intuition: ask the model twice and exaggerate the difference

Give the model the prompt and it says one thing about the noise; give it no prompt and it says something else. The difference between the two answers is exactly what the prompt contributes. If following the prompt is good, following it twice as much should be better: take the no-prompt answer and move from it along that difference by a chosen multiple. The multiple is the guidance scale w. Too small and the prompt is ignored; too large and the model is pushed past anything it saw in training, trading variety and realism for obedience.

Build it up

A conditional model. Give the network an extra input c (a class label, a text embedding): \epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,c). In classifier-free guidance one network learns both versions: during training the condition is replaced by a "null" value for a fraction of the examples (a common choice is about one in ten), so that \epsilon_\theta(x_t,t,\varnothing) is the unconditional prediction. At sampling time both are evaluated and combined:

\epsilon_{\text{guided}}=\epsilon_{\text{uncond}}+w\,\big(\epsilon_{\text{cond}}-\epsilon_{\text{uncond}}\big)
Sign and convention. \epsilon_{\text{cond}}-\epsilon_{\text{uncond}} (conditional minus unconditional) is the push of the prompt. w=0 returns the unconditional prediction (prompt ignored), w=1 returns the plain conditional prediction, and w>1 extrapolates beyond it. Some papers write (1+\omega)\,\epsilon_{\text{cond}}-\omega\,\epsilon_{\text{uncond}}; that is the same formula with \omega=w-1, so always check which convention a source uses before comparing numbers.

In terms of scores. Using \epsilon=-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,s the formula says s_{\text{guided}}=s_u+w(s_c-s_u). By Bayes' rule s_c=s_u+\nabla_x\log p(c\mid x_t), so s_{\text{guided}}=s_u+w\,\nabla_x\log p(c\mid x_t): classifier-free guidance amplifies the gradient of an implicit classifier by w, without training a separate classifier (hence "classifier-free"). It costs two network evaluations per step, usually as one batch of two.

Worked example: the toy conditional model

Two overlapping classes with centres \pm0.8 and width 0.4 each. The unconditional model knows both classes; the conditional model for the class "right" knows only the bump at +0.8. At \bar\alpha=0.5 and a noisy value x_t=0.2 the exact predictions are \epsilon_u=0.1110 and \epsilon_c=-0.4458; the push of the prompt is -0.4458-0.1110=-0.5568.

w=0: 0.1110. w=1: 0.1110+1\cdot(-0.5568)=-0.4458. w=3: 0.1110+3\cdot(-0.5568)=-1.5594.

What the numbers mean. The implied clean value is \hat x_0=(x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\epsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha}. For w=1: (0.2+0.7071\cdot0.4458)/0.7071=0.7287, near the class centre 0.8. For w=3: (0.2+0.7071\cdot1.5594)/0.7071=1.8423, far beyond the class centre. A negative noise estimate says "the noise was negative, so the clean value must be larger".

Common traps
  • Sign slips. The push is conditional minus unconditional. Reversing it moves the sample away from the prompt.
  • Convention. w=1 here means "no extra guidance". In the (1+\omega) convention that is \omega=0. Typical numbers quoted in papers are only comparable inside one convention.
  • "More guidance is always better." Fidelity to the prompt rises while diversity falls; very large w leaves the region where the model was trained, and image generators commonly show over-saturated or distorted results there.
  • It doubles the cost of each step (two evaluations).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Text-to-image and text-to-video diffusion models commonly use classifier-free guidance, with the scale exposed as a user setting; the value that works well differs between systems, so this module gives no recommended number.
  • "Negative prompts" can be implemented by replacing the unconditional prediction with the prediction for the unwanted prompt, so the sample is pushed away from it.
  • The same idea guides class-conditional models, audio models and molecule generators.

  • \epsilon_{\text{guided}}=\epsilon_u+w(\epsilon_c-\epsilon_u): w=0 unconditional, w=1 conditional, w>1 extrapolates (worked: 0.1110, −0.4458, −1.5594 for w = 0, 1, 3).
  • It amplifies the implicit classifier gradient \nabla\log p(c\mid x_t) without a separate classifier, at twice the cost per step.
  • It trades diversity for fidelity, and in the toy the result overshoots even the naive sharpened density: it is a heuristic, not a probabilistic guarantee.
8.7

Latent diffusion

The questionAn image has hundreds of thousands of numbers, and a diffusion model must process all of them dozens of times per sample. Do we really have to? And how does a text prompt get into the network so that each part of the picture can listen to the right words?

Intuition: sketch on a small canvas, then paint it large

Most of an image's pixels are predictable from their neighbours: sky, skin, wall. A pretrained autoencoder squeezes an image into a much smaller grid of numbers (the latent) from which a decoder can rebuild a faithful picture. Latent diffusion runs the entire noising and denoising process on this small grid, and uses the decoder once at the end. The expensive part is made small, and the decoder fills in the detail.

The prompt is injected by cross-attention. Every place in the latent grid asks the prompt, "which of your words matter to me?" and mixes in their information in proportion. The pixel positions provide the questions (queries); the words provide the answers (keys and values).

Build it up

Pipeline. Train an encoder E and decoder D (see variational autoencoders and, for images, autoencoders). Encode every training image, z_0=E(x), and train the noise predictor of lesson 8.2 on z_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}z_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon. To generate: draw z_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I), run the sampler of lessons 8.3 to 8.6 in latent space with the prompt, and decode x=D(z_0). The latent values are usually rescaled to roughly unit variance so the noise schedule means what it did before.

Worked example: counting what is saved (one configuration, not a specific system)

An image of 512\times512\times3=786{,}432 numbers is encoded with downsampling factor f=8 and c=4 latent channels into 64\times64\times4=16{,}384 numbers: 786{,}432/16{,}384=48 times fewer.

Self-attention compares every position with every other. In pixel space there are 512^2=262{,}144 positions, so 262{,}144^2=68{,}719{,}476{,}736 scores per layer and head. On the 64\times64=4{,}096 latent positions there are 4{,}096^2=16{,}777{,}216 scores: 68{,}719{,}476{,}736/16{,}777{,}216=4{,}096=8^4 times fewer, because the number of positions falls by f^2 and attention cost goes as its square.

Patches. A transformer backbone groups the latent into patches of side p; with p=2 that gives (64/2)^2=1{,}024 tokens and 1{,}024^2=1{,}048{,}576 scores: a further 16 times fewer.

Cross-attention from the 4 096 positions to a prompt of 77 tokens (the context length of some text encoders) needs only 4{,}096\cdot77=315{,}392 scores.

A tiny cross-attention (2-D, three prompt tokens with keys (1,0),(0,1),(-1,0) and one image position with query q=(1.5,0.5)): scores q\cdot k/\sqrt2=1.0607,\ 0.3536,\ -1.0607; softmax gives weights 0.6200,\ 0.3057,\ 0.0743 (sum 1.0000). The position receives 62% of the first token's value, 31% of the second and 7% of the third.

\text{CrossAttn}(h,y)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{(hW_Q)(yW_K)^{\top}}{\sqrt d}\Big)\,(yW_V)
Symbols. h: the image (latent) features, one row per position. y: the prompt features, one row per token, from a text encoder. W_Q,W_K,W_V: learned matrices that make queries from h and keys and values from y. d: the query/key dimension; the division by \sqrt d keeps the scores in a sensible range (d=2 in the worked example). The softmax runs over the prompt tokens, so every row of weights sums to 1. This is the attention of scaled dot-product attention, except that queries and keys come from different sources.

Backbones. The noise predictor must map a grid to a grid of the same shape. A U-Net (convolutions that shrink and then expand the grid, with skip connections and attention at some resolutions) has been the standard; a transformer over patch tokens is a common alternative, with the cost of attention from the numbers above. Both receive the noise level t as an embedding. Which one a given system uses is a design choice that has changed over time, and this module does not claim it for any product.

Costs and limits. The saving is in the denoiser (the part run S times); the encoder is used in training and the decoder once per sample. The price: the autoencoder is a ceiling on quality, since a detail the decoder cannot rebuild from the latent can never appear in an output, and training needs two stages (autoencoder first, then diffusion).

Common traps
  • "Latent diffusion makes the image smaller." The final picture is full size; the decoder restores it. Only the denoising runs on the small grid.
  • Compression is not free quality. A very aggressive f loses fine detail (small text, faces) irreversibly; the diffusion model cannot add back what the autoencoder removed.
  • Cross-attention is not symmetric. Queries come from the image, keys and values from the prompt. The prompt does not "look at" the image in this layer.
  • Cost depends on the whole step count. A cheaper denoiser helps every one of the S steps, and guidance doubles them.
Where it shows up in AI
  • The latent-diffusion recipe (autoencoder, diffusion on its latent, cross-attention to a text encoder) is the template behind the family of open and commercial text-to-image systems usually described as "Stable-Diffusion-style"; the exact sizes, encoders and backbones vary by system and version.
  • Video generators apply the same idea with a time axis added to the latent (see video and audio generation).
  • Pipeline view: the autoencoder and the text encoder are separate pretrained components; the denoiser is the main pretraining cost; at generation time the cost is S (times two with guidance) denoiser calls plus one decode.

Check yourself

Image: 256·256·3 = 196,608. Latent: 64·64·4 = 16,384. Ratio 196,608 / 16,384 = 12.
2. For the same f = 4 (no patching), how many times fewer self-attention scores does the latent need than the pixels?
Positions: 256² = 65,536 versus 64² = 4,096. Scores: 65,536² versus 4,096², a ratio of (65,536/4,096)² = 16² = 256 = f⁴.

Takeaways

  • Latent diffusion runs the noising and denoising on an autoencoder's small latent (for example 786,432 values become 16,384, 48× fewer) and decodes once at the end.
  • Attention cost falls with the fourth power of the downsampling factor (4,096× at f = 8); the autoencoder sets a ceiling on detail.
  • Text enters through cross-attention: queries from the image, keys and values from the prompt, weights \mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt d). The backbone may be a U-Net or a transformer.
8.8

Flow matching

The questionDiffusion destroys the data with noise and then learns to reverse the damage. Could we skip the destruction story entirely: pair a noise sample with a data sample, draw the straight line between them, and train a network to say, at every point and time, which way the particle should be moving? Does following that learned arrow really carry noise to data?

Intuition: straight conveyor belts, averaged over partners

Pick a noise value and a data value at random and draw a straight conveyor belt between them, to be crossed at constant speed in one unit of time. For a single pair the speed and direction are known exactly: distance divided by time. But a particle standing at some point at some time does not know which pair it belongs to; many belts cross at that point heading to different destinations. The network therefore learns the average velocity of all belts crossing that spot. Following this average field moves the whole cloud of noise into the cloud of data, even though no individual particle follows its own straight belt.

Build it up

Notation flip. In this lesson x_0 is a noise sample, x_1 is a data sample and time runs from 0 (noise) to 1 (data), the opposite of lessons 8.1 to 8.7. The straight-line path and its velocity are

x_t=(1-t)\,x_0+t\,x_1,\qquad \frac{dx_t}{dt}=x_1-x_0,\qquad x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\ x_1\sim p_{\text{data}},\ t\in[0,1]
Worked example: one pair, then the average

A single pair. Noise x_0=-1.2, data x_1=1. At t=0.25: x_{0.25}=0.75\cdot(-1.2)+0.25\cdot1=-0.65. The velocity is x_1-x_0=1-(-1.2)=2.2, constant: four Euler steps of size 0.25 give -1.2\to-0.65\to-0.10\to0.45\to1.00, arriving exactly at the data value.

The average over partners for the two-point data \{-1,+1\} (the case s\to0). Given x_1=\pm1, the noisy position is x_t\sim\mathcal N(\pm t,(1-t)^2), and the velocity for a pair is (x_1-x_t)/(1-t) (the remaining distance divided by the remaining time). Averaging over which data point the particle belongs to gives v^*(x,t)=\big(\tanh\!\big(t\,x/(1-t)^2\big)-x\big)/(1-t). At t=0.5, x=0.4: \tanh(0.5\cdot0.4/0.25)=\tanh(0.8)=0.6640, so v^*=(0.6640-0.4)/0.5=0.5281. The expected destination is 0.664, and the velocity is the remaining distance over the remaining time.

At t = 0 the average points to the middle. A pure noise value x_0=x knows nothing about its partner, so v^*(x,0)=\mathbb E[x_1]-x=-x. The field's arrows near t=0 point toward the centre of the data, and the paths bend later, as the lab shows.

The training loss. The network v_\theta(x,t) regresses the pair velocity, which is a plain squared-error loss just like lesson 8.2:

L(\theta)=\mathbb E_{t,\,x_0,\,x_1}\big\lVert v_\theta\big((1-t)x_0+t\,x_1,\ t\big)-(x_1-x_0)\big\rVert^2,\qquad v^*(x,t)=\mathbb E\big[x_1-x_0\mid x_t=x\big]
Symbols. x_0: a noise sample, x_1: a data sample, drawn independently (any pairing works). t: time in [0, 1], typically drawn uniformly. x_t: the point on the straight line. x_1-x_0: the target velocity of that pair. v_\theta: the network. v^*: the exact minimiser, the average velocity of all pairs passing through (x,t).

Sampling solves the ordinary differential equation dx/dt=v_\theta(x,t) from t=0 (a noise sample) to t=1, for example by Euler steps: x\leftarrow x+v_\theta(x,t)/S repeated S times, t\leftarrow t+1/S. This is the same kind of solver as in lesson 8.4.

Why it transports noise to data. The marginal density p_t of the straight-line interpolant x_t moves from the noise distribution at t=0 to the data distribution at t=1, and the velocity v^* is exactly the field that moves p_t along (it satisfies the continuity equation \partial_tp_t+\partial_x(p_tv^*)=0). Therefore integrating v^* from noise gives a sample of p_t at every time, and a data sample at t=1. For a mixture of normal distributions everything is explicit: with V_k(t)=(1-t)^2+t^2s_k^2, the interpolant is a mixture with components \mathcal N(t\,m_k,\,V_k) and

v^*(x,t)=\sum_k r_k(x,t)\Big[m_k+\frac{t\,s_k^2-(1-t)}{V_k(t)}\,(x-t\,m_k)\Big],\qquad r_k\propto w_k\,\mathcal N\big(x;\,t\,m_k,\,V_k(t)\big)

where r_k are the posterior weights of the components, w_k the mixture weights, m_k and s_k the component centres and widths. I checked this formula against a Monte Carlo average of x_1-x_0 over 3 million pairs at t=0.5: at x=0.4 the Monte Carlo value is 0.471 and the formula 0.480, at x=-1 they are -0.077 and -0.074 (bins of width 0.2 explain the small differences), and for s\to0 it reduces to the \tanh expression above (0.5281 both ways).

Relation to diffusion. Both generate by integrating an ODE from noise to data: for diffusion the field is built from the score (lesson 8.5) and the path is set by the noise schedule; for flow matching one chooses the path (here a straight line) and regresses its velocity directly. Both are regressions with a squared-error loss on a conditional target, and the same samplers ideas, guidance (lesson 8.6) and latent spaces (lesson 8.7) apply. Rectified flow is the name for this straight-line formulation, together with an optional reflow step: generate pairs by running the trained flow, then retrain on those pairs, so that paths become straighter and fewer steps are needed (an idea described here only conceptually; the lab does not run reflow).

Common traps
  • Opposite conventions. Here x_0 is noise and t=1 is data. In diffusion x_0 is data and t=T is noise. Always check which one a paper uses.
  • "The paths are straight." Each training pair is straight. The learned average field generally bends because many pairs cross at each point (the arrows at t = 0 point to the middle). Paths are straighter than a high-curvature path would be, not exactly straight; reflow reduces the bend.
  • One-step generation is not automatic. A single Euler step gives the data mean (S = 1 above). Few-step quality needs straighter fields, solvers or distillation, as in lesson 8.4.
  • The network does not see the pair. It receives only (x_t,t), so its best output is the average v^*, not x_1-x_0 of the sample at hand; the loss floor is positive, as in lesson 8.2.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Flow-matching and rectified-flow objectives appear in recent research on image and video generation; this module does not state which products use them.
  • The recipe (sample a time, make an interpolant, regress a velocity) is simple enough to apply to other data such as molecules, speech and robot actions, and shares the training-stage compute view of any regression pretraining.
  • Connections: normalising flows also move a simple density to data by an invertible map, but need a tractable Jacobian; flow matching avoids that by training the velocity by regression.

Check yourself

1. For the pair x₀ = 2 (noise) and x₁ = −1 (data), find x at t = 0.5 and the target velocity.
x₀.₅ = 0.5·2 + 0.5·(−1) = 0.5. Velocity = x₁ − x₀ = −1 − 2 = −3 (constant along the line).
2. Why can a network not output x₁ − x₀ exactly?
It sees only x_t and t, not the pair. Many different pairs pass through the same point with different velocities, so the best output is their average v*(x, t), and the loss cannot reach 0.
3. In the two-bump toy v*(x, 0) = −x. Where does a single Euler step (S = 1) send every particle, and why?
x ← x + v*(x, 0)·1 = x − x = 0: every particle lands at 0, the mean of the data, not on a bump. The one step uses the velocity at t = 0 for the whole journey, but the true velocity changes as the particle gets closer to a bump.

Takeaways

  • Flow matching: x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\,x_1 with x_0 noise and x_1 data; the target velocity of a pair is x_1-x_0 and the loss is a squared error.
  • The minimiser is the average velocity v^*=\mathbb E[x_1-x_0\mid x_t]; integrating it carries the noise density to the data density (verified on the toy, where the histogram matches the exact density at every t and the final distance falls from 0.455 at 2 steps to 0.051 at 100).
  • It shares samplers, guidance and latent spaces with diffusion, but the path is chosen rather than derived from a noise schedule; straighter paths are the goal of rectified flow.
