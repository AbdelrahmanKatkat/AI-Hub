8.1

Markov chains

The questionA web surfer clicks a random link on every page, forever. After a million clicks, what fraction of the time was she on each page? A language model has just written "the cat" — what decides the next word? Both are the same kind of problem: a system that hops between states, with the next hop depending only on where it is now. Can we predict, with a matrix, where it will be after 1, 10 or a million steps — and what it settles into?

Intuition

Think of a board game played with dice. Your piece sits on a square. To know what can happen next you need exactly one thing: which square you are on. How you got there — which dice you rolled earlier — is irrelevant. This "forget the past, remember only the present" property is the Markov property, and a process with it is a Markov chain.

The things the piece can stand on are the states (sunny / cloudy / rainy; web pages; words). Each state has a menu of arrows to other states, and each arrow carries a probability. A Markov chain is nothing more than a walker who, at every tick, chooses an arrow by rolling a loaded die. We want to understand the walker's behaviour on average and in the long run.

Build it up

Step 1 — the transition matrix. Number the states 1…n. Let P_{ij} be the probability of going to state j from state i in one step. Collect these into an n×n matrix P. Every entry is between 0 and 1, and because the walker must go somewhere, each row sums to 1 (such a matrix is called row-stochastic). Row i is the loaded die you roll when standing on i.

Worked example — a two-state weather chain

States: Sunny (1) and Rainy (2). After a sunny day it is sunny again with probability 0.9, rainy with 0.1. After a rainy day it is sunny with 0.5, rainy with 0.5:

P=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.1\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}

Both rows sum to 1 ✓. Sunny weather is sticky (it persists 90 % of the time), so we expect long-run weather to be mostly sunny — the matrix will tell us exactly how mostly.

Step 2 — a whole distribution moves at once. Suppose we don't know the state but have a probability vector \pi_t=(\pi_t(1),\dots,\pi_t(n)), a row of numbers summing to 1: \pi_t(i) is the probability we are in state i at time t. To land in state j at time t+1 we must have been in some state i and then taken the i→j arrow. The law of total probability (Module 3) adds up all such routes:

\pi_{t+1}(j)=\sum_{i}\pi_t(i)\,P_{ij}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \pi_{t+1}=\pi_t\,P

That sum is exactly a row vector times a matrix (Module 1). So one step of the chain is one matrix multiplication, and n steps is n multiplications: \pi_n=\pi_0P^n.

Worked example — today is sunny, what next?

Start sure to be sunny: \pi_0=(1,0).

\pi_1=\pi_0P=(0.9,\,0.1) — just row 1 of P.

\pi_2=\pi_1P=(0.9\cdot0.9+0.1\cdot0.5,\;0.9\cdot0.1+0.1\cdot0.5)=(0.86,\,0.14).

\pi_3=(0.86\cdot0.9+0.14\cdot0.5,\;\dots)=(0.844,\,0.156), and \pi_4=(0.8376,\,0.1624). The numbers are settling.

The same two-step answer comes straight from the matrix square: P^2=\begin{pmatrix}0.86&0.14\\0.70&0.30\end{pmatrix}. Its entry (P^2)_{ij} is the probability of going from i to j in exactly two steps (summed over the middle state), and in general (P^n)_{ij} is the n-step probability. Row 1 of P^2 is the \pi_2 we just found ✓.

Step 3 — where does it settle? The stationary distribution. Notice \pi_t stopped changing much. A distribution that the chain leaves unchanged is called stationary:

\pi=\pi P,\qquad \sum_i\pi(i)=1,\quad \pi(i)\ge 0

Read \pi P=\pi as an eigenvector equation (Module 1): \pi is a left eigenvector of P with eigenvalue 1 (equivalently an ordinary eigenvector of the transpose, P^\top\pi^\top=\pi^\top). Does such a thing always exist? Yes: because every row sums to 1, P\mathbf 1=\mathbf 1 (multiply P by the all-ones column and each entry is a row sum = 1). So 1 is always an eigenvalue of P; P and P^\top share eigenvalues, so a left eigenvector exists too, and the Perron–Frobenius theorem says it can be chosen with non-negative entries.

Worked example — solve for the stationary distribution

Write \pi=(a,b) with a+b=1. The first coordinate of \pi P=\pi says 0.9a+0.5b=a, i.e. 0.5b=0.1a, so a=5b. With a+b=1: b=\tfrac16,\;a=\tfrac56.

\pi=\left(\tfrac56,\tfrac16\right)\approx(0.8333,\,0.1667)

In words: in the long run 83 % of days are sunny. Check by multiplying: \tfrac56\cdot0.9+\tfrac16\cdot0.5=0.75+0.0833=0.8333 ✓.

How fast? The eigenvalues of P are 1 and (trace − 1) = 1.4 − 1 = 0.4. The gap between \pi_t and \pi is multiplied by 0.4 each step: \pi_1(1)-\tfrac56=0.0667, then 0.0267, then 0.0107 — each is 0.4 times the previous ✓. The second-largest eigenvalue magnitude controls how quickly the chain forgets its start; close to 1 means slow mixing.

Step 4 — when does it really converge? Ergodicity. Two things can go wrong. (a) Islands: if the state set splits into groups you can never leave (or a state that traps you — an absorbing state), where you end up depends on where you started and there is no single answer. (b) Rigid cycles: if the walker marches A→B→C→A→… the distribution never settles — it just rotates — even though a stationary distribution exists. A finite chain with neither problem is called ergodic: irreducible (every state can reach every other) and aperiodic (no forced cycle). Then (i) the stationary distribution is unique, (ii) \pi_0P^n\to\pi from any start, and (iii) the ergodic theorem: the fraction of time one single long walk spends in state i converges to \pi(i). This is why simulating one long trajectory can replace averaging over the whole distribution — it is the foundation of MCMC.

Step 5 — building a chain that has the distribution you want (Metropolis). Reverse the question: suppose I want samples from a target distribution \pi that I can only evaluate up to an unknown constant, \pi(i)\propto f(i) (typical for posteriors — next lesson). Construct a chain whose stationary distribution is π and run it. A convenient sufficient condition is detailed balance:

\pi(i)\,P_{ij}=\pi(j)\,P_{ji}\quad\text{for all }i,j

(Probability flow from i to j equals the flow back.) Summing over i gives \sum_i\pi(i)P_{ij}=\pi(j)\sum_iP_{ji}=\pi(j), which is stationarity ✓. The Metropolis rule achieves it: from state i, propose a move to j (with a symmetric proposal), then accept it with probability \min\!\big(1,f(j)/f(i)\big); otherwise stay. Only the ratio f(j)/f(i) appears, so the unknown constant cancels.

Worked example — checking detailed balance

Two states with unnormalised weights f(1)=1 and f(2)=3 (so the true target is π=(¼,¾)). Propose the other state with probability ½.

From 1→2: accept with min(1, 3/1) = 1, so P_{12}=\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac12. From 2→1: accept with min(1, 1/3) = ⅓, so P_{21}=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac16.

Detailed balance: \pi(1)P_{12}=\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac18 and \pi(2)P_{21}=\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac16=\tfrac18 ✓. The walker spends ¾ of its time in the heavier state, as it should, without anyone ever computing the normaliser 4.

Common traps
  • Row versus column convention. Here rows sum to 1 and distributions are rows (\pi P). Many books use columns summing to 1 and write P\pi. Always check which one a source uses.
  • "Stationary" does not mean "frozen". The walker keeps hopping; only the distribution stays the same.
  • The Markov property depends on what you call the state. If tomorrow depends on today and yesterday, define the state as the pair (yesterday, today) and it is Markov again.
  • MCMC samples are correlated and start wrong. Consecutive samples are neighbours, and the first steps come from the starting point, not from π ("burn-in" is discarded).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Language models. A bigram model is literally a Markov chain on words with P_{ij}=\Pr(\text{next}=j\mid\text{current}=i). An n-gram uses the last n−1 words as the state. An autoregressive transformer is Markov if you take the state to be its whole context window; generating text is repeated sampling from the row selected by the context, and temperature sharpens or flattens that row.
  • Reinforcement learning. A fixed policy turns an MDP into a Markov chain with matrix P^\pi. The value of each state satisfies v=r+\gamma P^\pi v, so v=(I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1}r. For the weather chain with rewards r=(1,0) and γ=0.9 this gives v≈(8.59, 7.03): sunny states are worth more. The stationary distribution says how often a policy visits each state.
  • PageRank is the stationary distribution of the "random surfer" chain (with 15 % teleportation to make it ergodic).
  • MCMC (Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs sampling, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) samples Bayesian posteriors — the topic of the next lesson.
  • Diffusion models noise data with a Markov chain, one small Gaussian step at a time (Lesson 8.5); hidden Markov models underlie classic speech recognition.

Check yourself

1. Find the stationary distribution of P = [[0.7, 0.3],[0.4, 0.6]].
Solve 0.7a + 0.4b = a ⇒ 0.4b = 0.3a ⇒ a = (4/3)b. With a + b = 1: b = 3/7, a = 4/7. So π = (4/7, 3/7) ≈ (0.571, 0.429). (The other eigenvalue is trace − 1 = 0.3.)
2. For P = [[0.5, 0.5],[0.2, 0.8]], what is the probability of going from state 1 to state 1 in exactly two steps?
Sum over the middle state: (P²)₁₁ = P₁₁P₁₁ + P₁₂P₂₁ = 0.5·0.5 + 0.5·0.2 = 0.25 + 0.10 = 0.35.
3. A chain simply alternates A → B → A → B … with probability 1. Does π₀Pⁿ converge? Does a stationary distribution exist?
It does not converge: starting at A the distribution flips between (1,0) and (0,1) forever (the chain is periodic). A stationary distribution still exists, (½, ½): P maps it to itself. The time-average of a single walk does equal (½, ½); it is the distribution at a fixed time that fails to converge.

Takeaways

  • A Markov chain forgets the past; its whole law is the row-stochastic matrix P.
  • One step is \pi\mapsto\pi P; n steps is \pi P^n.
  • The stationary distribution is the left eigenvector of P with eigenvalue 1; the second eigenvalue sets the convergence speed.
  • Ergodic (irreducible + aperiodic) chains converge to π from anywhere, and time averages equal π-averages — which is what lets MCMC sample unnormalised targets using detailed balance.
8.2

Bayesian inference & graphical models

The questionA coin lands heads 7 times in 10. Is its bias exactly 0.7? What if we were fairly sure it was a normal coin — how much should 10 flips move us? And a harder one: two machines make bolts of slightly different lengths, the measurements are mixed in one box, and nobody recorded which machine made which bolt. Can we still learn about both machines — and say which one probably made bolt number 17?

Intuition

The Bayesian viewpoint says: whatever you are unsure about, describe with a probability distribution — and update it when evidence arrives. Before looking at data you have a prior belief. The data tells you how well each possible truth explains what you saw (the likelihood). Multiply the two and renormalise: that is your posterior belief. A detective works the same way: a list of suspects (prior), each clue is more or less likely under each suspect (likelihood), and the list is reweighted.

The second puzzle introduces a new kind of unknown: a latent variable — a quantity that influenced the data but was never recorded (which machine made the bolt; which topic a document is about; the "idea" a painter had before painting). We model the world as a generative story: first nature picks the latent z, then z produces the observation x. Drawing the story as arrows (z → x) gives a graphical model: a node per variable, an arrow from each cause to its effects.

The graph is also a recipe for the joint probability: each node contributes one factor, "its probability given its parents". Here p(\theta,z_{1:N},x_{1:N})=p(\theta)\prod_i p(z_i\mid\theta)\,p(x_i\mid z_i,\theta). The picture tells you at a glance which variables depend on which.

Build it up

Step 1 — Bayes' rule with names. From Module 3, p(A\mid B)p(B)=p(B\mid A)p(A). Let θ be the unknown and D the data:

\underbrace{p(\theta\mid D)}_{\text{posterior}}=\frac{\overbrace{p(D\mid\theta)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(D)}_{\text{evidence}}},\qquad p(D)=\int p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta

The evidence p(D) is just the normaliser that makes the posterior sum to 1. Its formula is a marginalisation: to get the probability of the data regardless of θ, average the likelihood over every possible θ, weighted by the prior (sum instead of integral for discrete unknowns). Remember this integral — it is the villain of the lesson.

Step 2 — the coin, and conjugacy. Let θ be the probability of heads. A natural prior on a number in [0,1] is the Beta distribution: p(\theta)\propto\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}. Think of α and β as pseudo-counts: imaginary heads and tails you saw before the experiment (α=β=1 is flat; α=β=2 mildly favours fairness). After h real heads and t real tails the likelihood is \theta^h(1-\theta)^t. Multiply:

p(\theta\mid D)\propto\theta^{h}(1-\theta)^{t}\cdot\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}=\theta^{(\alpha+h)-1}(1-\theta)^{(\beta+t)-1}

That is again the shape of a Beta — with parameters α+h and β+t. When the posterior stays in the same family as the prior, the prior is called conjugate to the likelihood. We never had to compute the evidence: we recognised the shape and the normaliser comes for free. Learning became counting.

Worked example

Prior Beta(2,2) (a mild belief in fairness). Data: 7 heads, 3 tails. Posterior: Beta(2+7, 2+3) = Beta(9, 5).

Posterior mean \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}=\frac{9}{14}=0.643. The mode (most probable θ, also called the MAP estimate) is \frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+\beta-2}=\frac{8}{12}=0.667. The maximum-likelihood estimate that ignores the prior is h/(h+t)=0.7. The prior pulled us back toward ½.

With 100× more data (700 heads, 300 tails) the posterior is Beta(702, 302) with mean 702/1004 = 0.699: the data swamps the prior.

Step 3 — marginalising a latent variable. In the bolt story, z ∈ {1,2} is the machine, p(z=k)=\pi_k is how much of the output each makes, and given the machine the length is Gaussian: p(x\mid z=k)=\mathcal N(x;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2). We only see x, so the probability of a bolt length marginalises z out:

p(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(z=k)\,p(x\mid z=k)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)

This is a Gaussian mixture model (GMM). Bayes' rule now answers "which machine made bolt x?" — the posterior over the latent variable is called the responsibility:

r_k(x)=p(z=k\mid x)=\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)}{\sum_{j}\pi_j\,\mathcal N(x;\mu_j,\sigma_j^2)}
Worked example

Two machines with equal output (π=(½,½)), lengths \mathcal N(0,1) and \mathcal N(4,1). A bolt measures x=1.

Machine 1: \mathcal N(1;0,1)=\frac{e^{-0.5}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=0.2420. Machine 2: \mathcal N(1;4,1)=\frac{e^{-4.5}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=0.0044.

Responsibility of machine 1: \frac{0.5\cdot0.2420}{0.5\cdot0.2420+0.5\cdot0.0044}=\frac{0.2420}{0.2464}=0.982. We are 98 % sure.

Step 4 — the chicken-and-egg problem and EM. To learn the machine parameters (μ, σ, π) we face a loop. If we knew which machine made each bolt, estimation would be easy: group and average. If we knew the parameters, assigning machines would be easy: compute responsibilities. We know neither, so alternate (the Expectation–Maximisation algorithm):

  • E-step: with parameters fixed, compute responsibilities r_{ik} for every point i and component k.
  • M-step: with responsibilities fixed, re-estimate each component with weighted averages: N_k=\sum_ir_{ik},\;\mu_k=\frac1{N_k}\sum_ir_{ik}x_i,\;\sigma_k^2=\frac1{N_k}\sum_ir_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)^2,\;\pi_k=\frac{N_k}N.

Each round can only increase the data's log-likelihood, so EM climbs steadily — but possibly to a local peak, which is why the start matters.

Worked example — one M-step by hand

Three points x = (0, 1, 5). Suppose the E-step gave component A the responsibilities (0.9, 0.8, 0.1), so component B gets (0.1, 0.2, 0.9).

N_A=0.9+0.8+0.1=1.8, \mu_A=\frac{0.9\cdot0+0.8\cdot1+0.1\cdot5}{1.8}=\frac{1.3}{1.8}=0.722, \pi_A=\frac{1.8}{3}=0.6.

N_B=1.2, \mu_B=\frac{0.1\cdot0+0.2\cdot1+0.9\cdot5}{1.2}=\frac{4.7}{1.2}=3.917, \pi_B=0.4. The component means moved to the weighted centres of their points: A near the pair 0 and 1, B toward the far point 5.

Step 5 — why exact Bayesian inference is often hopeless. Everything above worked because we could write down the evidence p(x) (a finite sum of Gaussians, a Beta integral). In modern models we usually cannot:

  • If the latent z is continuous and the likelihood p(x\mid z) is a neural network, p(x)=\int p(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz has no closed form and no feasible numerical integration when z has dozens of dimensions.
  • With n binary latent variables the sum has 2^n terms (n = 100 already exceeds the number of atoms you could count).
  • Without p(x) we cannot normalise the posterior p(z\mid x)=p(x\mid z)p(z)/p(x), and EM's E-step needs that posterior.

Two escape routes dominate modern practice: sample from the posterior with a Markov chain that needs only unnormalised densities (Lesson 8.1), or approximate it with a simple distribution and optimise (Lesson 8.4). Conjugate cases like Beta–Bernoulli are the lucky exceptions.

Common traps
  • Likelihood is not a distribution over θ. p(D\mid\theta) need not integrate to 1 over θ. Only the posterior does.
  • "No prior" does not exist. A flat prior is still a choice (and flat in θ is not flat in, say, log θ).
  • Mode ≠ mean. MAP (mode) is 0.667 and the posterior mean 0.643 above; they differ whenever the posterior is skewed.
  • Responsibilities are soft assignments, not hard labels, and the component labels are arbitrary (swapping "machine 1" and "machine 2" gives the same model).
  • EM can collapse. A Gaussian centred on a single point can shrink its σ to 0 and make the likelihood infinite; implementations add a variance floor. It can also stall in a local optimum.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Naive Bayes classifiers: a latent class z with independent features given z, and Bayes' rule to get p(z\mid x).
  • Gaussian mixtures and EM for clustering, speaker identification, anomaly detection; hidden Markov models (trained by Baum–Welch, an EM) for classic speech recognition; topic models (LDA) use latent topics per word.
  • Thompson sampling in bandits and A/B testing keeps a Beta posterior per option and plays whichever option's sampled θ is largest.
  • Generative models are latent-variable models: a VAE has a latent code z and a neural decoder (Lesson 8.4); a diffusion model has a chain of latents x_{T},\dots,x_1 (Lesson 8.5).
  • Bayesian deep learning places a distribution over weights; the intractable posterior is why approximations (variational, MC dropout, ensembles) exist.

Check yourself

1. Start from a uniform prior Beta(1,1) and observe 3 heads and 1 tail. What is the posterior and its mean?
Beta(1+3, 1+1) = Beta(4, 2); mean = 4/(4+2) = 2/3 ≈ 0.667. (The MLE would be 3/4 = 0.75.)
2. Mixture weights π = (0.7, 0.3), components N(0,1) and N(4,1). What are the responsibilities for x = 2?
x = 2 is exactly halfway, so both Gaussians give the same density there. The likelihood terms cancel and the posterior equals the prior weights: (0.7, 0.3). (Check: 0.7·g / (0.7·g + 0.3·g) = 0.7.)
3. Why can we not just apply Bayes' rule exactly to a latent-variable model whose decoder is a deep neural network?
The denominator p(x) = ∫ p(x|z)p(z) dz has no closed form for a neural-network likelihood and is far too high-dimensional to integrate numerically, so the posterior p(z|x) cannot be normalised. We must sample (MCMC) or approximate (variational inference).

Takeaways

  • Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior; the evidence only normalises, and it is a marginalisation.
  • Conjugate priors (Beta for Bernoulli) turn updating into counting.
  • Latent variables explain data through hidden causes; responsibilities are posteriors over them; EM alternates E (infer) and M (re-fit).
  • For rich models the evidence integral is intractable — the motivation for MCMC and variational inference.
8.3

Kernels & similarity

The questionBlue points sit in the middle of a ring of orange points. A straight line — which is all a linear classifier can draw — cannot separate them. Yet a small trick (adding the single feature x²+y²) makes the problem trivial. Can we get that kind of power, even in infinitely many extra features, without ever computing those features? And can we say how similar two arbitrary objects are in a way that is mathematically well behaved?

Intuition

Imagine the blue and orange points drawn on a rubber sheet. Pull the sheet up in the middle like a bowl: points far from the centre rise, points near the centre stay low. Now the blue points are on the floor of the bowl and the orange ones on the rim, and a flat horizontal plane slides between them. A linear separator in the lifted space becomes a circle when we look from above. The lift is a feature map φ; the recipe "make new coordinates so that straight lines become powerful" is behind SVMs, kernel regression and — surprisingly — attention.

The second half of the story: many algorithms never need the new coordinates themselves, only dot products between lifted points, φ(x)·φ(x′). A dot product measures similarity (Module 1: big when two vectors point the same way). If some function k(x,x′) returns that dot product directly, we skip the lifting entirely. That function is a kernel.

Build it up

Use the lab to see the lift first; the algebra follows.

Step 1 — feature maps. A feature map φ sends an input to a (usually higher-dimensional) vector. Ours is \varphi(x,y)=(x,\,y,\,x^2+y^2). A linear model in feature space, w\cdot\varphi(x)+b, equals w_1x+w_2y+w_3(x^2+y^2)+b — a curved boundary in the original space (a circle when w₃≠0). Nothing about the learning algorithm changed; only the coordinates did.

Step 2 — the kernel trick. Define k(x,x')=\varphi(x)\cdot\varphi(x'). Take a different feature map, the degree-2 monomials of a 2-D input: \varphi(x)=(x_1^2,\;\sqrt2\,x_1x_2,\;x_2^2).

Worked example — the polynomial kernel

Let x = (1, 2) and y = (3, 1).

The long way. φ(x) = (1², √2·1·2, 2²) = (1, 2√2, 4). φ(y) = (9, √2·3·1, 1) = (9, 3√2, 1). Dot product: 1·9 + 2√2·3√2 + 4·1 = 9 + 12 + 4 = 25.

The shortcut. The plain dot product is x·y = 1·3 + 2·1 = 5, and 5² = 25. Indeed (x\cdot y)^2=(x_1y_1+x_2y_2)^2=x_1^2y_1^2+2x_1x_2y_1y_2+x_2^2y_2^2=\varphi(x)\cdot\varphi(y). The √2 in φ is there precisely to produce that 2.

Why it matters. For d-dimensional inputs and degree p, the feature vector has about \binom{d+p-1}{p} entries. With d = 1000, p = 3 that is 167,167,000 numbers; the kernel (x\cdot y)^3 costs ~1000 multiplications.

Step 3 — the Gaussian (RBF) kernel. The most-used kernel needs no explicit φ at all:

k(x,x')=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert x-x'\rVert^2}{2\ell^2}\right)

It equals 1 when the points coincide and decays toward 0 as they move apart; ℓ is the length-scale, "how far counts as near". It corresponds to a feature space with infinitely many dimensions: expanding e^{x\cdot x'/\ell^2}=\sum_{n}\frac{(x\cdot x')^n}{\ell^{2n}n!} shows it contains polynomial features of every degree at once.

Worked example

With ℓ=1: points 0 and 1 (distance 1): e^{-1/2}=0.6065. Points 0 and 2 (distance 2): e^{-4/2}=e^{-2}=0.1353. Now shrink ℓ to 0.5 and compare 0 and 1: e^{-1/(2\cdot0.25)}=e^{-2}=0.1353 — a smaller ℓ makes the same distance count as "far".

Step 4 — which functions are legal kernels? Given points x_1,\dots,x_n, the kernel (Gram) matrix is K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j). If k really is a dot product of features, then for any coefficient vector c:

c^\top Kc=\sum_{i,j}c_ic_j\,\varphi(x_i)\!\cdot\!\varphi(x_j)=\Big\lVert\sum_ic_i\varphi(x_i)\Big\rVert^2\;\ge\;0

So K is symmetric positive semi-definite (PSD): all eigenvalues ≥ 0 (Module 1 on eigenvalues). The converse is Mercer's theorem: any symmetric function whose Gram matrices are always PSD is a dot product in some feature space. Sums and products of kernels, and positive multiples, are again kernels.

Worked example

RBF, ℓ=1, points 0 and 1: K=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.6065\\0.6065&1\end{pmatrix}. Its eigenvalues are 1 ± 0.6065 = 1.6065 and 0.3935 — both positive ✓. (Eigenvectors (1,1) and (1,−1): "both points high together" is common, "one up, one down" is rarer because they are similar.)

  2. Switch to Linear and Polynomial: the number of non-zero eigenvalues equals the dimension of the feature space (1 and 3).

Step 5 — Gaussian processes: a distribution over functions. A Gaussian process (GP) says: the unknown function f is random, and for any chosen inputs x_1,\dots,x_n the vector of function values is jointly Gaussian with mean 0 and covariance matrix K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j). The kernel decides how correlated two function values are: with RBF, neighbouring inputs have nearly equal outputs, so draws from the prior are smooth curves, with ℓ controlling how fast they wiggle.

Observe noisy values y=f(X)+\text{noise}, noise variance \sigma_n^2. Conditioning a joint Gaussian on part of it (Module 3 formulas) gives a Gaussian at every new input x_*:

\mu_*=k_*^\top\,(K+\sigma_n^2I)^{-1}y,\qquad \sigma_*^2=k(x_*,x_*)-k_*^\top\,(K+\sigma_n^2I)^{-1}k_*
Worked example — one observation

RBF with ℓ=1 and amplitude 1, noise variance 0.1. We observed y = 1.2 at x = 0. Predict at x_*=1. Here K = k(0,0) = 1, so K+\sigma_n^2=1.1, and k_*=k(1,0)=0.6065.

Mean: 0.6065\cdot\frac{1.2}{1.1}=0.662. Variance: 1-\frac{0.6065^2}{1.1}=1-0.3344=0.666, so σ* ≈ 0.816.

At the observed point itself (x_*=0): mean = 1.2/1.1 = 1.091 (pulled slightly toward 0 because the data are noisy), variance = 1 − 1/1.1 = 0.091. The belief tightens where we have data and relaxes back to the prior (mean 0, variance 1) as we move away.

Step 6 — attention as a kernel smoother. The simplest kernel regressor, the Nadaraya–Watson smoother, predicts a weighted average of the known outputs, weighted by similarity:

\hat y(q)=\sum_i\frac{k(q,x_i)}{\sum_jk(q,x_j)}\,y_i
Worked example

Data (x,y): (0,1), (1,3), (2,2). Predict at q=0.5 with a Gaussian kernel, ℓ=1. Distances 0.5, 0.5, 1.5 give weights e^{-0.125}=0.8825, 0.8825, e^{-1.125}=0.3247 (sum 2.0897).

\hat y=\frac{0.8825\cdot1+0.8825\cdot3+0.3247\cdot2}{2.0897}=\frac{4.1794}{2.0897}=2.00: the two near points dominate, the far one counts less.

Compare with attention (Module 7): \text{out}(q)=\sum_i\frac{\exp(q\cdot k_i/\sqrt d)}{\sum_j\exp(q\cdot k_j/\sqrt d)}\,v_i. It is a Nadaraya–Watson smoother with the kernel \exp(q\cdot k/\sqrt d) acting on the values v_i. And since e^{-\lVert q-k\rVert^2/2}=e^{q\cdot k}\,e^{-\lVert q\rVert^2/2}\,e^{-\lVert k\rVert^2/2}, the query factor cancels in the normalisation and the key factor is constant when keys have equal norm (as after normalisation layers): softmax attention is, up to those factors, Gaussian-kernel smoothing over keys.

⟨·,·⟩inner (dot) product

Common traps
  • Not every similarity is a kernel. It must produce PSD Gram matrices (the "−|x−x′|" example fails). Using a non-PSD "kernel" can make an SVM objective non-convex or a GP covariance invalid.
  • Kernels cost n² memory and about n³ time (the matrix inverse) in the number of data points, which is why exact GPs and kernel SVMs struggle past ~10⁵ points.
  • ℓ is a real modelling choice. Too small: memorises noise (overfits). Too large: oversmooths (underfits). Learn it by cross-validation or marginal likelihood.
  • GP uncertainty is conditional on the kernel. The band narrows because the model assumes smoothness; a wrong kernel gives confident wrong answers.
  • The kernel trick applies to algorithms that touch data only through dot products (SVM, ridge regression, PCA, k-means variants), not to every model.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Support vector machines with RBF or polynomial kernels dominated classification before deep learning; kernel ridge regression and kernel PCA use the same trick.
  • Gaussian processes power Bayesian optimisation (hyper-parameter tuning picks the next trial using the GP's mean and uncertainty), calibrated regression and surrogate models.
  • Attention is a kernel smoother (above). Linear attention and Performers approximate exp(q·k) by explicit random features φ(q)·φ(k) — the kernel trick run backwards to make attention cost O(n) instead of O(n²).
  • Neural tangent kernel: infinitely wide networks trained by gradient descent behave like kernel regression. MMD (maximum mean discrepancy), a kernel-based distance between distributions, is used to compare generated and real data.

Takeaways

  • A feature map can make hard problems linear; a kernel k(x,x′) = φ(x)·φ(x′) delivers the dot product without building φ.
  • Valid kernels are exactly those with positive semi-definite Gram matrices; RBF corresponds to infinitely many features.
  • A GP is a Gaussian prior over functions whose covariance is the kernel; conditioning gives a mean and an uncertainty everywhere.
  • Attention is a normalised kernel-weighted average of values.
8.4

Variational inference & VAEs

The questionWe want a model that generates faces. Give it a short code z (a point in, say, ℝ³²) and a neural-network decoder that turns z into an image. Training means maximising \log p(x) for real faces x, where p(x)=\int p(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz — an integral nobody can compute. Worse, to know "which code made this face?" we need the posterior p(z\mid x), which also needs that integral. How can we train anyway?

Intuition

When you cannot measure something exactly, you can often find a guaranteed lower bound you can measure and push it up. Imagine a floor beneath a hidden ceiling: you cannot see the ceiling's height (log p(x)), but you can measure the floor's, and raising the floor can only help. Our floor is the evidence lower bound (ELBO).

The second trick: since the true posterior is intractable, propose a simple distribution q(z|x) (a Gaussian whose mean and spread are produced by an encoder network) and tune it to resemble the posterior. The gap between the floor and the ceiling will turn out to be exactly "how different q is from the true posterior", measured by a KL divergence (Module 5). Making q better closes the gap.

Build it up

Step 1 — the setup. Three pieces: a prior p(z)=\mathcal N(0,I); a decoder (likelihood) p_\theta(x\mid z) with network weights θ; an encoder q_\phi(z\mid x)=\mathcal N\big(\mu_\phi(x),\operatorname{diag}\sigma_\phi^2(x)\big) with weights φ.

Step 2 — deriving the ELBO with Jensen's inequality. Start from the quantity we want and multiply and divide by q inside the integral (that changes nothing):

\log p(x)=\log\!\int p(x,z)\,dz=\log\!\int q(z\mid x)\,\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\,dz=\log\mathbb E_{z\sim q}\!\left[\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right]

We turned an integral into an expectation under q. Now Jensen's inequality: for a concave function like log, \log\mathbb E[Y]\ge\mathbb E[\log Y] ("the log of the average is at least the average of the logs"). Sanity check: Y is 1 or 9 with equal chance: \log\mathbb E[Y]=\log5=1.609, \mathbb E[\log Y]=\tfrac12(0+2.197)=1.099 ✓. Applying it:

\log p(x)\;\ge\;\mathbb E_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right]=\mathbb E_q[\log p(x\mid z)]+\mathbb E_q\!\left[\log\frac{p(z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right]
\mathrm{ELBO}=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q}[\log p(x\mid z)]}_{\text{reconstruction}}-\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\,\Vert\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regulariser}}

(We split p(x,z)=p(x\mid z)p(z) and recognised \mathbb E_q[\log q/p] as a KL.) The right side is the ELBO. Two forces: reconstruction — codes sampled from q should decode back to x — and regularisation — q must not stray far from the prior, so that sampling z from the prior at generation time gives sensible codes.

Step 3 — the exact size of the gap. Jensen only gave an inequality; here is the identity behind it. Expand the KL between q and the true posterior, using p(z\mid x)=p(x,z)/p(x):

\mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\Vert\,p(z\mid x)\big)=\mathbb E_q\big[\log q-\log p(x,z)+\log p(x)\big]=\log p(x)-\mathrm{ELBO}
\Longrightarrow\quad\log p(x)=\mathrm{ELBO}+\mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\Vert\,p(z\mid x)\big)

The KL is never negative, so ELBO ≤ log p(x) again, and the gap is exactly the KL from q to the true posterior. Training φ (the encoder) shrinks the gap; training θ (the decoder) pushes up the evidence. One objective does both.

Step 4 — the KL between Gaussians in closed form. For one latent dimension, q = N(μ,σ²) and p = N(0,1). Use \mathrm{KL}=\mathbb E_q[\log q-\log p]. We have \log q=-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\tfrac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} whose expectation under q is -\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\tfrac12 (since \mathbb E(z-\mu)^2=\sigma^2), and \log p=-\tfrac12\ln2\pi-\tfrac{z^2}2 whose expectation is -\tfrac12\ln2\pi-\tfrac12(\mu^2+\sigma^2) (since \mathbb E z^2=\mu^2+\sigma^2). Subtract:

\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\,\Vert\,\mathcal N(0,1)\big)=\tfrac12\left(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\ln\sigma^2\right)

For a d-dimensional diagonal Gaussian, add this over the d coordinates. It is zero exactly when μ=0 and σ=1 and grows as q drifts or narrows. With μ=1, σ=0.5: \tfrac12(1+0.25-1-\ln0.25)=\tfrac12(0.25+1.386)=0.818.

Step 5 — the reparameterisation trick. To train we need gradients of \mathbb E_{z\sim q_\phi}[f(z)] with respect to φ. But "draw z from q_φ" is a random black box: backpropagation cannot pass through a random draw. The trick is to move the randomness out of the way. Draw noise \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I) (which has no parameters) and compute

z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\varepsilon

This z has exactly the right distribution, but it is now an ordinary differentiable function of μ and σ, with the noise as just another input. Gradients flow: \partial z/\partial\mu=1, \partial z/\partial\sigma=\varepsilon.

Worked example

μ=1, σ=0.5, drawn ε=0.8 → z = 1 + 0.5·0.8 = 1.4. (With ε = −1.2 we'd get z = 0.4.) Suppose the downstream loss is L=(z-2)^2. By the chain rule: \partial L/\partial z=2(z-2)=-1.2, so \partial L/\partial\mu=-1.2\cdot1=-1.2 and \partial L/\partial\sigma=-1.2\cdot\varepsilon=-0.96. Both say "increase μ and σ to move z toward 2". Averaging such gradients over a few ε draws estimates the true gradient.

Step 6 — a full ELBO by hand. Make the decoder a simple Gaussian, p(x\mid z)=\mathcal N(x;dz,s^2). Then \mathbb E_q[\log p(x\mid z)]=-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi s^2)-\frac{(x-d\mu)^2+d^2\sigma^2}{2s^2}. Because everything is Gaussian, we can even compute the true evidence: p(x)=\mathcal N(0,d^2+s^2) and the true posterior is Gaussian with variance (1+d^2/s^2)^{-1}.

Worked example (the numbers in the lab's defaults)

x=1.5, d=1, s=0.5, encoder μ=1, σ=0.5.

Reconstruction: -\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\cdot0.25)-\frac{0.5^2+0.25}{2\cdot0.25}=-0.226-1.000=-1.226.

KL (from Step 4): 0.818. So ELBO = −1.226 − 0.818 = −2.044.

True evidence: \log\mathcal N(1.5;0,1.25)=-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\cdot1.25)-\frac{2.25}{2.5}=-1.031-0.9=-1.931. Gap = −1.931 − (−2.044) = 0.113.

The true posterior is N(1.2, 0.2) (precision 1+1/0.25=5, mean (1.5/0.25)/5=1.2). Its KL from q = N(1, 0.25) is \ln\frac{\sqrt{0.2}}{0.5}+\frac{0.25+(1-1.2)^2}{2\cdot0.2}-\frac12=-0.112+0.725-0.5=0.113 ✓ — the gap really is KL(q‖posterior). Setting q to N(1.2, 0.2) would make the gap 0.

Step 7 — β-VAE. Multiply the KL term by a weight β: \mathbb E_q[\log p(x\mid z)]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}. With β>1 the encoder is pushed harder toward the prior, which tends to make latent dimensions more independent and "disentangled" but reconstructions blurrier; β<1 gives sharper reconstructions but a messier latent space that is harder to sample from. (β is also annealed from 0 upward in practice to avoid posterior collapse, where q ignores x and equals the prior.)

The formal definition

\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x)=\mathbb E_{\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)}\Big[\log p_\theta\big(x\mid \mu_\phi+\sigma_\phi\odot\varepsilon\big)\Big]-\beta\cdot\tfrac12\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\ln\sigma_j^2\big)\;\le\;\log p_\theta(x)
𝓛the (β-)ELBO for one data point x; training maximises its average over the data
pθ(x | z)decoder: probability of the data given the code (θ = decoder weights)
qφ(z | x)encoder: approximate posterior (φ = encoder weights)
μφ, σφmean and standard deviation vectors output by the encoder

Common traps
  • The ELBO is a bound, not log p(x). A rising ELBO may come from a better decoder, a better encoder, or both; the gap is invisible unless the posterior is known.
  • KL is not symmetric. VAEs minimise KL(q‖p), which punishes q for putting mass where p has little; the result is "mode-seeking" and q tends to be too narrow. KL(p‖q) behaves differently.
  • Posterior collapse. A powerful decoder can ignore z entirely; the KL term goes to 0 and the latent code carries no information. Remedies: KL annealing, "free bits", weaker decoders.
  • At generation time sample from the prior p(z), not from the encoder. If q strayed from the prior, samples fall in regions the decoder never trained on.
  • Networks should output \ln\sigma^2 (any real number), not σ, so the variance stays positive.
Where it shows up in AI
  • VAEs for images, molecules (smooth latent space for drug search), anomaly detection (high reconstruction error or low ELBO) and representation learning.
  • Latent diffusion / Stable Diffusion first compresses images with a VAE-style autoencoder, then runs diffusion in the small latent space — cheaper by orders of magnitude (next lesson). VQ-VAE uses discrete latents for image and audio tokens.
  • Variational inference generally: Bayesian neural networks ("Bayes by backprop"), topic models, and world models in reinforcement learning (Dreamer) all optimise an ELBO.
  • The diffusion training objective is itself an ELBO over a chain of latent variables, and the reparameterisation z = μ + σε is the same algebra as the noising formula x_t = √ᾱ x₀ + √(1−ᾱ) ε.

Takeaways

  • ELBO = E[log p(x|z)] − KL(q‖p(z)) ≤ log p(x), derived with Jensen's inequality.
  • The gap is exactly KL(q‖true posterior): a better encoder tightens the bound.
  • For Gaussians the KL is closed-form: ½Σ(μ² + σ² − 1 − ln σ²).
  • Reparameterisation z = μ + σε lets gradients flow through sampling; β trades reconstruction against regularity.
8.5

Diffusion & score matching

The questionDestroying a picture is easy: keep adding a little random static until nothing is left but noise. Creating a picture is hard. But what if we could learn to undo the destruction one tiny step at a time — and then start from pure noise and walk backwards into a brand-new picture? What exactly must a network learn for that, and how do we train it?

Intuition

Drop a spot of ink in a glass of water. It spreads until the water is uniformly grey. Run the film backwards and the ink gathers into a spot. The forward film (diffusion) is trivial; for the backward one each water molecule would need to know which way to move. But over a very short time the backward motion is simple: each particle drifts a little toward where the ink used to be thicker. So if a network can answer, at every noise level, "in which direction does the density of plausible data increase from here?", we can run the film backwards.

That direction field is the score: the gradient of the log-density, \nabla_x\log p(x) — an arrow at every point pointing uphill toward more probable data. For a hill-shaped density, the arrows point toward the peak and are longer where the hill is steeper. Because the arrow is a gradient of a log, the unknown normalising constant of p vanishes: \nabla\log\frac{f(x)}{Z}=\nabla\log f(x). That sidesteps the intractable integrals of the last two lessons.

Build it up

Step 1 — the forward (noising) process. Choose small noise levels \beta_1,\dots,\beta_T (the schedule), and let \alpha_t=1-\beta_t. Each step shrinks the signal slightly and adds a little Gaussian noise: x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_t, with \varepsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I). This is a Markov chain (Lesson 8.1) with Gaussian steps. The shrink factor \sqrt{\alpha_t} is chosen so the total variance stays about 1 instead of exploding.

Step 2 — jump straight to any t. Apply the step twice and collect terms:

x_2=\sqrt{\alpha_2}\big(\sqrt{\alpha_1}x_0+\sqrt{1-\alpha_1}\,\varepsilon_1\big)+\sqrt{1-\alpha_2}\,\varepsilon_2=\sqrt{\alpha_1\alpha_2}\,x_0+\underbrace{\sqrt{\alpha_2(1-\alpha_1)}\,\varepsilon_1+\sqrt{1-\alpha_2}\,\varepsilon_2}_{\text{two independent Gaussians}}

Independent Gaussians add by adding variances (Module 3): \alpha_2(1-\alpha_1)+(1-\alpha_2)=1-\alpha_1\alpha_2, so the sum is a single Gaussian \sqrt{1-\alpha_1\alpha_2}\,\varepsilon. Repeating the argument (induction) gives the key formula, with \bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^t\alpha_s:

x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\;x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)

No loop over t is needed: given a clean x₀ we can create its noisy version at any t in one line. The two weights satisfy (\sqrt{\bar\alpha})^2+(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha})^2=1 — a point on the unit circle, from signal (t=0) to noise.

Worked example

β₁=0.1, β₂=0.2, so α=(0.9, 0.8) and ᾱ₂ = 0.9·0.8 = 0.72. Then x_2=\sqrt{0.72}\,x_0+\sqrt{0.28}\,\varepsilon=0.8485\,x_0+0.5292\,\varepsilon. The noise variance, step by step: α₂β₁ + β₂ = 0.8·0.1 + 0.2 = 0.28 ✓ (= 1 − 0.72).

For x₀ = 2 and ε = 1: x₂ = 0.8485·2 + 0.5292 = 1.697 + 0.529 = 2.226.

A real schedule: β rises linearly from 10⁻⁴ to 0.02 over T = 1000 steps. Then ᾱ_T = 4.0×10⁻⁵, so \sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}=0.0064: the signal is gone and x_T is essentially pure N(0,I) noise — a distribution we can sample from trivially.

  2. In the calculator, set t=0: x_t = x₀. Set t=1000: x_t is almost entirely ε. Find t where signal and noise weights are equal (both 0.707): that is the halfway point of destruction (t ≈ 259 for this schedule).

Step 3 — the score of a noisy distribution. If the clean data have density p_0, the noisy variable x_t has some density p_t. For a Gaussian \mathcal N(m,v) the score is simply -(x-m)/v: an arrow pointing at the mean, growing with distance and shrinking when v is large.

Worked example

Suppose 1-D data are x_0\sim\mathcal N(2,\,0.25) and ᾱ=0.5. Then x_t=\sqrt{0.5}x_0+\sqrt{0.5}\,\varepsilon is Gaussian with mean \sqrt{0.5}\cdot2=1.414 and variance 0.5\cdot0.25+0.5=0.625.

Score at x = 2: -(2-1.414)/0.625=-0.937: the density is larger to the left, so the arrow points left, toward 1.414.

Step 4 — learning the score = learning to predict the noise. We cannot compute \nabla\log p_t directly (we don't know p_t), but one conditional score is easy. Given the clean x₀, x_t\mid x_0\sim\mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,x_0,\,(1-\bar\alpha)I), so

\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid x_0)=-\frac{x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}

(since x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha}x_0=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\varepsilon). That is the score given the clean image — the added noise, reversed and rescaled. The marginal score is its average over the clean images that could have produced x_t:

\nabla\log p_t(x_t)=\frac{\nabla p_t(x_t)}{p_t(x_t)}=\frac{\int p_0(x_0)\,\nabla p(x_t\mid x_0)\,dx_0}{p_t(x_t)}=\mathbb E_{x_0\mid x_t}\!\big[\nabla\log p(x_t\mid x_0)\big]=-\frac{\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid x_t]}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}

(we used \nabla p=p\nabla\log p). Now recall a fact from Module 6: the function that minimises squared error \mathbb E\|\varepsilon-g(x_t)\|^2 is the conditional mean g(x_t)=\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid x_t]. So a network trained to predict the noise that was added is automatically learning the score:

\mathcal L=\mathbb E_{x_0,\,t,\,\varepsilon}\Big\|\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\;t\big)\Big\|^2,\qquad s_\theta(x_t,t)=-\frac{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}
Worked example

From the previous example the true score at x=2 is −0.937 with ᾱ=0.5, so the ideal noise prediction is \hat\varepsilon=-s\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}=0.937\cdot0.7071=0.663. A perfectly trained ε_θ outputs 0.663 there; its score is −0.663/0.7071 = −0.937 ✓.

Training in four lines: (1) pick a data point x₀ and a random t; (2) draw ε ~ N(0,I); (3) form x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\varepsilon; (4) take a gradient step on \|\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\|^2. It is ordinary regression (Module 6) — no adversary, no intractable integral.

Step 5 — the reverse process. To run the film backwards we need p(x_{t-1}\mid x_t). For tiny β this reverse step is also (approximately) a Gaussian whose mean nudges x_t up the score: x_{t-1}\approx\frac1{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\big(x_t+\beta_t\,s(x_t,t)\big)+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,z. Substituting s=-\varepsilon_\theta/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t} gives the DDPM sampling step:

x_{t-1}=\frac1{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right)+\sqrt{\beta_t}\;z,\qquad z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\ (z=0\text{ at the last step})
Worked example — one reverse step

At some step: β_t = 0.1 so α_t = 0.9, ᾱ_t = 0.5, current x_t = 1.0, and the network predicts ε_θ = 0.4.

Correction: \frac{0.1}{\sqrt{0.5}}\cdot0.4=0.1414\cdot0.4=0.0566. Mean: \frac{1-0.0566}{\sqrt{0.9}}=\frac{0.9434}{0.9487}=0.9945.

Add noise \sqrt{0.1}\,z=0.3162z; for a draw z = −0.5: x_{t−1} = 0.9945 − 0.158 = 0.836.

Sampling algorithm. Draw x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I). For t = T, T−1, …, 1: predict ε_θ(x_t,t), apply the step above (adding the noise z except at t=1). The result x₀ is a new sample. The fresh noise z is not decoration: it keeps the walker from collapsing onto a single mode, and it is what the reverse-time mathematics requires.

Step 6 — the continuous-time picture (SDEs, at intuition level). Make the steps infinitely small. The noising chain becomes a stochastic differential equation: dx=-\tfrac12\beta(t)\,x\,dt+\sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dw — the first term shrinks x (a spring toward 0), the second kicks it randomly (dw is a tiny Gaussian kick of variance dt). A theorem of Anderson (1982) states that the reverse process is also an SDE and the only ingredient that is new is the score:

dx=\Big[-\tfrac12\beta(t)\,x-\beta(t)\,\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\Big]dt+\sqrt{\beta(t)}\,d\bar w

DDPM is simply a discretisation of this equation (the 1/√α ≈ 1 + β/2 factor is the spring term; βs is the score drift). There is even a noise-free twin, the "probability-flow ODE" with the same marginal distributions at every t, used by fast samplers such as DDIM. This viewpoint unifies "score matching", "denoising diffusion" and later "flow matching".

Lab: noise a shape, then denoise it

Try this
  1. Pick a shape and press "Noise it". Over about 14 s the points diffuse into a round Gaussian cloud. Pause and drag the t slider to scrub: the readout shows the signal and noise weights from the formula.
  2. Switch on the purple score arrows and scrub t. At small t the arrows pull sharply onto the curve; at large t they all point gently toward the centre. Large t means a blurry, almost Gaussian density.
  3. Press "Generate (denoise)": fresh random noise is turned into the shape step by step, each step following the arrows plus a little added noise. Slow the speed further to follow individual steps.
  4. Turn off "inject fresh noise" and generate again. The points follow the score only and tend to collapse onto fewer, sharper spots — fresh noise is what preserves diversity. (Also note: with an exact score of a finite sample the process reproduces training points; a trained network smooths between them, which is how it generalises.)
Common traps
  • The score is a gradient with respect to x, not θ. It is a vector field on data space ("which way is more probable?"), unrelated to the loss gradient used to train the network.
  • ᾱ_T must really reach ≈ 0. If a schedule leaves residual signal at t=T, sampling from pure N(0,I) mismatches training (a known flaw of some early schedules; fixed by "zero terminal SNR" schedules).
  • Predicting ε, predicting x₀ and predicting the score are the same information up to known scalings (x_0=(x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha}); they differ in numerical behaviour, not in what is being learned.
  • Do not skip the injected noise z in DDPM sampling (except at the final step), and note that deterministic samplers (DDIM / ODE) are a different, correct algorithm — not the same one with noise turned off.
  • Many steps are slow. T=1000 network evaluations per image motivated faster samplers, distillation and latent-space diffusion.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Image and video generation: DALL·E 2, Imagen, Stable Diffusion, Midjourney-class systems and video models are diffusion models; a U-Net or transformer plays ε_θ. Latent diffusion runs the process in the compressed space of a VAE (Lesson 8.4).
  • Classifier-free guidance combines two noise predictions, \hat\varepsilon=\varepsilon_\emptyset+w(\varepsilon_c-\varepsilon_\emptyset) — adding a multiple of "score with the text prompt minus score without it" to push samples toward the prompt.
  • Audio, molecules and proteins: speech/music synthesis, protein-structure design (RFdiffusion), and molecular conformation generators are all score-based.
  • Robotics: "diffusion policies" generate action sequences by denoising; inpainting and editing work by constraining the reverse process.
  • Theory links: the training loss is a weighted ELBO (Lesson 8.4), the forward process is a Markov chain (8.1), and Langevin sampling (a score-driven random walk) is an MCMC method.

Check yourself

1. A 3-step schedule has β = (0.1, 0.2, 0.5). What are ᾱ₃ and the weights on x₀ and ε in x₃?
α = (0.9, 0.8, 0.5), so ᾱ₃ = 0.9·0.8·0.5 = 0.36. Signal weight √0.36 = 0.6; noise weight √(1 − 0.36) = √0.64 = 0.8. (0.6² + 0.8² = 1 ✓.)
2. The noised data at some t is N(0, 4). What is the score at x = 2?
Score = −(x − m)/v = −(2 − 0)/4 = −0.5: an arrow of length 0.5 pointing toward the mean (to the left).
3. At ᾱ = 0.75 the network outputs ε_θ = 1.0. What score does that correspond to, and why does training to predict noise teach the score?
s = −ε_θ/√(1−ᾱ) = −1.0/0.5 = −2. The conditional score of x_t given x₀ is −ε/√(1−ᾱ); the best squared-error predictor of ε given x_t is E[ε|x_t], and the marginal score equals the conditional score averaged over x₀ given x_t, i.e. −E[ε|x_t]/√(1−ᾱ). So the noise predictor and the score differ only by the known factor −1/√(1−ᾱ).

Takeaways

  • Forward noising has a closed form: x_t = √ᾱ_t x₀ + √(1−ᾱ_t) ε, a Markov chain that ends in pure Gaussian noise.
  • The score ∇log p_t points toward higher density and ignores the normaliser; predicting the added noise is equivalent to learning the score (s = −ε_θ/√(1−ᾱ)).
  • Generation runs the chain backwards: each DDPM step moves x up the score and injects a little noise; in the continuous limit this is a reverse-time SDE.
  • Training is plain regression on noise, which is why diffusion models scale so well.
