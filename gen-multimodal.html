9.1

Multimodal models: images and audio as tokens, and contrastive learning

The questionA language model reads a sequence of token vectors. A photograph is a grid of pixel numbers. How can the model "read" the photograph, and how can we train a pair of encoders so that a picture of a dog and the sentence "a dog on grass" end up close together in the same vector space, while a picture of a boat does not?

Intuition: everything becomes a row of vectors

A Transformer does not care what its inputs mean. It needs a sequence of vectors of one fixed length d. Text already is such a sequence once words are turned into token embeddings. To feed a picture, we chop it into small square patches and turn each patch into one vector. Now the picture is also "a sequence of tokens", and the same attention machinery can mix it with words. Sound works the same way once it is cut into short frames. Two ideas follow, and most multimodal systems use one or both.

  • The adapter idea. Use an image encoder to produce patch vectors, pass them through a small projection so they have the language model's width d, and place them in the text sequence like special words: "[image tokens] Describe this picture." The language model then answers in text.
  • The contrastive idea. Use two separate encoders, one for images and one for text, each ending in a vector of the same size. Train them so that matching pairs point the same way and mismatched pairs do not. The result is a shared "meaning space" where a sentence can be compared with a picture by a single cosine.

This lesson sits at the start of the pipeline's last stages: the contrastive encoders are pre-trained first, and a vision-language assistant is built by connecting such an encoder to a language model that has already been pre-trained and aligned (CLIP and multimodal models in the vision track show the encoder side).

Build it up

Step 1, counting image tokens. A square image of 224 × 224 pixels is cut into patches of 16 × 16 pixels.

Worked example, patches as tokens

Patches per side: 224/16=14, so the image becomes 14\times14=196 patch tokens. Each patch holds 16\times16\times3=768 numbers (three colour channels), and a learned linear map turns those 768 numbers into a vector of width d.

Put the 196 image tokens in front of a 20-token question and the language model sees a sequence of 196+20=216 tokens. Because attention cost grows with the square of the length, the picture costs about 196/20\approx10 times more tokens than the question; this is why systems often shrink or pool image tokens before the language model.

Step 2, the contrastive goal. Take a small batch of N=3 image-text pairs: (image 1, text 1), (image 2, text 2), (image 3, text 3). Encode every image into a unit vector u_i and every text into a unit vector v_j. The cosine of the angle between them, s_{ij}=u_i\cdot v_j, says how well image i matches text j. Arrange all nine cosines in a matrix. The three true pairs are the diagonal; we want the diagonal to beat the six wrong cells in every row and every column.

text 1text 2text 3
image 10.90.20.1
image 20.30.80.2
image 30.10.40.7

Turn each row into a three-way multiple-choice question: "given image i, which of the three texts is its caption?" The model's answer is a softmax over the row of cosines divided by a temperature \tau. A small \tau sharpens the choice. The loss is the usual negative log probability of the right answer.

Worked example, symmetric InfoNCE with τ = 0.5

Row 1 of the matrix divided by 0.5 is (1.8, 0.4, 0.2). Exponentials: e^{1.8}=6.0496,\ e^{0.4}=1.4918,\ e^{0.2}=1.2214, sum 8.7629. The probability of the right text is 6.0496/8.7629=0.6904, so the row loss is -\ln0.6904=0.3705.

Row 2 gives (0.6, 1.6, 0.4)→ probabilities with the right one 0.5991 and loss 0.5123. Row 3 gives loss 0.6152. The image-to-text loss is their mean: (0.3705+0.5123+0.6152)/3=0.4993.

Now do the same for the columns ("given text j, which image?"): losses 0.4075, 0.5599, 0.5123, mean 0.4932. The symmetric loss is the average of the two directions: (0.4993+0.4932)/2=0.4963.

The temperature matters: with \tau=1 the same matrix gives 0.7532, and with \tau=0.2 it gives 0.1287, because dividing by a smaller \tau widens the gaps between cosines before the softmax.

Step 3, what this has to do with mutual information. If the matching pairs really share information, then knowing the image should make the right text easier to pick out among decoys. The InfoNCE loss measures exactly how well that multiple-choice question is answered, and it gives a lower bound on the mutual information I(X;Y) between images and texts (mutual information): the better the model picks the right partner, the more information it must be capturing.

\mathcal L_{i\to t}=-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\frac{\exp(s_{ii}/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(s_{ij}/\tau)},\qquad \mathcal L=\tfrac12\bigl(\mathcal L_{i\to t}+\mathcal L_{t\to i}\bigr),\qquad I(X;Y)\ \ge\ \ln N-\mathcal L
Symbols. N: pairs in the batch (here 3). s_{ij}=u_i\cdot v_j: cosine between image i and text j (unit vectors). \tau: the temperature, a positive number that scales the cosines before the softmax. \mathcal L_{i\to t}: the row-wise loss, image chooses text. \mathcal L_{t\to i}: the same over columns, text chooses image. \mathcal L: the symmetric loss. I(X;Y): mutual information in nats. The inequality holds when the wrong candidates in each row are drawn independently from the marginal distribution; with a learned model and real batches it is a guide, not an exact measurement.
Worked example, the bound

\ln3=1.0986. With \mathcal L=0.4963 the bound says I\ge1.0986-0.4963=0.6023 nats. Two facts follow. If the model cannot tell the pairs apart (all nine cosines equal), every row is uniform, \mathcal L=\ln3 and the bound is 0: no information captured. And the bound can never exceed \ln N, so a batch of 3 can certify at most 1.0986 nats; this is one reason contrastive training uses very large batches.

Step 4, from encoders to assistants. After contrastive pre-training the image encoder produces features that already line up with language. A vision-language assistant commonly keeps such an encoder, adds a small trained adapter that maps its patch features into the language model's token space, and then fine-tunes on image-question-answer examples. Which parts are frozen and which are trained differs between designs.

  2. Drag τ from 0.5 down to 0.2 and up to 1. The loss falls and rises as in the worked example. Then use the diagonal slider to subtract 0.5: what happens to the lower bound on mutual information?

Common traps
  • Treating the bound as the mutual information. It is a lower bound that saturates at \ln N. A small batch cannot certify a large amount of shared information.
  • Forgetting both directions. The loss is symmetric on purpose: images should find their captions and captions should find their images.
  • Thinking a high similarity proves understanding. The encoder learns what separates the batch's captions, which can rely on shortcuts such as a visible word in the image.
  • "The model sees the image". It sees a sequence of patch vectors produced by an encoder; detail lost in the encoder or by pooling cannot be recovered by the language model.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Contrastive image-text pre-training (the CLIP family) yields encoders used for zero-shot classification, retrieval, and as the image side of many vision-language assistants.
  • Adapters and image tokens: multimodal assistants commonly place projected patch vectors in the language model's input sequence; audio inputs are handled analogously with frame embeddings.
  • Text-to-image conditioning: text embeddings from such encoders guide diffusion models (see the vision track's generative models).

Check yourself

1. For the printed matrix at τ = 0.5, what is the loss of row 2?
Row 2 divided by 0.5 is (0.6, 1.6, 0.4); exponentials 1.8221, 4.9530, 1.4918, sum 8.2669. The right probability is 4.9530/8.2669 = 0.5991 and the loss is −ln 0.5991 = 0.5123.
2. What is the largest mutual information a batch of 256 pairs can certify with InfoNCE?
\ln256=5.545 nats, reached only when the loss is 0. Larger batches raise the ceiling.
3. All nine cosines are equal. What are the loss and the bound for N = 3?
Every softmax is uniform, so every row and column loss is \ln3=1.0986, the symmetric loss is 1.0986 and the bound is 1.0986-1.0986=0.

Takeaways

  • Images become patch tokens (224 px with 16 px patches gives 196 tokens) and audio becomes frame tokens; then attention treats them like words.
  • Contrastive pre-training turns each row and column of a similarity matrix into a classification, and the symmetric InfoNCE loss averages both directions.
  • \ln N-\mathcal L lower-bounds mutual information and cannot exceed \ln N.
9.2

Video and audio generation

The questionA four-second clip at 8 frames per second is 32 images. A diffusion model that treated every pixel of every frame as a token and let all of them attend to all of them would need astronomically many attention pairs, and sound is even longer: 24,000 numbers every second. How do video and audio generators shrink the problem, and why do frames still flicker unless they are tied together?

Intuition: compress first, then generate in the small space

Both ideas of earlier modules return here. As in latent diffusion, an autoencoder squeezes the raw signal into a much smaller latent array, and the generator works there. For video the compression runs along space and time. For audio a neural codec turns the waveform into a short sequence of discrete codes, so that generating sound looks like generating text: predict the next code. The remaining difficulty is time: every frame must agree with its neighbours about where objects are.

The details of real systems differ and change quickly. This lesson teaches the counting and the principles, with round numbers marked illustrative.

Build it up

Step 1, counting spatiotemporal tokens.

Worked example, a latent video

Start with 4 s at 8 fps: 32 frames of 256 × 256 pixels. An autoencoder reduces each spatial side by 8: 256/8=32, a 32 × 32 latent grid. Group the grid into 2 × 2 patches: 32/2=16 per side, 16\times16=256 tokens per latent frame. Compress time by 4: 32/4=8 latent frames. The video is N=8\times256=2048 tokens.

Full attention over all of them costs N^2=4{,}194{,}304 pairs per layer. A cheaper pattern alternates two kinds of attention: spatial (each token sees the 256 tokens of its own frame) and temporal (each token sees the same grid position in all 8 frames). The pair count is T\,S^2+S\,T^2=8\cdot256^2+256\cdot8^2=524{,}288+16{,}384=540{,}672, about 7.8 times fewer, and information still travels everywhere in two hops.

N=T\cdot S,\qquad \text{pairs}_{\text{full}}=N^2,\qquad \text{pairs}_{\text{factorised}}=T\,S^2+S\,T^2
Symbols. T: number of latent frames (after temporal compression). S: tokens per latent frame (after spatial compression and patching). N: total tokens. "Pairs" counts query-key pairs in one attention layer, the quantity the memory and compute of attention grow with.

Step 2, audio as discrete codes. Raw audio at 24,000 samples per second, cut by a codec that outputs one code vector every 320 samples, gives 24000/320=75 frames per second. Each frame is described not by one code but by several, from residual vector quantisation: pick the nearest entry in a first codebook, then quantise the leftover error with a second codebook, and so on. Here is the idea on one number.

Worked example, residual quantisation of the value 0.73

Codebook 1 holds {0, 0.5, 1}. The nearest entry to 0.73 is 0.5; the residual is 0.73-0.5=0.23. Codebook 2 holds {−0.25, 0, 0.25}; the nearest to 0.23 is 0.25 and the new residual is 0.23-0.25=-0.02. Codebook 3 holds {−0.03, 0, 0.03}; the nearest to −0.02 is −0.03 (distance 0.01), leaving -0.02-(-0.03)=0.01. Reconstruction after 1, 2 and 3 codebooks: 0.50, 0.75, 0.72, with errors 0.23, 0.02, 0.01. Each extra codebook refines the previous answer.

With 4 codebooks the codec emits 75\times4=300 tokens per second, so 10 s of sound is 3000 tokens instead of 240{,}000 samples, an 80-fold shorter sequence that a language-model-style generator can handle. (Round illustrative numbers.)

Step 3, consistency over time. Suppose each frame, generated on its own, has a small random error in where it puts a ball. Three frames with independent errors of +0.08, −0.08, +0.08 (box width 1) make the ball jump 0.16 between frames: it flickers, even though each frame is individually only slightly wrong. Three frames whose errors are coupled, +0.08, +0.07, +0.06, are just as wrong on average yet differ by only 0.01 between frames: the motion looks smooth. A simple model of coupling is an autoregressive error

e_t=\rho\,e_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\;\xi_t,\qquad \xi_t\sim\mathcal N(0,1)
Symbols. e_t: the error in frame t. \rho\in[0,1): how strongly this frame's error repeats the previous one (0 = independent, near 1 = nearly identical). \xi_t: fresh random noise. The square-root factor keeps the typical size of e_t equal to 1 for every \rho, so changing \rho changes only the smoothness, not the error size.

Real video generators obtain this coupling in several ways: temporal attention lets each frame see its neighbours while it is being denoised; frames share initial noise or conditioning; and some systems generate a few key frames first and then fill in between them. Each choice trades computation, length of clip and controllability.

Common traps
  • Quality per frame is not quality of the clip. Sharp individual frames can still flicker, drift in object shape, or break physical plausibility. Check motion, not only stills.
  • Token counts are not durations. More compression makes sequences shorter but removes detail; fine fast motion and speech sounds suffer first.
  • Treating the numbers as a particular system. Frame rates, compression ratios and codebook counts here are illustrative; real systems choose their own.
  • Audio is not "just text". Codes at the first codebook carry coarse content and later ones fine detail; a generator must predict all of them consistently.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Latent video diffusion and spatiotemporal Transformers: a latent array of T\times S tokens denoised with spatial and temporal attention.
  • Neural audio codecs with token language models: speech and music generated as sequences of residual-quantised codes, then decoded to a waveform.
  • Evaluation: video and audio need temporal metrics and human review; the next lesson explains why scores for any generator must be read with their uncertainty.

Check yourself

1. With temporal compression 2 instead of 4, how many video tokens and full-attention pairs are there (other settings as in the worked example)?
T=32/2=16, N=16\times256=4096, and N^2=16{,}777{,}216.
2. How many audio tokens does a 6-second clip need with 8 codebooks at 75 frames per second?
75\times8\times6=3600.
3. Why do independently generated frames flicker even when each is almost correct?
Small errors that are independent from frame to frame point in random directions, so the difference between neighbouring frames is about as large as the errors themselves (for example +0.08 then −0.08 is a jump of 0.16). Coupling the errors, or generating frames together with temporal attention, makes neighbours wrong in the same way, so their difference is small.

Takeaways

  • Compress first: video becomes T\times S latent tokens, audio becomes codec codes at a few hundred tokens per second.
  • Full attention costs N^2; alternating spatial and temporal attention costs TS^2+ST^2.
  • Consistency over time needs frames to share information; independent per-frame errors show up as flicker.
9.3

Evaluating generators

The questionModel A beat model B in 13 of 20 side-by-side comparisons. Does that mean A is better? Another model scores 0.68 on a public benchmark; can you trust the number? And how do you score a generator of images when there is no single right answer?

Intuition: every score is a noisy measurement of something narrower than you want

Evaluating a generator is a measurement problem. A score has three weaknesses to check, each treated below: does it measure the right thing (contamination, judge bias, feature choice), how much would it change on another sample (an interval), and how is it aggregated (ratings from pairwise outcomes). We take them in turn on tiny tables.

Build it up

Step 1, perplexity: the intrinsic score for a language model. Perplexity is the exponential of the average negative log probability the model gives to the true next tokens. It is "the effective number of equally likely choices per token". It needs no reference answers, but it only compares models with the same tokeniser on the same text.

Worked example, perplexity of four tokens

The model gave the true tokens probabilities 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.5. Negative logs: 0.6931, 1.3863, 2.0794, 0.6931; mean 1.2130; perplexity e^{1.2130}=3.3636. Check: it equals the geometric mean of the inverse probabilities, (2\cdot4\cdot8\cdot2)^{1/4}=128^{1/4}=3.3636.

\mathrm{PPL}=\exp\Bigl(-\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\ln p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})\Bigr)
Symbols. n: tokens in the text. x_t: the true token at position t. x_{<t}: the tokens before it. p_\theta: the model's probability. Lower perplexity means the text was less surprising to the model (cross-entropy, here).

Step 2, benchmarks and contamination. A benchmark is a fixed set of questions with known answers. If some of those questions (or their answers) appear in the training data, a model can answer from memory, and the score no longer measures the ability the benchmark is meant to test: this is contamination (also called leakage).

Worked example, an inflated score

A test set has 100 questions. On genuinely new questions a model is right 60% of the time. Suppose 20 of the 100 questions leaked into training and the model answers all of those from memory. The reported score is 0.20\times1+0.80\times0.60=0.20+0.48=0.68, eight points above its real ability of 0.60. With only 10 leaked questions the score is 0.10+0.90\times0.60=0.64. The reader of "0.68" cannot see the difference.

\text{reported}=c\,m+(1-c)\,b
Symbols. c: fraction of test items that leaked. m: accuracy on leaked items (memorisation; 1 if every leaked item is answered from memory). b: true accuracy on unseen items. The inflation is c\,(m-b).

Defences are practical rather than perfect: check for overlap between test and training text, keep part of the test set private, refresh tests with new items, and report performance on freshly written questions next to the public score.

Step 3, human preference and Elo ratings. For open-ended outputs, people are asked which of two answers they prefer. Individual outcomes are noisy, so they are combined into a rating. In the Elo system every model has a rating R; the expected score of A against B is a logistic function of the rating difference, and after each game ratings move toward the result.

E_A=\frac{1}{1+10^{(R_B-R_A)/400}},\qquad R_A\leftarrow R_A+K\,(S_A-E_A),\qquad R_B\leftarrow R_B-K\,(S_A-E_A)
Symbols. R_A,R_B: current ratings. E_A: A's expected score (probability of winning) against B; equal ratings give 0.5. S_A: the actual result, 1 if A won, 0 if A lost. K: the step size; larger K reacts faster and is noisier. The 400 sets the scale: a 400-point gap means expected score 1/(1+10^{-1})=0.909.

Step 4, an interval by the bootstrap. Twenty judges compared model A with model B; the outcomes are, with 1 = A preferred:

1 1 0 1 1 1 0 1 1 0 1 1 0 1 1 0 1 0 1 0

That is 13 ones, a win rate \hat p=0.65. The bootstrap asks: if the 20 judgements are all we know about the world, what other sets might we have collected? Resample 20 judgements with replacement from the 20, compute the win rate, repeat many times, and read the middle 95% of those rates.

Worked example, the exact bootstrap distribution

Resampling 20 times from a set with 65% ones means each draw is a 1 with probability 0.65, so the number of ones in a resample is Binomial(20, 0.65). Its cumulative probabilities: at 8 wins 0.0196, at 9 wins 0.0532 (so the 2.5th percentile is 9 wins, 0.45); at 16 wins 0.9556, at 17 wins 0.9879 (so the 97.5th percentile is 17 wins, 0.85). The percentile interval is 0.45 to 0.85.

A quick formula agrees: the standard error is \sqrt{0.65\times0.35/20}=0.1067, and 0.65\pm1.96\times0.1067 gives 0.441 to 0.859. The interval contains 0.5, so 13 of 20 does not establish that A is better. With four times the data (52 wins of 80, the same rate) the standard error is 0.0533, and the interval narrows to about 0.55 to 0.75.

\hat p=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i,\qquad \text{bootstrap: } \hat p^{*}_b=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}y^{*}_{b,i},\ \ y^{*}_{b,i}\sim\text{uniform over }\{y_1,\dots,y_n\},\ \ b=1,\dots,B
Symbols. y_i\in\{0,1\}: the i-th judgement. n: how many judgements. \hat p: the observed win rate. y^{*}_{b,i}: the i-th item of the b-th resample. B: number of resamples. The interval is the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of \hat p^{*}_1,\dots,\hat p^{*}_B. More on intervals: confidence intervals and the bootstrap.

Step 5, model-based judges and their known biases. Because human judging is slow, a strong model is often asked to judge two answers. This is cheap and scalable, but judges have systematic biases reported in published studies of this practice: position bias (preferring the first or the second answer shown), length bias (preferring longer answers regardless of content), and self-preference (favouring answers written by the same model or style as the judge). The size of each bias depends on the judge and the task, so measure it for your own setup rather than assume a number. A common mitigation for position bias is to judge each pair twice with the order swapped and count only consistent verdicts.

Worked example, swapping the order

Judged only in order (A,B), A is preferred in pairs 1, 2, 4, 5, so 4/6=0.667. Using both orders, A wins 2 pairs, B wins 1 and 3 pairs are ties (half a point each): (2+3\times0.5)/6=3.5/6=0.583. Half of the first number's advantage came from the slot the answer was shown in. The same idea checks length bias (compare verdicts after trimming answers to equal length) and self-preference (compare judges from different families).

Step 6, scoring images: Fréchet distance and CLIP score. For images there is no reference text. A popular idea is to compare the distribution of generated images with the distribution of real ones in a feature space. A fixed pre-trained network turns every image into a feature vector. Model the real features as a Gaussian with mean \mu_r and covariance \Sigma_r and the generated features as \mu_g,\Sigma_g. The squared Fréchet distance between two Gaussians (the quantity behind FID) is

d^2=\lVert\mu_r-\mu_g\rVert^2+\mathrm{Tr}\Bigl(\Sigma_r+\Sigma_g-2\,(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}\Bigr)
Symbols. \mu_r,\mu_g: mean feature vectors of real and generated images. \Sigma_r,\Sigma_g: their covariance matrices. \lVert\cdot\rVert^2: squared length. \mathrm{Tr}: the trace, the sum of the diagonal. (\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}: the matrix square root of the product. The first term measures how far apart the centres are; the second measures how different the spreads and tilts are. Zero means identical Gaussians.
Worked example, a tiny 2-D case

Real features: \mu_r=(0,0), \Sigma_r=\mathrm{diag}(1,4). Generated: \mu_g=(3,4), \Sigma_g=\mathrm{diag}(4,1). Mean term: 3^2+4^2=25. For diagonal matrices \Sigma_r\Sigma_g=\mathrm{diag}(4,4), whose square root is \mathrm{diag}(2,2) with trace 4. Trace term: (1+4)+(4+1)-2\times4=2. So d^2=25+2=27.

For a general 2 × 2 case the trace of the square root has a shortcut: \mathrm{Tr}\bigl((\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}\bigr)=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)+2\sqrt{\det\Sigma_r\det\Sigma_g}}. In the example \sqrt{8+2\sqrt{16}}=4, matching. The lab uses this formula.

Its weaknesses follow from its recipe: it depends on the chosen feature network; a Gaussian is a crude summary; and when the covariances are estimated from few images the estimate is biased and noisy, which the lab shows. A different score, CLIP score, checks text-to-image prompt agreement: the cosine between the image's and the prompt's embeddings from a contrastive model (lesson 9.1), sometimes rescaled. It does not judge realism or fine detail, and inherits the encoder's blind spots. This module deliberately quotes no scores of real systems: they depend on the data, network and version used.

  2. Set the generated mean to (0, 0) and the spreads to 1 and 2: the distance should reach 0 for the population, but the sample estimate does not at small n. Why?

Common traps
  • Comparing scores from different setups. Perplexity across tokenisers, or an FID computed with different networks or sample sizes, is not comparable.
  • No interval. A difference smaller than the interval is not a ranking. 13 of 20 does not beat 0.5.
  • Believing a public benchmark. Without a contamination check a high score may be memory, not ability.
  • Trusting a judge you have not audited. Swap positions, control length and compare judges before using a model-based judge for decisions.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Model selection: choosing between candidates uses benchmarks, human or model-judged preference ratings, and intervals around each.
  • Preference data for alignment uses the same pairwise comparisons and logistic (Bradley–Terry) form as Elo.
  • Image generation research: distribution distances such as FID and prompt-agreement scores are reported together with human study results.

3. Compute the squared Fréchet distance for means (0,0) and (1,2) and identical covariance matrices.

Takeaways

  • Perplexity is \exp of the mean negative log probability; contamination adds c(m-b) to a benchmark score.
  • Elo turns pairwise outcomes into ratings; always attach an interval, for example by the bootstrap (13 of 20 gives 0.45 to 0.85).
  • Model-based judges show position, length and self-preference bias; swap the order and audit them.
  • The Fréchet distance is \lVert\Delta\mu\rVert^2 plus a covariance term (27 in the worked example); it depends on the feature network and the sample size.
9.4

Hallucination and factuality

The questionA language model states a wrong date in a smooth, confident sentence. Why does that happen, how would we notice that its confidence is unreliable, and is "well calibrated" the same thing as "factually correct"?

Intuition: fluent is not the same as true

A language model is trained to continue text so that the next token is plausible given the context. A sentence that sounds like a correct answer is a plausible continuation whether or not it is correct. When the model has seen a fact often, the plausible continuation is usually the true one. When the fact is rare, or absent, the model still has to emit something, and it emits what fits the pattern: a date in the right format, a name that looks like an author's. That plausible-but-unsupported output is called a hallucination. Several things contribute (hedged, because their relative weight is still studied): rare facts, ambiguity in the question, training that seldom rewards "I do not know", and a sampling process that sometimes picks less likely tokens.

Two different questions must be kept apart. Factuality: is the statement true or supported by evidence? Calibration: does the model's stated confidence match how often it is right? A model can be well calibrated and often wrong (it says "60%" and is right 60% of the time), and a model can be accurate and badly calibrated.

Build it up

Step 1, calibration on a table. Suppose a model answers 100 questions and gives a confidence with each answer. Group the answers into confidence bins. In a well calibrated model, among answers given 80% confidence about 80% should be right.

Worked example, expected calibration error

Add the last column: 0.0050+0.0175+0.0375+0.0450+0.0400=0.1450. The expected calibration error (ECE) is 0.145: on average the stated confidence is about 14.5 points away from the observed accuracy. Overall accuracy is 64/100=0.64 while the mean confidence is 0.785: the model is overconfident in every bin (accuracy below confidence).

ECE can hide errors that cancel. Take two bins: 40 answers at confidence 0.9 with accuracy 0.8, and 60 answers at confidence 0.6 with accuracy 0.7. Mean confidence is 0.4(0.9)+0.6(0.6)=0.72 and accuracy 0.4(0.8)+0.6(0.7)=0.74, nearly equal, yet ECE is 0.4(0.1)+0.6(0.1)=0.10. Binned ECE also depends on how the bins are chosen.

\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{n_b}{n}\,\bigl|\,\mathrm{acc}(b)-\mathrm{conf}(b)\,\bigr|
Symbols. B: number of confidence bins. n_b: answers in bin b; n: all answers. \mathrm{acc}(b): fraction correct in the bin. \mathrm{conf}(b): the mean stated confidence in the bin. A perfectly calibrated model has ECE 0. See also calibration and uncertainty.

Step 2, using confidence: answer or abstain. If confidence is informative, the model can decline to answer when it is low. From the table, answering only where the confidence is at least 0.8 keeps the 50 answers of the last two bins; 21 + 15 = 36 are correct, so accuracy rises from 0.64 to 36/50=0.72, while coverage (the share of questions answered) falls to 0.50. Choosing the threshold trades accuracy against coverage.

Step 3, factuality is about claims, not confidence. To measure factuality of a long answer, split it into small checkable claims and ask, for each, whether a trusted source supports it. A simple score is the fraction of claims supported.

Worked example, claim-level factuality

A short biography contains 10 atomic claims. Checked against a reference: 7 supported, 2 contradicted, 1 not verifiable. The supported fraction is 7/10=0.70. Reporting the three counts is more honest than one number, because "unverifiable" and "contradicted" call for different responses. Note that a model that said all ten claims at 95% confidence would be miscalibrated and only 70% factual; one that said all ten at 70% would be calibrated and exactly as factual.

Step 4, grounding and verification. Two families of remedies reduce unsupported statements. Grounding supplies evidence in the prompt (retrieved documents, tool results), asks the model to answer only from it and to cite it, so that each claim can be traced. Verification checks the output afterwards: compare claims with the sources, sample several answers and check agreement, or ask a second pass to look for unsupported statements. None is perfect: retrieval can bring wrong or outdated documents, a model can misread a correct one, and a verifier can share the generator's blind spots.

Common traps
  • Equating calibration with correctness. A calibrated model that is right 40% of the time is honestly unreliable; it is still unreliable.
  • Reading a model's verbal confidence as a probability. Stated confidence ("I am sure") must itself be checked against outcomes, as in the table.
  • One ECE number. Binned ECE depends on bin choice and hides cancelling errors; also look at the reliability diagram and at accuracy by slice.
  • Trusting a citation because it exists. A cited source may not say what the claim says; verify that the cited text supports the claim.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Retrieval-augmented assistants ground answers in documents and show citations so a user can check them.
  • Selective answering and abstention use confidence or agreement across samples to decline low-confidence questions.
  • Factuality benchmarks typically score atomic claims against references, with the evaluation cautions of lesson 9.3 (intervals, contamination, judge bias).

Check yourself

1. Two bins: 40 answers at confidence 0.9, accuracy 0.8; 60 answers at confidence 0.6, accuracy 0.7. What is the ECE and the overall accuracy?
ECE = 0.4 × 0.1 + 0.6 × 0.1 = 0.10; accuracy = 0.4 × 0.8 + 0.6 × 0.7 = 0.74.
2. In the printed table, if the model answers only when confidence is at least 0.7, what are the accuracy and coverage?
Bins from 0.7 up hold 25 + 30 + 20 = 75 answers with 15 + 21 + 15 = 51 correct: accuracy 51/75 = 0.68, coverage 0.75.
3. Can a model be perfectly calibrated and still hallucinate often? Explain.
Yes. Perfect calibration only says that among answers given 60% confidence, 60% are right. If the model gives most answers low or moderate confidence and is right 40% to 60% of the time, it is calibrated but a large share of its statements are false. Calibration says whether you can trust the confidence, not whether the statement is true.

Takeaways

  • Hallucination is fluent output without support; factuality (is it true?) and calibration (does confidence match accuracy?) are different properties.
  • ECE = \sum (n_b/n)|\mathrm{acc}-\mathrm{conf}|; the printed table gives 0.145 and an overconfident model.
  • Measure factuality claim by claim; reduce errors with grounding and verification, and know their limits.
9.5

Misuse, provenance and consent

The questionGenerative systems make fluent text, convincing images and cloned voices cheaply. Which risks does that create, what can technology do to help people tell where content came from, what can it not do, and why do reasonable people disagree about copyright and consent?

Intuition: capability and trust move apart

When a tool makes a piece of media easy to produce, the media stops being evidence by itself. A photograph used to imply that a camera was somewhere; a recording used to imply that someone spoke. The honest response has three parts: reduce the risk where the system is built (safeguards, access limits, monitoring), add evidence about origin (provenance, watermarks), and maintain social and legal norms (disclosure, consent, accountability). No single part is sufficient. This lesson is conceptual and defensive: it explains what the risks are and how the countermeasures work, and it does not describe how to carry out an attack.

Build it up

Step 1, the risk landscape at a glance.

  • Jailbreaks. Attempts to talk a model out of its safety training with clever wording. Models are trained and filtered against known patterns, and new phrasings keep being found, so this is an ongoing contest and not a solved problem.
  • Prompt injection. A model that reads untrusted text (a web page, an email, a document) cannot reliably tell "data to summarise" from "instructions to follow", so text planted in that content may try to redirect the assistant. The structural defences are: give the model the least privilege it needs, never let untrusted content authorise consequential actions without human confirmation, treat model output as untrusted input to other systems, and layer several checks.
  • Synthetic media. Fabricated images, audio and video can be used for fraud, impersonation, harassment (including non-consensual imagery) and disinformation. A second effect is the "liar's dividend": real evidence can be dismissed as fake.
  • Scale. Automation lowers the cost of producing spam, persuasion and low-quality content, which can overwhelm human review.

Step 2, provenance: evidence about where content came from. The most robust idea is signed metadata. When a camera or a generator creates a file, it records a manifest (who or what made it, with which tool, what edits followed), computes a hash of the content, and signs the manifest with a secret key. Anyone can later recompute the hash and check the signature with the matching public key. Industry content-credential standards exist along these lines; this lesson explains the mechanism, not a particular product.

\text{valid}\iff \mathrm{hash}(\text{content})=h_{\text{manifest}}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathrm{verify}\bigl(\text{signature},\,(h_{\text{manifest}},\text{claims}),\,\text{public key}\bigr)
Symbols. \mathrm{hash}: a function that maps any content to a short fingerprint; changing the content changes the fingerprint. h_{\text{manifest}}: the fingerprint recorded at signing. claims: statements such as "made by tool X". signature: made with a private key; checked with the public key. If either test fails the credential is broken.

What a valid credential does show: this content is exactly what the signer signed, and the signer made the stated claim. What it does not show: that the content is true, that the signer is trustworthy, or anything at all when the credential is missing. Metadata is easily dropped when a file is re-saved, screenshotted or uploaded to a service that strips it, so absence is normal and must not be read as "fake". Provenance works best when most legitimate tools attach it and users are shown the result.

Try this
  1. Press "Verify again" with the original text and watch the three checks turn green. Read the verdict sentence closely.
  2. Change one letter in the content. Which step fails, and why does a harmless edit also fail?
  3. Press "Sign with a different key", then "Strip the metadata". Compare the two verdicts: failed versus unverifiable.

Step 3, watermarks: evidence hidden in the content itself. A watermark is a signal placed in the output as it is generated, so it survives when the metadata is gone. For text, one published scheme splits the vocabulary at each position into a "green" list and a "red" list by a secret rule, and gently nudges the generator toward green tokens. A detector that knows the rule counts green tokens. Ordinary text is green by chance with probability \gamma, so an excess of green is statistical evidence.

z=\frac{G-\gamma\,T}{\sqrt{T\,\gamma\,(1-\gamma)}}
Symbols. T: tokens examined. G: how many of them are green. \gamma: the chance an ordinary token is green (the green fraction). \gamma T: the number expected by chance and \sqrt{T\gamma(1-\gamma)} its standard deviation, so z counts standard deviations above chance (a normal approximation). A threshold such as z\ge4 keeps false alarms on ordinary text rare.
Worked example, a detection and its erosion

Take T=200 tokens and \gamma=0.25. By chance we expect 50 green tokens with standard deviation \sqrt{200\times0.25\times0.75}=6.124. A watermarked text in which 40% of tokens are green has G=80, so z=(80-50)/6.124=4.90. For ordinary text, P(z\ge4)\approx3.2\times10^{-5} per test.

Now suppose half of the tokens are later replaced by other words, which are green only 25% of the time. The expected green count becomes 200\,(0.5\times0.4+0.5\times0.25)=65, so z=(65-50)/6.124=2.45, below the threshold: detection fails although the text began as watermarked. Detection is therefore evidence that weakens with editing, short texts, and translation, and it applies only to systems that embed the mark and share the check.

Try this
  1. With rewriting 0%, watch the orange line cross the red threshold while the blue line wanders near zero. About how many tokens does it need?
  2. Increase the rewriting share to 50% and then 100%. At what share does the watermarked line stay below the threshold at 400 tokens?
  3. Raise γ to 0.5 with the other sliders fixed. Is detection easier or harder, and why?

Step 4, detectors of AI-made content and the base-rate problem. A classifier that guesses whether something was AI-generated, without any embedded signal, is a statistical guess that can be wrong in both directions, and the rates depend on the model, the text and the writer. Reports and studies have raised concern that such detectors can mislabel the writing of some groups, for example people writing in a second language. Even an apparently accurate detector produces many wrong accusations when the thing it looks for is rare.

Worked example, a detector and a rare event

Suppose a detector flags 90% of AI-written texts and 5% of human-written ones (illustrative). Among 1,000 submissions, 10 are AI-written. The detector flags 0.90\times10=9 of them and 0.05\times990=49.5 of the human ones. Of the flagged texts only 9/(9+49.5)=0.154, about 15%, were really AI-written. Decisions about people should never rest on such a flag alone.

Step 5, copyright and consent, stated from both sides. These questions are open, differ between countries, and are the subject of ongoing court cases and legislation, so nothing here is legal advice.

  • Arguments made in favour of training on public works: the model learns statistical patterns, not stored copies; analysing works to extract facts and style has long been accepted in some legal traditions; broad training data is said to benefit society and competition; blocking it could favour a few large rights-holders.
  • Arguments made against: works are copied and used commercially without permission or payment; models can sometimes reproduce passages or imitate a living creator's style closely enough to substitute for their work; creators did not agree to this use when they published.
  • Designs under discussion: opt-out and opt-in registries, licensing and revenue sharing, disclosure of training sources, filters against reproducing protected passages, and rules about using a person's likeness or voice, where consent is the central principle.

The technical facts relevant to both sides are that models can memorise some training examples, especially those repeated often, and that no technical measure today can fully prove what a given model did or did not learn from a given work.

Common traps
  • "No credential, so it is fake". Most authentic content has no credential. Missing metadata is not evidence.
  • "Valid signature, so it is true". A signature proves origin and integrity only.
  • Trusting one defence. Safeguards, provenance, watermarks, detection, human review and policy each fail in different ways; they are layered for that reason.
  • Treating a detector's output as proof. With a rare target the large majority of flags can be false.
  • Treating contested law as settled. Copyright and consent rules vary and change; check current local guidance for any real decision.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Content credentials and signed metadata from creation tools, with verification shown in viewers and platforms.
  • Statistical watermarks for generated text and invisible marks for images and audio, with their detection tests.
  • Safety work on models and agents: red-teaming for jailbreaks, least-privilege design against prompt injection, and policies on likeness, voice and training data. The next lesson covers the guardrails around a deployed system.

Check yourself

1. A text of 100 tokens, γ = 0.25, has 45 green tokens. What is z?
Expected 25, standard deviation \sqrt{100\times0.25\times0.75}=4.330, so z=(45-25)/4.330=4.62.
2. What is the difference between "verification failed" and "no credential"?
Failed verification means a credential is present but the content or signature does not match it: something was altered or the signer cannot be trusted. No credential means nothing can be checked, which is common for genuine content and says nothing about authenticity either way.
3. A detector flags 90% of AI texts and 5% of human texts, and 1% of submissions are AI-written. What share of flagged items is really AI-written?
Per 1,000 items: 9 true flags and 49.5 false flags, so 9 / 58.5 = 0.154, about 15%.

Takeaways

  • Risks include jailbreaks, prompt injection, synthetic-media abuse and scale; defences are layered because each one fails somewhere.
  • Signed provenance proves origin and integrity when present; its absence proves nothing. Statistical watermarks give evidence (z-score) that weakens with editing.
  • Copyright and consent debates have serious arguments on both sides and unsettled law; consent for likeness and voice is a central principle.
9.6

Running generative systems in production

The questionYour assistant works in a demo. Before you launch it, a manager asks: how long will a user wait, what will a million requests a month cost, how do we stop harmful requests without blocking legitimate ones, and how will we know next month that something broke?

Intuition: a deployed model is a service with a budget

In production the model is one component of a service. It has a latency budget (how long the user waits), a cost budget (money per request, times volume), a safety layer that checks inputs and outputs, and a monitoring and release process that catches regressions. Each has a simple model you can compute before you launch. All prices and speeds in this lesson are invented round numbers for illustration, not real prices of any product.

Build it up

Step 1, latency. A request waits in a queue, then the model prefills (reads the whole prompt in parallel, fast per token), then decodes (writes the answer one token at a time, slow per token). The user sees the first token after the time to first token, and the whole answer after the total latency.

\text{TTFT}=t_{\text{queue}}+\frac{P}{r_{\text{prefill}}},\qquad \text{latency}=\text{TTFT}+\frac{O-1}{r_{\text{decode}}}
Symbols. P: prompt tokens. O: output tokens. r_{\text{prefill}}: tokens read per second; r_{\text{decode}}: tokens written per second. t_{\text{queue}}: waiting before processing starts. The first output token arrives with the end of prefill, so the remaining O-1 tokens take the decode time.
Worked example, an illustrative budget

Take P=1500, O=300, prefill 5,000 tokens/s, decode 40 tokens/s, queue 0.1 s. TTFT = 0.1+1500/5000=0.40 s. Decode = 299/40=7.475 s. Total latency = 7.875 s. Decode dominates: output length is the lever. With O=200 the latency is 0.4+199/40=5.375 s.

Cost with illustrative prices of $2 per million input tokens and $8 per million output tokens: 1500\times2\times10^{-6}+300\times8\times10^{-6}=0.0030+0.0024=\$0.0054 per request, so $5,400 for a million requests.

Step 2, caching. Many requests start with the same long system prompt. If the first 1000 tokens match a stored prefix, the system can skip reading them again and often charges less for them. Suppose cached tokens cost 10% of the input price and need no prefill. Then a hit costs 1000\times0.2\times10^{-6}+500\times2\times10^{-6}+0.0024=0.0002+0.0010+0.0024=\$0.0036, a third cheaper, and its TTFT falls to 0.1+500/5000=0.20 s (total 7.675 s). If 60% of requests hit the cache, the average cost is 0.6(0.0036)+0.4(0.0054)=\$0.00432, or $4,320 per million, with expected TTFT 0.28 s.

  2. Set the latency budget to 6 s. Both lanes miss it. Which single slider change (output length, decode speed, queue) meets the budget at the least cost to the user?
  3. Double the prompt to 3000 tokens with the cache at 1000 tokens. Does caching help more or less in relative terms?

Step 3, guardrails and their error trade-off. A guardrail is a check around the model: an input check (is this request allowed?), an output check (is the answer safe to show?), often by a classifier that outputs a risk score. A threshold turns the score into block or pass. There are two kinds of error: a false positive (a harmless request blocked, which frustrates users) and a false negative (a harmful one let through). Moving the threshold trades one for the other. Worked example, counting and the base rate

At threshold 0.5 the benign scores 0.55 and 0.62 are blocked (2 false positives) and the harmful scores 0.35 and 0.48 pass (2 false negatives): FPR = 2/10 = 0.2 and FNR = 2/10 = 0.2, so the true positive rate is 0.8. No threshold gives zero for both, because the two groups overlap (benign up to 0.62, harmful down to 0.35).

Real traffic is mostly benign. Suppose only 1% of requests are harmful. At threshold 0.5, per 10,000 requests, 100 are harmful and 80 are blocked; 9,900 are benign and 1,980 are blocked. Of the 2,060 blocked, only 80/2060=0.039, about 4%, are harmful. That is \mathrm{TPR}\,\pi/(\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi+\mathrm{FPR}(1-\pi))=0.8(0.01)/(0.008+0.2(0.99))=0.0388. At threshold 0.7 the 10 benign samples show 0 false positives, but that does not mean the true rate is 0; with only 10 benign cases the 95% upper limit is roughly 3/10 = 30% (the "rule of three"). A real evaluation needs hundreds of labelled cases and intervals (lesson 9.3).

\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN},\quad \mathrm{FNR}=\frac{FN}{FN+TP},\quad \text{precision}=\frac{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi}{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi+\mathrm{FPR}\,(1-\pi)}
Symbols. FP: benign requests blocked. TN: benign passed. FN: harmful passed. TP: harmful blocked. \mathrm{TPR}=1-\mathrm{FNR}. \pi: the share of real traffic that is harmful. Precision is the share of blocked requests that are truly harmful. See classification metrics.

  2. Change the harmful share from 1% to 20%. How does the share of blocked requests that are truly harmful change at threshold 0.5?

Step 4, monitoring, evaluation harnesses and versioning. Quality in production changes silently: user mix shifts, upstream documents change, a model or prompt is updated. Four habits catch this.

  • Monitor what you can measure: latency percentiles, error and refusal rates, token counts, cost per request, block rates of each guardrail, and sampled human or judged quality scores with intervals. Alert on changes against a baseline (the drift ideas of data science in production apply to input statistics).
  • An evaluation harness: a fixed, versioned set of test requests with expected properties, run automatically before every change, so a "small prompt tweak" cannot silently break a case that worked.
  • Version everything together: model version, prompt text, retrieval index, guardrail thresholds and settings. Record all of them with each response so any answer can be reproduced and a regression traced to the change that caused it.
  • Release gradually: send a small share of traffic to the new version, compare with the old on the harness and on live metrics, and keep a quick way to roll back.

Together these make a pipeline: input guard, then the model with its cached prefix, then output guard, with logs and metrics feeding the monitors, and the harness gating each release.

Common traps
  • Averaging latency. Users feel the slow tail; track high percentiles, and remember that long outputs dominate decode time.
  • Budgeting only the happy path. Retries, longer-than-expected prompts and tool calls multiply tokens; budget with the realistic distribution, not the average demo.
  • Setting a guardrail threshold on a tiny set. Ten examples cannot show a rate near zero. Measure on enough cases, report intervals and revisit as traffic changes.
  • Forgetting the base rate. A filter with a modest false-positive rate on mostly benign traffic mostly blocks benign requests.
  • Changing the prompt without a test run. Prompts are code; version and test them.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Serving stacks report time to first token and tokens per second, use prefix caching and batching, and price input and output tokens separately.
  • Moderation and policy layers are classifiers with thresholds tuned on labelled data, and are re-evaluated as misuse patterns change (lesson 9.5).
  • MLOps for generative systems adds prompt and index versions to the model registry, with evaluation harnesses as release gates (compare monitoring and drift).

Check yourself

1. At $2 per million input tokens and $8 per million output tokens, what does one request with 2000 prompt tokens and 500 output tokens cost?
2000\times2\times10^{-6}+500\times8\times10^{-6}=0.004+0.004=\$0.008.
2. With a 0.2 s queue, a 3000-token prompt and prefill at 5,000 tokens/s, what is the time to first token?
0.2+3000/5000=0.8 s.
3. A filter blocks 80% of harmful and 20% of benign requests, and 5% of traffic is harmful. What share of blocked requests is harmful?
0.8(0.05)/(0.8(0.05)+0.2(0.95))=0.04/0.23=0.174, about 17%.

Takeaways

  • Latency = queue + prefill + decode, and decode (output length) usually dominates; cost is input tokens times input price plus output tokens times output price (7.875 s and $0.0054 in the illustrative example).
  • Caching reduces both cost and time to first token for shared prefixes.
  • A guardrail threshold trades false positives against false negatives; the base rate decides how many blocks are real.
  • Monitor, test with a versioned harness, version model, prompt and settings together, and release gradually.
