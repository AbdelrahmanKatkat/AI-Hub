Module 2 · Kinematics & dynamics

A robot is a chain of rigid links joined by motors. Kinematics asks where the parts are and how fast they move, without caring why. Dynamics asks what torques are needed to make them move that way. By the end you will chain link transforms by hand, solve for the joint angles that reach a target, map joint speeds and forces through the Jacobian, write the equations of motion of a two-link arm, model a wheeled base, and simulate all of it without the numbers blowing up.

6 lessons≈ 3 hoursNeeds Module 0 (frames) and Mathematics (matrices, derivatives)

Where this module sits in the loop. Kinematics and dynamics run inside the think stage of the sense–think–act loop. A joint controller reads encoders (sense) at 1 kHz, computes the torque the motor must make (think), and sends the command to the motor driver (act). Forward kinematics is computed at the estimator rate, often 100 Hz to 1 kHz. Inverse kinematics runs when a planner asks for a pose, usually at 10 to 100 Hz.

Units. Angles are in radians inside the equations and in degrees in the readouts; each lab says which. Lengths are metres, times are seconds, torques are newton-metres (N·m), and gravity is g = 9.81 m/s².

2.1

Forward kinematics

The questionA two-link arm has encoders on both joints. They read 30° at the shoulder and 45° at the elbow. Where is the gripper tip, in metres, relative to the shoulder? You need that answer thousands of times a second to know whether the hand is about to hit the table.

Intuition

Imagine two rigid sticks, one pinned to a wall and the other pinned to the end of the first. Each joint is a hinge. Once you know the two hinge angles, the whole arm is fixed, so the position of the tip must be a function of those two numbers alone. Forward kinematics is that function. It is a bookkeeping exercise: start at the base, move along the first stick, turn at the elbow, move along the second stick. Where you end up is the tip.

The one subtle point is the word relative. The elbow encoder measures how far the forearm has turned relative to the upper arm, not relative to the floor. The forearm's true direction in the world is the shoulder angle plus the elbow angle. Most mistakes in robot code come from mixing these two conventions.

Build it up: one link, then two

Step 1: one link. A stick of length l that makes angle \theta with the x-axis ends at (l\cos\theta,\ l\sin\theta). This is just the definition of cosine and sine: the stick is the hypotenuse, its horizontal projection is the cosine and its vertical projection is the sine.

Step 2: two links, absolute angles. Put the second stick at the end of the first. Its tip offset from the elbow is (l_2\cos\phi,\ l_2\sin\phi), where \phi is its absolute direction. Adding the two offsets gives the tip.

Step 3: switch to relative angles. The encoder gives \theta_2, the turn at the elbow. The absolute direction of the forearm is \phi=\theta_1+\theta_2. Substituting gives the familiar form x=l_1\cos\theta_1+l_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2).

Step 4: chain the frames. Each link gets its own coordinate frame, a rotation followed by a translation along the link. The composition of the frames is one matrix product, and the tip position is its last column. This is how a robot with 6 or 7 joints is handled with the same code.

Worked example: the two-link arm at 30° and 45°

Take l_1=1.00\text{ m}, l_2=0.50\text{ m}, \theta_1=30^\circ, \theta_2=45^\circ. Then the forearm's absolute direction is \theta_1+\theta_2=75^\circ.

Elbow. \cos 30^\circ=0.8660, \sin 30^\circ=0.5000, so the elbow is at (1.00\times0.8660,\ 1.00\times0.5000)=(0.8660,\ 0.5000)\text{ m}.

Forearm offset. \cos 75^\circ=0.2588, \sin 75^\circ=0.9659, so the offset is (0.50\times0.2588,\ 0.50\times0.9659)=(0.1294,\ 0.4830)\text{ m}.

Tip. Adding: x=0.8660+0.1294=0.9954\text{ m} and y=0.5000+0.4830=0.9830\text{ m}. The tip sits about 1.4 m from the shoulder, up and out.

Check by the matrix chain. Each link is a 3×3 homogeneous transform T_i=\text{Rot}(\theta_i)\,\text{Trans}(l_i). Multiplying them gives a total transform whose rotation block is \begin{pmatrix}0.2588 & -0.9659\\0.9659 & 0.2588\end{pmatrix} (a rotation by 75°) and whose last column is (0.9954,\ 0.9830,\ 1). The same numbers come out, now as one product.

The formal definition

For a planar arm with n revolute joints, the forward kinematics map takes the joint vector to the end-effector pose. For the two-link arm:

\mathbf{x}=f(\mathbf{q})=\begin{pmatrix} l_1\cos\theta_1+l_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\\ l_1\sin\theta_1+l_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\end{pmatrix}

The general tool is the Denavit–Hartenberg (DH) convention. Each joint gets four numbers, and the transform from frame i-1 to frame i is

T_i=\text{Rot}_z(\theta_i)\,\text{Trans}_z(d_i)\,\text{Trans}_x(a_i)\,\text{Rot}_x(\alpha_i)

For the planar two-link arm, \alpha=0 and d=0 for both joints, so the DH table is short:

Joint\theta_i (variable)d_ia_i\alpha_i
1\theta_10l_1=1.00 m0
2\theta_20l_2=0.50 m0

The tip pose is T_{\text{tip}}=T_1T_2. Its translation part is the vector \mathbf{x} above.

Try this
  1. Set both angles to 0°. The arm is straight along x, so the tip should be at (1.5, 0) m. Why is that the reach limit?
  2. Set the elbow to 90° with the shoulder at 0°. Check the tip against the formula by hand: (1,0)+(0,0.5)=(1,0.5).
  3. Set the shoulder to 30° and the elbow to 45°. Compare the readout with the worked example above.
  4. Edit the expression for the shoulder trajectory to pi/3*sin(0.3*t) and press play. The tip traces a curve; notice it is never more than 1.5 m from the shoulder.
Common traps
  • Absolute versus relative. Using the elbow reading as if it were the forearm's world direction gives a tip that is wrong by the shoulder angle. Check which convention your driver reports.
  • Degrees in a radian function. cos(45) in most programming languages means 45 radians. Convert once, at the boundary.
  • Frames are not commutative. T_1T_2\neq T_2T_1 in general. Multiply in the order the links are attached, base first.
  • Forward kinematics is a function, inverse is not. One set of angles gives one tip pose; a pose can come from several angle sets (Lesson 2.2).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Learned manipulation policies receive joint readings, but their training data often stores end-effector poses. Forward kinematics converts between the two, so a policy can be trained in either space.
  • Pose estimation for grasping (Module 4) reports the object in the camera frame; forward kinematics of the arm, through the hand-eye transform, tells the planner where that object is in the base frame.
  • Simulators such as MuJoCo and Pinocchio evaluate the same chain of transforms, once per simulated step, for every robot in a batch.

Check yourself

1. A two-link arm has l_1=0.8 m and l_2=0.6 m. What is the largest distance the tip can reach from the shoulder?
The arm is straightest when \theta_2=0, so the distance is l_1+l_2=1.4 m. Any bend reduces it.
2. With l_1=1 m, l_2=0.5 m, \theta_1=0^\circ and \theta_2=90^\circ, what is the tip position?
The forearm's absolute direction is 90^\circ, so the tip is the elbow (1,0) plus (0,0.5), which gives (1.0,\ 0.5) m.
3. Why do the rotation parts of the frames multiply, but the translations do not simply add?
Each link's translation is expressed in its own rotated frame, so it must be rotated by everything upstream before it is added. The homogeneous product handles this automatically: the last column of T_1T_2 is (l_1\cos\theta_1,\ l_1\sin\theta_1)+\text{Rot}(\theta_1)(l_2\cos\theta_2,\ l_2\sin\theta_2), which is the formula with the relative angle, as derived in Step 3.

Takeaways

  • Forward kinematics maps joint angles to the tip pose by adding link vectors, each rotated by the sum of the angles before it.
  • Keep one convention for angles, and write the DH table before coding a chain of more than two links.
  • The tip pose is a single matrix product of link transforms; the last column is the position.
Pipeline positionStage 3, Estimate (state of the arm) and stage 4, Plan. Forward kinematics reads the encoders and writes the end-effector pose, a small set of numbers stored per control tick.
2.2

Inverse kinematics

The questionThe gripper must reach a cup at (1.0,\ 0.8) m from the shoulder. Which shoulder and elbow angles put the tip there? Is there more than one answer, and what if the cup is 2 m away?

Intuition

Forward kinematics went from angles to a point. Inverse kinematics runs the other way. Picture the shoulder as a centre and the elbow as a point that must lie on a circle of radius l_1. The tip must lie on a circle of radius l_2 around the elbow. The two circles intersect in at most two points, and each intersection is a valid elbow position. Those two points are the elbow-up and elbow-down solutions, the same hand reached with the elbow above or below the line from shoulder to target.

If the circles do not meet, the target is out of reach. If the target is exactly at the far edge of both circles, the arm is straight and there is one solution.

Build it up: the triangle

Step 1: the reach. The distance from shoulder to target is r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}. Two links of lengths l_1,l_2 can only span distances between |l_1-l_2| and l_1+l_2. Outside that band there is no solution.

Step 2: the elbow angle from the cosine rule. The three sides l_1,l_2,r form a triangle. The angle between the two links is \pi-\theta_2 at the elbow, and the cosine rule gives \cos\theta_2=\dfrac{r^2-l_1^2-l_2^2}{2l_1l_2}. The sign of \sin\theta_2 chooses elbow up or down.

Step 3: the shoulder angle. Let \alpha=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x) be the direction of the target, and \beta=\operatorname{atan2}(l_2\sin\theta_2,\ l_1+l_2\cos\theta_2) the offset caused by the bent elbow. Then \theta_1=\alpha-\beta.

Worked example: reaching (1.0, 0.8) m

Links l_1=1.00 m and l_2=0.50 m. Distance: r^2=1.0^2+0.8^2=1.64, so r=1.281 m. This is inside the reach band [0.5,\ 1.5], so a solution exists.

Elbow. \cos\theta_2=\dfrac{1.64-1.00-0.25}{2\times1.00\times0.50}=\dfrac{0.39}{1.00}=0.390. So \theta_2=\pm67.05^\circ (the two signs are the two elbows), and \sin\theta_2=\pm0.9208.

Shoulder, elbow up (\theta_2=+67.05^\circ). \alpha=\operatorname{atan2}(0.8,\,1.0)=38.66^\circ. \beta=\operatorname{atan2}(0.5\times0.9208,\ 1.0+0.5\times0.390)=\operatorname{atan2}(0.4604,\ 1.195)=21.07^\circ. So \theta_1=38.66-21.07=17.59^\circ.

Shoulder, elbow down (\theta_2=-67.05^\circ). The sine flips sign, so \beta becomes -21.07^\circ and \theta_1=38.66+21.07=59.73^\circ.

Check by forward kinematics. Elbow up: x=\cos17.59^\circ+0.5\cos(84.64^\circ)=0.9532+0.5\times0.0935=1.000 and y=\sin17.59^\circ+0.5\sin84.64^\circ=0.3022+0.4978=0.800. Elbow down gives the same tip, (1.000,\ 0.800).

Out of reach. Target (2.0,\ 0): r=2.0 is above l_1+l_2=1.5. The cosine formula gives \cos\theta_2=(4.00-1.25)/1.00=2.75, which is greater than 1, so there is no angle. Correctly, there is no solution.

The formal definition

Inverse kinematics solves f(\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{x}^* for \mathbf{q}, where \mathbf{x}^* is the desired pose. For the planar arm the closed-form solution is

\cos\theta_2=\frac{x^2+y^2-l_1^2-l_2^2}{2l_1l_2},\qquad \theta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)-\operatorname{atan2}\!\big(l_2\sin\theta_2,\ l_1+l_2\cos\theta_2\big)

A solution exists when |\cos\theta_2|\le1, that is when |l_1-l_2|\le r\le l_1+l_2.

rdistance from the shoulder to the target, \sqrt{x^2+y^2} (m)
\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)the angle of the point (x,y) from the positive x-axis, in the correct quadrant (rad)

Closed form versus numerical. A closed-form solution like this exists for simple arms. A general arm with six or seven joints has no tidy formula. Then a numerical solver starts from a guess and repeats the update \Delta\mathbf{q}=J^{+}(\mathbf{x}^*-f(\mathbf{q})), where J^{+} is a pseudo-inverse of the Jacobian (Lesson 2.3). The lab uses a damped version, which is more stable near singular poses.

Redundancy. A seven-joint arm reaching a point in space has a whole family of joint solutions, because the extra joint lets the elbow swivel. This extra freedom is useful for avoiding obstacles, and it is handled by the null space of the Jacobian, which Module 5 uses for planning.

Try this
  1. Drag the target into the middle of the reach band. Two arms appear: the solid one is elbow up, the dashed one elbow down. Both touch the target.
  2. Drag the target out to the edge of the band (a distance of 1.5 m). Watch the two solutions merge into one straight arm.
  3. Drag the target beyond 1.5 m. The target turns red and the solver reports no solution.
  4. Press Solve numerically from the current pose. Count the iterations and watch the error fall. Then change the damping and see how fast the error falls at a close target.
Common traps
  • Using atan instead of atan2. \arctan(y/x) loses the quadrant: the target (-1,-1) and (1,1) give the same answer. Use atan2 with both arguments.
  • Forgetting the second solution. Code that returns only elbow up will fail when an obstacle sits on that side. Return both, and pick one with a rule.
  • Clipping an out-of-range cosine. Rounding can give \cos\theta_2=1.0000001. Clamp it to [-1,1], but only after you have checked that the target is reachable within a tolerance.
  • Singular poses. Near a straight arm the solution is very sensitive: a tiny error in the target asks for a large change in the joints. Expect fast joint motion there.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Teleoperation and learned policies often output a desired tip pose. An inverse-kinematics layer turns it into joint targets that the motor controller can track.
  • Motion planners sample configurations and need the joint angles for each sampled pose (Module 5). Fast, reliable IK is a bottleneck for sampling-based planning.
  • Reinforcement learning with end-effector action spaces relies on an IK solver to produce the joint commands that the simulator executes.

Check yourself

1. Links are l_1=1 m and l_2=1 m. The target is at distance r=1 m from the shoulder. What is \cos\theta_2, and how many elbow solutions are there?
\cos\theta_2=(1-1-1)/(2\cdot1\cdot1)=-0.5, so \theta_2=\pm120^\circ. Two solutions, elbow up and down, both valid because r is strictly inside the band [0,2].
2. Why does the closed-form solver need atan2 for \theta_1?
The target can be in any of four quadrants, and \arctan(y/x) cannot tell (x,y) from (-x,-y). atan2(y,x) returns the angle in the correct quadrant.
3. A seven-joint arm has more joints than the task has degrees of freedom. Is inverse kinematics then easier or harder?
Usually easier to make feasible, since many configurations reach the target and one can be chosen to avoid obstacles. It is harder to specify, because the answer is not unique and the solver must choose one, typically by an extra cost such as staying close to a rest pose.

Takeaways

  • Inverse kinematics finds joint angles for a target pose. A two-link arm has at most two solutions, and none outside its reach band.
  • Closed forms come from the cosine rule and atan2; general arms use iterative solvers built on the Jacobian.
  • Redundant arms have many solutions, and the choice among them is a planning decision.
Pipeline positionStage 4, Plan. Inverse kinematics runs when a pose is requested, typically at 10 to 100 Hz, and returns joint targets for stage 5, Control.
2.3

The Jacobian and velocity mapping

The questionThe operator wants the gripper to slide 10 cm per second along the x-axis, from the arm pose of Lesson 2.1. How fast must each joint turn? And if the gripper pushes with 10 N in the x-direction, what torque must each motor supply to hold that push?

Intuition

Zoom in on any point of the forward-kinematics map. Over a tiny range of joint angles, the curve from joints to tip looks like a straight line. The best straight line is the Jacobian, a matrix of slopes. It is the derivative of forward kinematics. If the joints move a little, the tip moves by the Jacobian times that little. If the arm is at a different pose, the Jacobian is different, so the Jacobian is a function of the configuration.

The same matrix also works in reverse for forces. A push at the tip is equivalent to a set of torques at the joints, and the rule that converts one into the other is the transpose of the Jacobian. That symmetry is the reason force control and velocity control share one object.

Build it up: slopes first

Step 1: one joint. If only \theta_1 moves, with speed \dot\theta_1, the tip moves on a circle of radius r around the shoulder. The tip speed is l\dot\theta_1 in the tangent direction, perpendicular to the arm. Linear speed equals radius times angular speed.

Step 2: two joints. Each joint contributes its own tangent velocity, and the total is the sum. Writing the sum as a matrix product gives \dot{\mathbf{x}}=J(\mathbf{q})\dot{\mathbf{q}}.

Step 3: the columns of J. Column j of J is the tip velocity produced by moving joint j alone at unit speed. The first column is the tangent direction at the shoulder, the second is the tangent at the elbow.

Worked example: velocity and force at 30° and 45°

From Lesson 2.1: l_1=1.00 m, l_2=0.50 m, \theta_1=30^\circ, \theta_2=45^\circ, \theta_1+\theta_2=75^\circ. Differentiating the forward kinematics gives

J=\begin{pmatrix}-l_1\sin\theta_1-l_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -l_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\\ l_1\cos\theta_1+l_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & l_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\end{pmatrix}

With the numbers: -1.00(0.5000)-0.50(0.9659)=-0.9830, -0.50(0.9659)=-0.4830, 0.9954, 0.50(0.2588)=0.1294. So

J=\begin{pmatrix}-0.9830 & -0.4830\\ 0.9954 & 0.1294\end{pmatrix}\ \text{m/rad}

Velocity. Joint speeds \dot{\mathbf{q}}=(0.10,\ 0) rad/s (only the shoulder turns, at 0.10 rad/s) give \dot{\mathbf{x}}=(-0.0983,\ 0.0995) m/s. The tip moves up and left, perpendicular to the arm, as the circle picture predicts.

Joint speeds for a desired tip velocity. For the operator's request \dot{\mathbf{x}}=(0.10,\ 0) m/s, we need J\dot{\mathbf{q}}=(0.10,0). Here \det J=(-0.9830)(0.1294)-(-0.4830)(0.9954)=-0.1272+0.4808=0.3536\ \text{m}^2, which equals l_1l_2\sin\theta_2=0.5\times0.7071=0.3536. Inverting the 2×2 matrix gives \dot{\mathbf{q}}=J^{-1}(0.10,0)^{\!\top}=(0.0366,\ -0.2816)\ \text{rad/s}. The shoulder turns forward, the elbow backward.

Force. A tip force of \mathbf{F}=(10,0) N in the x-direction needs joint torques \boldsymbol\tau=J^{\top}\mathbf{F}=\begin{pmatrix}-9.83\\-4.83\end{pmatrix}\text{ N·m}. Both motors must push back against the load, and the shoulder carries most of it.

Singularity. \det J=l_1l_2\sin\theta_2 is zero when the elbow is straight (\theta_2=0) or folded flat (\theta_2=\pi). At those poses the arm cannot move the tip in one direction at any joint speed.

The formal definition

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=J(\mathbf{q})\,\dot{\mathbf{q}},\qquad J_{ij}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial q_j}\qquad\qquad \boldsymbol\tau=J(\mathbf{q})^{\top}\mathbf{F}
\mathbf{q},\ \dot{\mathbf{q}}joint angles (rad) and joint velocities (rad/s)
\dot{\mathbf{x}}tip velocity in the base frame (m/s)
J(\mathbf{q})the Jacobian, a matrix of partial derivatives with units m/rad; its entries depend on the pose
\mathbf{F}force applied at the tip (N)
\boldsymbol\taujoint torques (N·m) that produce the force \mathbf F in static equilibrium
\det Jdeterminant; zero at a singularity

The force relation comes from energy: the power delivered by the joints, \boldsymbol\tau^{\top}\dot{\mathbf q}, must equal the power at the tip, \mathbf F^{\top}\dot{\mathbf x}. Substituting \dot{\mathbf x}=J\dot{\mathbf q} gives \boldsymbol\tau^{\top}=\mathbf F^{\top}J, so \boldsymbol\tau=J^{\top}\mathbf F.

Try this
  1. Set the elbow to 45°. Watch the grey parallelogram: it shows the tip velocities that unit joint speeds produce. Its area is |\det J|.
  2. Slide the elbow toward 0°. The parallelogram flattens to a line and the readout of \det J falls to zero. That is the singularity.
  3. Set the elbow to 0.5°. Press play: the joint velocities needed for a fixed tip velocity become very large. Caption: "near-singular, joint speeds explode".
  4. Change the tip force in the readout to (10, 0) and then (0, 10). Compare which motor works hardest in each case.
Common traps
  • The Jacobian is local. Its linear map is good only for small joint changes. Predicting a large motion by multiplying once is an approximation.
  • Transpose, not inverse. Forces map through J^{\top}. Using J^{-1} for forces gives the wrong torques, and does not even exist at a singularity.
  • Position and orientation mix. A 6-DoF Jacobian stacks linear and angular rows. Their units differ (m/s and rad/s), so scale them before you read a single "speed".
  • Singularity is a property of the pose, not the robot. A singular pose is a place to avoid or to plan around, not a crash. Most controllers switch to damped solutions there.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Teleoperation converts a hand-controller velocity into joint velocities with the Jacobian, and feeds force feedback back through J^{\top}.
  • Visual servoing (Module 4) uses an image Jacobian, a close cousin of this one, to convert pixel error into camera motion.
  • Learned controllers sometimes predict task-space actions and rely on the Jacobian to map them to joints. Its conditioning is a diagnostic for poses where learning will be unstable.

Check yourself

1. At \theta_1=0^\circ,\ \theta_2=90^\circ with l_1=1, l_2=0.5, what is \det J?
\det J=l_1l_2\sin\theta_2=0.5\times1=0.5\ \text{m}^2. The arm is far from singular.
2. A tip force of (0, 10) N is applied with the arm at 30° and 45°. Find the torques.
\boldsymbol\tau=J^{\top}(0,10)^{\top}=(J_{21}\cdot10,\ J_{22}\cdot10)=(9.95,\ 1.29)\ \text{N·m}. The shoulder works harder because it is farther from the tip.
3. Why is the force mapping J^{\top} and not J^{-1}, even though joint speeds use the inverse?
Velocities and forces live in different spaces. Power balance requires \boldsymbol\tau^{\top}\dot{\mathbf q}=\mathbf F^{\top}\dot{\mathbf x}, and substituting \dot{\mathbf x}=J\dot{\mathbf q} gives the transpose. The inverse is only needed when you want joint speeds for a given tip speed, and it exists only away from singularities.

Takeaways

  • The Jacobian is the local linear map from joint velocities to tip velocities, and its columns are the tip velocities of each joint.
  • Forces map through its transpose, by power balance; this is why one matrix serves both velocity and force control.
  • Where \det J=0 the arm loses a direction of motion, and joint speeds diverge nearby.
Pipeline positionStage 5, Control. The Jacobian is recomputed at the control rate (often 500 Hz to 1 kHz) and is a small 6×6 or smaller matrix for most arms.
2.4

Manipulator dynamics

The questionA motor must hold a 2 kg link out horizontally, its centre of mass 0.25 m from the joint. What torque does the motor need? If the motor switches off, how fast does the link accelerate downward?

Intuition

Kinematics describes motion; dynamics explains it. A link resists being accelerated: the heavier it is and the farther its mass sits from the joint, the more torque it takes to speed it up. This resistance is called inertia. Gravity adds a torque that depends on how far the centre of mass sticks out horizontally. When several links move together, the torque on one joint also depends on how fast the others are moving, which is the Coriolis and centrifugal effect. A spinning ice skater pulls in her arms to spin faster; the same physics couples the joints of an arm.

The full set of equations is a balance of torques: torque from the motor equals inertia times acceleration, plus the velocity-coupling terms, plus gravity. It is compact in matrix form, and the same form works for any number of joints.

Build it up: one link, then two

Step 1: a single link about its joint. Newton's second law for rotation says torque equals moment of inertia times angular acceleration: \tau=I\ddot q. The inertia about the joint is the sum of the link's own inertia about its centre (I) and the mass times the square of the distance to the centre (mlc^2). This is the parallel-axis theorem.

Step 2: gravity. The weight mg acts at the centre of mass, a horizontal distance lc\cos q from the joint when the link is at angle q above horizontal. Its torque is mg\,lc\cos q.

Step 3: one link balance. Putting the two together, the motor torque must balance inertia and gravity: \tau=(mlc^2+I)\ddot q+mg\,lc\cos q.

Worked example: the one-link arm

Mass m=2.00 kg, centre of mass at lc=0.250 m from the joint, centroidal inertia I=0.050 kg·m², g=9.81 m/s².

Inertia about the joint. I_o=mlc^2+I=2.00\times0.0625+0.050=0.175\ \text{kg·m}^2.

Holding torque. Horizontal link, q=0, so \cos q=1 and with \ddot q=0: \tau=mg\,lc=2.00\times9.81\times0.250=4.905\ \text{N·m}. A motor rated below about 5 N·m cannot hold the arm out horizontally.

Release. The motor switches off (\tau=0) with the link horizontal and at rest. Then \ddot q=\dfrac{\tau-mg\,lc\cos q}{I_o}=\dfrac{0-4.905}{0.175}=-28.03\ \text{rad/s}^2. The angular acceleration is large and negative: the link drops quickly, but more slowly than a point mass at the same radius would (g\cos q/lc=39.2 rad/s²), because the link's own inertia resists the change.

The two-link arm has the same structure, but the torques are coupled and the inertia depends on the pose. Here is the complete equation of motion and one evaluated example.

The formal definition

M(\mathbf q)\,\ddot{\mathbf q}+C(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q})\,\dot{\mathbf q}+\mathbf g(\mathbf q)=\boldsymbol\tau

For the planar two-link arm, with link masses m_1,m_2, lengths l_1,l_2, centre-of-mass distances lc_1,lc_2 and centroidal inertias I_1,I_2, let

a=m_1lc_1^2+I_1+m_2(l_1^2+lc_2^2)+I_2,\qquad b=m_2l_1lc_2,\qquad c=m_2lc_2^2+I_2
M=\begin{pmatrix}a+2b\cos\theta_2 & c+b\cos\theta_2\\ c+b\cos\theta_2 & c\end{pmatrix},\qquad C=\begin{pmatrix}-b\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2 & -b\sin\theta_2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)\\ b\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1 & 0\end{pmatrix}

Worked example: the two-link arm at \theta_2=0

Parameters: m_1=2 kg, l_1=1 m, lc_1=0.5 m, I_1=0.10; m_2=1 kg, lc_2=0.4 m, I_2=0.02. (Link 2 has length l_2=0.8 m, which does not enter the equations here because only lc_2 is needed.)

Constants. a=2(0.25)+0.10+1(1+0.16)+0.02=1.78, b=1(1)(0.4)=0.40, c=1(0.16)+0.02=0.18.

Inertia matrix at \theta_2=0. \cos 0=1, so M=\begin{pmatrix}2.58 & 0.58\\ 0.58 & 0.18\end{pmatrix}. Its determinant is 2.58\times0.18-0.58^2=0.128>0, so it is positive definite, as it must be for a physical system.

Gravity at rest, horizontal arms (\theta_1=\theta_2=0). \mathbf g=\big((2\times0.5+1\times1.0)\times9.81+1\times0.4\times9.81,\ 0.4\times9.81\big)=(23.54,\ 3.92)\ \text{N·m}. Check the first entry: link 1's weight acts at 0.5 m and link 2's at 1.0 + 0.4 = 1.4 m from the shoulder, so the moment is (2\times0.5+1\times1.4)\times9.81=23.54 N·m. The motors must supply these torques to hold the arm still.

Holding torque is the gravity term. At rest, \dot{\mathbf q}=\ddot{\mathbf q}=0 and the equation reduces to \boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf g(\mathbf q).

Try this
  1. Set the one-link torque to 4.905 N·m and press play with the link held horizontal. It should stay still. Change the torque to 4.0 and watch it fall.
  2. Edit the torque expression to 4.905*cos(t). The motor now oscillates. Find the time at which the link is at its highest and watch the angle follow the torque.
  3. Change the centre-of-mass distance lc to 0.4 m. Note how the holding torque rises and the inertia rises faster.
  4. Set the viscous friction b to 0.4 N·m·s and release the link from horizontal. It swings past the bottom and back before settling. Now set b to 1.85 N·m·s and release again: the link returns to hanging down with no overshoot. The term -b\dot q is an extension of the formula above; 1.85 is the critical value 2I_o\omega_n with \omega_n=\sqrt{mg\,lc/I_o}=5.29 rad/s.
Common traps
  • Centroid versus joint. Use I_o=mlc^2+I when the link pivots about its end. Using the centroidal I alone underestimates the inertia and makes the controller too aggressive.
  • Gravity sign depends on the angle convention. If q is measured downward from the vertical, the gravity term is \sin and not \cos. Write the convention beside the equation.
  • M depends on the pose. A controller tuned for one configuration may be unstable in another; the inertia at the elbow-straight pose is larger than at the elbow-folded pose.
  • Torques, not forces. Motor specifications give torque at the output shaft, after the gear ratio. Mixing motor-side and link-side torques is a common source of factor-of-gear-ratio errors.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Model-based control (computed torque, impedance control, Module 6) inverts this equation to compute the torque that gives a desired acceleration. Learned models are often trained to predict M, C and \mathbf g from data when the parameters are unknown.
  • Physics simulators (MuJoCo, Pinocchio, Isaac-based simulators) evaluate this equation thousands of times per second to step the robot forward. Reinforcement learning inherits its speed and accuracy.
  • Learned policies that output torques are easier to train when the gravity term is removed or predicted. Many controllers add a learned residual on top of the known \mathbf g(\mathbf q).

Check yourself

1. The one-link arm with m=2 kg, lc=0.25 m and I=0.05 kg·m² is held vertically up (q=90^\circ). How much torque does it need?
\cos 90^\circ=0, so the gravity torque is zero. The arm is balanced at this pose and needs no holding torque, which is why a vertical arm is easy to hold and a horizontal one is hard.
2. The two-link inertia matrix at \theta_2=0 is \begin{pmatrix}2.58&0.58\\0.58&0.18\end{pmatrix}. At \theta_2=90^\circ, what is the off-diagonal entry?
c+b\cos 90^\circ=0.18+0.40\times0=0.18. The coupling term is smaller when the elbow is folded, so the two joints interact less.
3. Why is M(\mathbf q) positive definite?
Its quadratic form is the kinetic energy: \tfrac12\dot{\mathbf q}^{\top}M\dot{\mathbf q}>0 for any nonzero joint velocity, since a moving mass always has positive kinetic energy. Hence the matrix has a positive determinant (here 0.128) and can always be inverted.

Takeaways

  • Torque balances inertia, Coriolis and gravity: M\ddot q+C\dot q+g=\tau.
  • Inertia about the joint is mlc^2+I; the one-link worked example gives 4.905 N·m to hold horizontal and -28.0 rad/s² on release.
  • The inertia matrix depends on pose and is positive definite; a two-link example gives M=\begin{pmatrix}2.58&0.58\\0.58&0.18\end{pmatrix} at \theta_2=0.

2.5

Mobile robot kinematics & odometry

The questionA warehouse robot has two drive wheels and no GPS. Its wheel encoders say how far each wheel has turned. Where is the robot now, and which way is it facing? How wrong does the answer become if the wheel radius is off by a little?

Intuition

A differential-drive base has two powered wheels on one axle and a caster for balance. Turn both wheels at the same speed and the robot drives straight. Turn them at different speeds and it curves, pivoting around a point on the axle line. Turn them in opposite directions and it spins in place. Every motion is a combination of a forward speed and a turning rate, so the whole base can be described by two numbers.

Wheel encoders integrate those numbers over time to estimate the pose. This is odometry, dead reckoning from motion alone. It is fast and smooth, but its errors accumulate, because each estimate starts from the last one.

Build it up: wheels to body motion

Step 1: wheel speed. A wheel of radius r turning at \omega_R rad/s has a rim speed r\omega_R m/s.

Step 2: body speed. The robot's forward speed v is the average of the two rim speeds, v=\tfrac{r}{2}(\omega_R+\omega_L). Its turning rate \omega comes from the difference divided by the track width L (the distance between the wheel contact points): \omega=\tfrac{r}{L}(\omega_R-\omega_L).

Step 3: pose. The state is (x,y,\theta): position and heading. The body moves forward at speed v in its heading direction, and the heading changes at rate \omega.

Step 4: the constraint. The wheels cannot slide sideways. This gives a rule that the velocity has no component perpendicular to the heading: \dot x\sin\theta-\dot y\cos\theta=0. A constraint on velocity that cannot be integrated into a constraint on position is called non-holonomic. The robot can reach any pose, but not by moving sideways, so parking requires a sequence of manoeuvres.

Worked example: two seconds of driving

Wheel radius r=0.050 m, track width L=0.300 m, wheel speeds \omega_R=10 rad/s and \omega_L=8 rad/s. Start at (0,0) with heading 0.

Body motion. v=\tfrac{0.05}{2}(10+8)=0.450 m/s and \omega=\tfrac{0.05}{0.30}(10-8)=0.3333 rad/s.

Exact after 2 s. With constant v and \omega the robot moves on a circle of radius R=v/\omega=1.350 m. The heading changes by \Delta\theta=\omega T=0.6667 rad (38.20°). So x=R\sin\Delta\theta=0.8348 m and y=R(1-\cos\Delta\theta)=0.2891 m.

Approximate, 1-second steps. Treat each second as straight-line motion in the current heading. Step 1: (0.4500,\ 0), heading 0.3333 rad. Step 2: add 0.45\cos(0.3333)=0.4252 to x and 0.45\sin(0.3333)=0.1472 to y. Result (0.8752,\ 0.1472) m. The heading is the same, 0.6667 rad.

Error of the approximation. Compared with the exact (0.8348,\ 0.2891), the coarse steps are off by 0.040 m in x and by 0.142 m in y. Halving the step size reduces the error, which is the point of the next lesson on integration.

The formal definition

\dot x=v\cos\theta,\qquad \dot y=v\sin\theta,\qquad \dot\theta=\omega,\qquad v=\frac{r}{2}(\omega_R+\omega_L),\qquad \omega=\frac{r}{L}(\omega_R-\omega_L)
\text{non-holonomic constraint:}\quad \dot x\sin\theta-\dot y\cos\theta=0
(x,y,\theta)pose: position (m) and heading (rad) in the world frame
v,\ \omegaforward speed (m/s) and turning rate (rad/s), in the body frame
\omega_R,\ \omega_Lright and left wheel angular speeds (rad/s)

Ltrack width, the distance between the two wheel contact points (m)

Odometry. The encoders give the wheel angles, so the wheel speeds are known from differences over the sample time. The pose is then updated by integrating the equations above. Integration error and wheel slip both accumulate, so odometry is always combined with other sensors (Module 3).

Try this
  1. Set both wheel speeds to 10 rad/s. The robot drives straight. Read the distance travelled: it should be r\omega T after time T.
  2. Set the right wheel to 10 and the left to 8. Watch the curve. Compare the readout with the exact pose in the worked example after 2 s.
  3. Set the right wheel to 10 and the left to -10. The robot spins in place. Its position does not change.
  4. Increase the odometry step (the coarse dead-reckoning curve). Watch the error between the two trails grow. Then add a 2 % wheel-radius error to the odometry only, and see how it drifts.
Common traps
  • Track width is contact to contact. A wide or squashed tyre changes the effective contact separation, so the value on the datasheet can be wrong. Calibrate it by driving in a measured circle and comparing the heading change with the encoder counts.
  • Odometry drifts. A 1 % error in radius gives roughly a 1 % error in distance, and a small heading error sends the estimate sideways by an amount that grows with the distance travelled. Expect drift; do not treat odometry as ground truth.
  • Wheel slip. Encoders count rotation, not motion. On wet floors or hard turns the wheels slip and the odometry thinks the robot went farther.
  • Non-holonomic does not mean stuck. A car cannot drive sideways, but it can park in a bay by reversing and turning. The constraint shapes the path, not the reachable set.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Warehouse and delivery robots run odometry at 50 to 200 Hz and fuse it with lidar or camera localisation (Module 3, extended Kalman filter).
  • Learned navigation policies and simulators use this unicycle model, because it is accurate for wheeled bases and cheap to evaluate.
  • Odometry error is a major source of "sim-to-real" gaps in mobile robots; a simulator with perfect odometry trains policies that fail in the warehouse.

Check yourself

1. With r=0.05 m and L=0.30 m, the right wheel turns at 6 rad/s and the left at 6 rad/s. What is the robot's motion?
\omega=0 and v=0.05\times6=0.30 m/s. The robot drives straight at 0.30 m/s.
2. The right wheel is at 4 rad/s and the left wheel at 4 rad/s, but the radius is 1 % larger than the odometry assumes. Which way does the estimate err, and by how much?
The odometry assumes r, but the true radius is 1.01r. The true speed is 1 % higher, so the estimate under-counts distance by about 1 %. For a 10 m straight run, the error is about 0.1 m.
3. Why can a differential-drive robot reach any pose, even though its velocity is constrained?
The constraint is on the velocity, not the position. By combining forward motion with turning (or turning in place) the robot can reach any (x,y,\theta), with manoeuvres such as parallel parking. The constraint is non-holonomic: it limits the path, not the set of reachable poses.

Takeaways

  • A differential drive has a forward speed v=\tfrac r2(\omega_R+\omega_L) and a turning rate \omega=\tfrac rL(\omega_R-\omega_L).
  • Odometry integrates these speeds into a pose; it is smooth and fast, but its errors accumulate.
  • The wheels cannot slide sideways (non-holonomic), so motion is a sequence of forward and turning moves.

2.6

Simulating robots

The questionYou want to simulate a 1 kg mass on a spring (stiffness 100 N/m) for 10 seconds, to test a controller. Computers only take small steps in time. How big must the step be? If the step is too big, the simulated energy will grow for no physical reason, so which integration method should you use, and how big a step does it allow?

Intuition

A simulator is a clock and a rule. At each tick it asks, "given where everything is and how fast it moves, how fast is each thing accelerating?" Then it moves everything forward by a small time step h. The rule is the physics from Lesson 2.4. The clock is discrete, so the simulator is always an approximation. The art is keeping the approximation honest.

Think of walking a curved road by taking steps in straight lines. Each step cuts a corner. Short steps cut small corners; long steps cut big ones and you drift off the road. A good integrator is one that drifts in a controlled way, staying close to the road even over long walks.

Build it up: the spring, step by step

Step 1: the state and the rule. A spring with stiffness k and mass m has position x and velocity v. Newton's law gives \dot x=v and \dot v=-\omega^2 x with \omega=\sqrt{k/m}. For k=100 N/m and m=1 kg, \omega=10 rad/s, so the true motion is a cosine with a period of 2\pi/10=0.628 s.

Step 2: explicit Euler. The simplest step uses the current rates: x_{n+1}=x_n+hv_n, v_{n+1}=v_n-h\omega^2x_n. It looks right. Tested on a spring, it adds a little energy every step, so the motion grows.

Step 3: semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler. Change one line: update the velocity first, then use the new velocity to move the position. v_{n+1}=v_n-h\omega^2x_n, then x_{n+1}=x_n+hv_{n+1}. The energy now wobbles within a fixed band instead of growing.

Worked example: how much energy the explicit step adds

Start at x_0=1 m, v_0=0, so the energy is E_0=\tfrac12kx_0^2=50 J.

Growth per step. Explicit Euler multiplies the energy by 1+\omega^2h^2 at each step (a short calculation from the two update rules). With h=0.01 s, \omega^2h^2=100\times0.0001=0.01, so the factor is 1.01.

After 10 s. That is n=10/0.01=1000 steps. The energy becomes E=E_0(1.01)^{1000}=50\times20\,959=1.05\times10^6 J, about 21 000 times the start. The simulated spring is violently gaining energy.

Semi-implicit at the same step. The simulated energy stays near 50 J, wobbling between 0.95 and 1.05 times E_0. Running the same 10 s with h=0.05 s the wobble is wider, between 0.80 and 1.33 times E_0, but it does not grow: that is the bounded band, not thousands.

Stability limit. The semi-implicit method is stable when h<2/\omega. Here 2/\omega=0.2 s, so h=0.05 s is safe and h=0.25 s would blow up.

Contact and friction

Robots touch things, and contact is where simulations most often fail. A simple model of dry friction, Coulomb friction, says the friction force is at most \mu N, where \mu is the friction coefficient and N the normal force pressing the surfaces together. If the applied force is smaller than that limit, the surfaces stick and friction matches the applied force. If it is larger, the object slides and friction is exactly \mu N.

Worked example: a block on a table

Mass 1 kg, normal force N=10 N, friction coefficient \mu=0.5. The friction limit is \mu N=5.0 N.

Push with F=4 N: 4<5, so the block sticks and the friction is 4 N. Net force is zero, and the block stays still.

Push with F=6 N: 6>5, so the block slides and friction is 5 N. Net force is 6-5=1 N, and the acceleration is 1/1=1 m/s².

Friction is a discontinuous rule (stuck or slipping), which means a simulator with a large step can make the block chatter between the two states. Stiff contacts (a steel gripper on a steel part) need even smaller steps. A contact stiffness of 10^5 N/m on a 1 kg object gives \omega=\sqrt{10^5}=316 rad/s, so the semi-implicit step must satisfy h<2/316=6.3\times10^{-3} s.

The formal definition

\dot{\mathbf s}=f(\mathbf s,t)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \mathbf s_{n+1}=\mathbf s_n+h\,f(\mathbf s_n,t_n)\quad\text{(explicit Euler)}
\text{semi-implicit Euler (spring):}\quad v_{n+1}=v_n-h\omega^2x_n,\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n+h\,v_{n+1}
\text{Coulomb friction:}\quad |F_f|\le\mu N\quad\text{(sticking)},\qquad F_f=-\mu N\,\operatorname{sign}(\dot x)\quad\text{(sliding)}
\mathbf s=(x,v)state: position and velocity (m, m/s)
htime step (s); it must be smaller than the fastest time scale
\omega=\sqrt{k/m}natural angular frequency of the spring (rad/s)
E=\tfrac12mv^2+\tfrac12kx^2total mechanical energy (J); conserved in the true motion

Fidelity versus speed. Smaller steps and more accurate contact models cost more compute. Robot learning often runs thousands of simulators in parallel, so teams choose the coarsest model that still reproduces the behaviour they care about, and then close the gap with real data.

  2. Switch to semi-implicit with the same step. The energy wobbles inside a band. Watch the phase plot: the orbit stays closed.
  3. Set the step to 0.25 s with semi-implicit. Watch it blow up, and compare with the stability limit 2/\omega=0.2 s.

Common traps
  • Energy drift is a bug, not a feature. A simulation that gains energy steadily is wrong, even if its picture looks plausible.
  • A smaller step is not always enough. Explicit Euler diverges for any step on an oscillator. Changing the method matters more than shrinking the step.
  • Friction is a switch. Friction with a stiff rule and a large step produces chatter. Use a smoothed approximation or a small step near contact.
  • Fixed time step versus real time. If the simulator cannot finish a step in real time, a controller tested in simulation will see different delays from the real robot. Log the step timing.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Reinforcement learning for locomotion trains policies in simulators (MuJoCo and similar engines) that run millions of steps. The choice of integrator and time step is part of the environment definition.
  • Sim-to-real transfer (Module 7) depends on matching friction, contact and latency. A policy that works only at one friction value has learned the simulator, not the robot.
  • Learned dynamics models, which predict the next state from the current state and action, are trained on data produced by simulators or logs, and inherit their integration errors.

Check yourself

1. Explicit Euler, \omega=10 rad/s, step h=0.02 s. How much does the energy grow per step?
The factor is 1+\omega^2h^2=1+100\times0.0004=1.04, so 4 % per step. In 100 steps (2 s) that is about 1.04^{100}=50.5 times the energy.
2. A block of mass 2 kg is pressed with N=20 N and \mu=0.3. An applied force of 5 N acts. Does it slide, and what is the friction force?
The limit is \mu N=0.3\times20=6 N. Since 5<6, the block sticks and the friction is 5 N. The net force is zero.
3. A contact spring of stiffness 10^4 N/m acts on a 0.5 kg part. What is the largest stable step for semi-implicit Euler?
\omega=\sqrt{10^4/0.5}=141.4 rad/s, so h<2/141.4=0.0141 s. A step of 10 ms is stable; 20 ms is not.

Takeaways

  • A simulator advances the state by small steps; explicit Euler adds energy to an oscillator, and semi-implicit Euler keeps it bounded.
  • Stability needs h well below 2/\omega, the period of the fastest dynamic, including stiff contacts.
  • Coulomb friction is a stick-or-slip rule; a stiff contact in a simulator forces small steps, so fidelity and speed trade off directly.
