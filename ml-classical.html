3.1

k-nearest neighbours

The questionA new customer walks in. You have no formula for "who buys", but you have 10 000 past customers with known outcomes. What is the laziest sensible prediction you can make? And when does "lazy" stop working?

Intuition

"Tell me who your friends are and I will tell you who you are." To classify a new point, find the k most similar points you already know and let them vote. There is no training step at all: the "model" is simply the stored dataset. That is why k-NN is called a lazy learner (or instance-based): all the work is postponed until a question arrives.

Two choices define it. How do we measure "similar"? (a distance) and how many neighbours do we ask? (k). With k=1 the boundary hugs every training point, even noisy ones, so it is jagged and has low bias but high variance. With a huge k the vote is nearly the global majority, so the boundary is smooth and may be too simple: high bias, low variance. This is the bias–variance trade-off in its cleanest form.

Worked example — the vote

k = 1: nearest is P6 (R) → predict R.

k = 3: P6 (R), P4 (R), P3 (B) → R gets 2 votes, B 1 → R with probability 2/3.

k = 5: add P5 (R) and P2 (B) → R 3, B 2 → R, probability 3/5.

k = 6 (every point): 3 vs 3 — a tie! Using all the data erased the local information, which is why an odd k (or a tie-break rule) is used.

Distance-weighted vote (weight 1/d), k = 3: R weight = 1/0.5 + 1/1.414 = 2.707; B weight = 1/2.0 = 0.5 → R share = 2.707/3.207 = 0.844. Close neighbours count more.

Scaling matters. Suppose person q has (age 30, income 50 000), person A is (31, 52 000) and person B is (60, 50 500). Raw distances: d(q,A)=2000.0, d(q,B)=500.9 — B looks "nearer" although he is 30 years older, because income's huge units swamp age. Standardise (age std ≈ 10, income std ≈ 10 000): A becomes (0.1, 0.2) away → 0.224; B (3.0, 0.05) → 3.00. Now A is nearest, as common sense says. Distance-based models need scaled features (see Module 1).

Common traps
  • Forgetting to scale (shown above) and fitting the scaler on test data (leakage).
  • Choosing k on the training set: at k = 1 training accuracy is always 100% (each point is its own neighbour). Choose k by validation.
  • Using k-NN on hundreds of raw features: distances concentrate; reduce dimensions first (lesson 3.6).
Where it shows up in AI
  • Retrieval and recommendation: "find the nearest item embeddings" is k-NN, served by approximate indexes (FAISS, HNSW) in vector databases and RAG systems.
  • Imputation (KNNImputer), anomaly scores (distance to the k-th neighbour) and few-shot / prototype classifiers on top of deep embeddings.
  • Use it when data is small, boundaries are irregular and a quick, explainable baseline is wanted; avoid it for huge, high-dimensional, latency-critical problems.

Check yourself

1. In the table above, what does k = 4 predict, and what is the probability?
Neighbours: P6 (R), P4 (R), P3 (B), P5 (R) → R 3, B 1 → predict R with probability 3/4.
2. Why is training accuracy 100% at k = 1 (with no duplicate points of different labels), and why is that useless?
Each training point is its own nearest neighbour (distance 0), so it votes for itself. It says nothing about new data; only validation or test accuracy does.
3. Brute-force k-NN on n = 1 000 000 points with d = 100: roughly how many multiply-adds per query?
About n·d = 10⁸ (times a small constant). That is why indexes or approximate search are used at scale.

Takeaways

  • k-NN stores the data and votes among the k closest points; no training, O(n·d) inference.
  • Small k → jagged, high variance; large k → smooth, high bias.
  • Always scale features; beware high dimensions.
3.2

Naive Bayes

The questionAn email contains the words "win" and "now". Given a pile of emails already labelled spam or ham, how likely is this one to be spam — and how can we compute that with almost no training time and a few counts?

Intuition

Spam tends to use certain words; ham uses others. Ask: if this were spam, how surprising would these words be? If it were ham, how surprising? Whichever class finds the words less surprising (after weighting by how common the class is) wins. That is Bayes' rule (probability module). The "naive" part: to avoid modelling every word combination we pretend that, given the class, words are independent of each other. This is false ("free" and "money" go together) but the resulting classifier is often surprisingly good because we only need the ranking of classes, not exact probabilities.

Build it up: spam filter by hand

Training emails (vocabulary of V = 9 distinct words):

  • Spam: "win money now", "win prize money", "free money" → 8 word tokens
  • Ham: "meeting now", "lunch meeting", "project update now" → 7 word tokens

count in spam231110000
count in ham002002111

Priors: 3 of 6 emails are spam → P(\text{spam})=P(\text{ham})=0.5. Without smoothing, P(w\mid c)=\text{count}(w,c)/N_c. The query is "win now".

Worked example

No smoothing. P(\text{win}\mid\text{ham})=0/7=0, so the whole ham product is 0 and spam wins with probability 100% — a single unseen (word, class) pair vetoes a class. Worse, for "free meeting" both classes get 0 and the result is 0/0.

Laplace smoothing (α = 1): add 1 to every count and V = 9 to every denominator: P(w\mid c)=\frac{\text{count}+1}{N_c+V}. Denominators: spam 8+9 = 17, ham 7+9 = 16.

Spam: 0.5\cdot\frac{2+1}{17}\cdot\frac{1+1}{17}=0.5\cdot\frac{3}{17}\cdot\frac{2}{17}=0.01038.

Ham: 0.5\cdot\frac{0+1}{16}\cdot\frac{2+1}{16}=0.5\cdot\frac1{16}\cdot\frac3{16}=0.00586.

Normalise: P(\text{spam}\mid\text{win now})=\frac{0.01038}{0.01038+0.00586}=0.639 → spam, but with honest uncertainty.

"free meeting" with smoothing: spam 0.5\cdot\frac{2}{17}\cdot\frac1{17}=0.00346, ham 0.5\cdot\frac1{16}\cdot\frac3{16}=0.00586 → P(spam) = 0.371 → ham.

Underflow: multiplying hundreds of small probabilities underflows, so implementations add log-probabilities instead and compare the sums.

Common traps
  • Reading the output as a calibrated probability. Naive Bayes is a good ranker but its probabilities are extreme; calibrate before using thresholds (Module 5).
  • Forgetting that α is added to every vocabulary word, so the denominator grows by V, not 1.
  • Multiplying probabilities instead of summing logs for long documents.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Classic spam filters, sentiment baselines, document routing, language identification.
  • The generative-classifier idea (model P(x\mid c), then flip with Bayes) reappears in Gaussian mixtures, LDA/QDA, and the "generative vs discriminative" debate.
  • Choose it for fast baselines on text or high-dimensional sparse data; avoid it when feature interactions decide the label.

Check yourself

1. With α = 1, compute P(prize | spam) and P(prize | ham).
Spam: (1+1)/(8+9) = 2/17 = 0.118. Ham: (0+1)/(7+9) = 1/16 = 0.0625.
2. Why do the smoothed probabilities of all 9 words in a class still sum to 1?
Sum of (count+1) over V words = N_c + V, divided by N_c + V gives 1.
3. "free" and "money" are strongly correlated in spam. What does naive Bayes do with them?
It counts both as independent evidence, so it double-counts, making the spam score too extreme. The ranking is often still right.

Takeaways

  • Naive Bayes = prior × product of per-feature likelihoods, using Bayes' rule and conditional independence.
  • Laplace smoothing prevents zero-count vetoes.
  • Training is one counting pass; inference is a sum of logs — ideal text baseline.
3.3

Decision trees

The questionA bank officer wants a model they can read aloud: "if hours studied ≤ 2.5 then fail, else …". How do we learn such a list of questions from data, and which question should come first?

Intuition

Play twenty questions. A good first question splits the possibilities into groups that are as pure as possible — mostly one answer in each. A tree does exactly this: it picks one feature and one threshold, splits the data in two, then repeats the game on each half (recursive partitioning) until the groups are pure or a stopping rule fires. Geometrically each split is an axis-parallel line that cuts the plane; the final leaves are rectangles, each predicting its majority class.

"Purity" needs a number. Two standard impurity measures for a node with class fractions p_c: Gini G=1-\sum_c p_c^2 and entropy H=-\sum_c p_c\log_2 p_c. Both are 0 for a pure node and maximal for a 50/50 node (Gini 0.5, entropy 1 bit for two classes). The gain of a split is the parent impurity minus the size-weighted impurity of the children. The tree greedily picks the split with the largest gain.

Worked example — reading the table

Row "x₁ ≤ 2.5": left holds students 1, 2, 8 (all fail, G = 0); right holds students 3–7 (1 fail, 4 pass): G=1-(0.2^2+0.8^2)=0.32. Weighted: \tfrac38\cdot0+\tfrac58\cdot0.32=0.20. Gain = 0.5 − 0.2 = 0.30. Entropy: right H=-(0.2\log_20.2+0.8\log_20.8)=0.722, weighted \tfrac58\cdot0.722=0.451, information gain 1 − 0.451 = 0.549.

Best split: x₁ ≤ 2.5 and x₁ ≤ 3.5 tie (the tie is broken by taking the first). Both criteria agree on the ranking here; they usually do. Sleep alone is almost useless (best gain 0.07).

Next level, using the right child (rows 3–7, G = 0.32): the best split is x₁ ≤ 3.5, which puts rows 3 and 7 (one fail, one pass, G = 0.5) on the left and rows 4–6 (all pass) on the right: gain = 0.32 − 0.4·0.5 = 0.12. Rows 3 and 7 share x₁ = 3 and can only be separated by sleep (x₂ ≤ 4) — after which every leaf is pure.

Stopping and pruning. Grown to purity, a tree memorises noise (a leaf for every point: variance explodes). Control it with max_depth, min_samples_leaf, a minimum gain, or prune a full tree back (cost-complexity pruning minimises error + λ·leaves). Regression trees use variance (MSE) as the impurity and predict the mean of the leaf.

Where it shows up in AI
  • Random forests and gradient boosting (Module 4) are many trees: the strongest default on tabular data (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost).

Check yourself

1. A node has 3 fail and 1 pass. Compute its Gini and entropy.
p = 0.75/0.25. Gini = 1 − 0.5625 − 0.0625 = 0.375. Entropy = −(0.75·log₂0.75 + 0.25·log₂0.25) = 0.811 bits.
2. Why can the gain never be negative with Gini or entropy?
Both are concave, so the weighted average of the children's impurity is ≤ the parent's impurity. A split can have zero gain (as x₂ ≤ 2.5 above) but not negative.
3. Why are no scaling or standardisation steps needed for trees?
Splits compare one feature to a threshold; any monotone rescaling moves the threshold but leaves the ordering, and thus the partitions, unchanged.

Takeaways

  • A tree greedily chooses the split with the largest impurity decrease, recursively.
  • Gini and entropy usually agree; regression trees use variance.
  • Unrestricted trees overfit: limit depth/leaf size or prune. Trees are the building block of ensembles.
3.4

Support vector machines

The questionMany lines separate two classes perfectly. Which one should we trust on new data? Intuitively, the one that stays as far as possible from both classes — but how do we turn "as far as possible" into something we can optimise?

Intuition

Imagine a road between two villages. A road squeezed against one village's houses is risky; the best road is the widest one that still separates them. The road's width is the margin, and only the houses on the road's edge — the support vectors — decide where it goes; remove any other house and nothing changes. The classifier is the road's centre line w\cdot x+b=0; the edges are w\cdot x+b=\pm1.

Real data overlap, so we allow some points inside or across the road and pay a price: the soft margin with a dial C (large C = punish violations hard, narrow road; small C = tolerate violations, wide road). When no straight line works, the kernel trick lets the machine act as if the data lived in a much higher-dimensional space where a line does work (the RBF kernel makes bumpy, local boundaries).

Build it up: margin and hinge loss by hand

Four points: negatives (0,0) and (1,1); positives (2,2) and (4,1). Labels y=\mp1.

Worked example

The widest road. The closest opposite pair is (1,1) and (2,2), at distance √2 = 1.414. The best separator is their perpendicular bisector x_1+x_2=3, i.e. w=(1,1),\ b=-3. Check the edges: (1,1) → 1+1−3 = −1, (2,2) → 4−3 = +1 ✓; the others are outside: (0,0) → −3, (4,1) → +2.

Margin width = 2/\lVert w\rVert=2/\sqrt2=1.414 ✓ (equals the gap between the two support vectors). Support vectors: (1,1) and (2,2) only.

Hinge loss per point \max(0,\,1-y(w\cdot x+b)): scores y(w·x+b) = 1, 3, 1, 2 for (1,1),(0,0),(2,2),(4,1) → hinge = 0, 0, 0, 0. Every point is on or outside its correct edge.

  • Text classification with sparse TF-IDF features (linear SVMs are still strong), bioinformatics, small-data image classifiers on top of CNN features.

Check yourself

1. For w = (3, 4), what is the margin width 2/‖w‖?
‖w‖ = 5, so the width is 0.4.
2. Which training points can be removed without changing a hard-margin SVM?
All points that are not support vectors (their α = 0), e.g. (0,0) and (4,1) in the example.
3. What does lowering C do, and which side of the bias–variance trade-off does it move you toward?

Takeaways

  • SVM maximises the margin 2/‖w‖; only support vectors matter.
  • Soft margin = hinge loss + C; kernels bend the boundary (RBF with γ).
  • Scale features; kernel SVMs do not scale to huge n.
3.5

Clustering

The questionYou have 100 000 customers and no labels. Can the data itself suggest natural groups — "bargain hunters", "weekend big spenders" — and how would you know if the groups are any good?

Intuition

Clustering is unsupervised: no right answers, only structure. k-means works like organising a fair: drop k flags (centroids) on the map, let everyone walk to the nearest flag (assign), then move each flag to the centre of its crowd (update), and repeat until nobody switches. Each full round can only lower the total squared distance to the flags (the inertia), so it always settles — but possibly in a poor spot, which is why we restart from several random starts.

Hierarchical clustering starts with every point alone and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters, producing a tree (dendrogram) you can cut at any height. DBSCAN asks a different question: where is the data dense? Points with at least minPts neighbours within radius ε are "core"; connected core points form a cluster; the rest is noise. It finds arbitrary shapes and needs no k. Gaussian mixtures model each cluster as a bell and use EM (soft assignments ↔ re-estimate means/covariances), giving probabilities instead of hard labels.

Worked example

① Assign (table above). Inertia with the starting flags: cluster 1 → 0² + 1² + 2² = 5; cluster 2 → 2.24² + 0 + 1.41² = 5 + 2 = 7; total 12.

② Update: c_1=\big(\tfrac{1+2+1}3,\tfrac{1+1+3}3\big)=(1.333,1.667), c_2=\big(\tfrac{6+7+6}3,\tfrac{5+7+6}3\big)=(6.333,6). Inertia with the new flags: 6.0 (it fell from 12).

③ Re-assign: nobody changes cluster → converged after one iteration. With a bad start (both flags inside one blob) the algorithm can settle with one flag splitting a blob and another flag covering two — a local minimum.

Choosing k. Plot inertia vs k (the elbow: it always falls, look for where it flattens) or use the silhouette: for a point, a = mean distance to its own cluster, b = mean distance to the nearest other cluster, s=\frac{b-a}{\max(a,b)}. For A=(1,1): a = (1.00+2.00)/2 = 1.5, b = (6.40+8.49+7.07)/3 = 7.32, so s=0.795 (near 1 = well clustered, near 0 = on a border, negative = probably wrong cluster).

Common traps
  • Clusters are not "ground truth": a different feature scaling gives different clusters. Validate them against business meaning.
  • k-means on non-spherical or very unequal groups, and on unscaled features.
  • Picking k from the elbow alone — often ambiguous; combine with silhouette and usefulness.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Customer segmentation, image colour quantisation, vector quantisation / product quantisation inside vector search, codebooks in audio and image tokenisers.
  • Initialising or pre-grouping data for other models; anomaly detection via DBSCAN noise.
  • Use k-means as a fast default for roundish groups; DBSCAN for shapes and noise; GMM for overlapping, soft clusters.

Check yourself

1. Why can k-means never increase its inertia in a full iteration?
Assignment moves each point to its nearest centroid (not worse); the update moves each centroid to the mean of its points, which minimises the sum of squared distances for that fixed assignment. Both steps are non-increasing; as the number of assignments is finite, it converges.
2. In the worked example, compute the silhouette of F = (6,6).
Own cluster D=(6,5), E=(7,7): distances 1.000 and 1.414 → a = 1.207. Other cluster A, B, C: 7.07, 6.40, 5.83 → b = 6.43. s = (6.43 − 1.207)/6.43 = 0.81.
3. When would you pick DBSCAN over k-means?
When clusters are non-convex (rings, moons), you do not know k, or you want outliers labelled as noise — and density is roughly uniform.

Takeaways

  • k-means alternates assign and update; inertia only falls; restart to avoid poor local minima.
  • Choose k by elbow and silhouette; scale features.
  • DBSCAN = density, hierarchical = a tree of merges, GMM = soft assignments.
3.6

Dimensionality reduction

The questionYour table has 300 correlated columns. You want to plot it on a screen, speed up a model, or compress it — which few directions keep most of the information?

Intuition

Photograph a flat book from an angle: you lose little, because the book is nearly 2-D inside 3-D. Principal component analysis (PCA) finds the viewing direction that keeps the most spread. The first principal component is the direction along which the data vary the most; the second is the best direction at right angles to it; and so on. Projecting onto the first few components keeps the biggest variations and discards the rest, which is often noise. (The linear algebra — eigenvectors of the covariance, or SVD — is in the linear algebra module.)

t-SNE and UMAP are non-linear: they try to place points in 2-D so that neighbours stay neighbours. They produce beautiful cluster pictures, but the distances between clusters, cluster sizes and even the number of clusters can be artefacts of the settings (perplexity, n_neighbours). Use them for exploration, never as proof.

Build it up: variance explained from a 2×2 covariance

Suppose two centred features have covariance matrix

\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}
Worked example

Eigenvalues solve \det(\Sigma-\lambda I)=\lambda^2-7\lambda+6=0 (trace 7, determinant 6) → \lambda_1=6,\ \lambda_2=1.

Eigenvector for 6: (5-6)x+2y=0\Rightarrow y=x/2 → v_1=(2,1)/\sqrt5. Then v_2=(-1,2)/\sqrt5.

Variance explained: PC1 = 6/(6+1) = 85.7%, PC2 = 14.3%. Total variance 7 = trace ✓. Keeping one component loses 14.3% of the variance.

Projecting a point. Take the centred point x=(3,2). Score z_1=x\cdot v_1=(6+2)/\sqrt5=3.578. Reconstruction z_1v_1=(3.2,1.6); residual (-0.2,0.4). Check: \lVert x\rVert^2=13=3.578^2+\lVert\text{residual}\rVert^2=12.8+0.2 ✓ — PCA is a rotation, and the discarded part is small because the point lies near PC1.

Scree plot and choosing k. Plot eigenvalues in order and keep enough components to reach, say, 90–95% cumulative variance, or stop at the "elbow". Always standardise first when features have different units, or the biggest-unit column hijacks PC1.

When to reduce: visualisation, removing multicollinearity, denoising, speeding up k-NN/SVM, compression. When not to: tree models rarely need it, interpretability matters (components mix features), or the signal lives in low-variance directions (PCA keeps variance, not label information — see LDA for supervised reduction).

The formal definition

\Sigma=\tfrac1{n-1}X_c^{\top}X_c,\quad \Sigma v_j=\lambda_jv_j,\quad z=V_k^{\top}(x-\mu),\quad \text{explained}_j=\frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_i\lambda_i}

Common traps
  • Reading t-SNE/UMAP cluster distances or sizes as real, or running them once with default settings.
  • Fitting PCA on the full dataset before splitting (leakage), or forgetting to apply the same transform to test data.
  • Assuming the top-variance directions are the predictive ones.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Eigenfaces, genomics, whitening before other methods, visualising embeddings from neural networks (t-SNE/UMAP plots of word or image embeddings).
  • Compressing vectors for search (PCA then product quantisation); autoencoders are the non-linear cousin (Module 7).
  • Reduce when dimensions are redundant and a downstream distance-based model suffers; skip when a tree ensemble handles the raw features well.

Check yourself

1. Covariance [[4, 0],[0, 1]]: what are the components and variance explained?
Already diagonal: PC1 = x-axis (λ = 4, 80%), PC2 = y-axis (λ = 1, 20%).
2. Why standardise before PCA when one feature is in metres and another in millimetres?
The mm feature has a variance 10⁶ times larger and would dominate PC1 purely because of units. Standardising puts features on comparable footing.
3. A t-SNE plot shows two clusters far apart and one tiny. What can you conclude?
Only that there is local neighbourhood structure. The gap and sizes may be artefacts of perplexity; verify with other settings and with the original data.

Takeaways

  • PCA keeps the directions of largest variance; explained variance = λ/Σλ.
  • Centre and usually scale; fit on training data only.
  • t-SNE/UMAP are for looking, not for measuring.
3.7

Anomaly detection

The questionOne transaction in 100 000 is fraud, and tomorrow's fraud looks different from yesterday's. You cannot train a normal classifier on examples you do not have. How do you flag "unusual" — and how can you evaluate it without labels?

Intuition

Anomalies are few and different. Four families of ideas, from simple to strong:

  • Statistical: far from the typical value — a z-score |x-\mu|/\sigma above 3, or outside the box-plot fences.
  • Distance / density: far from its k nearest neighbours, or in a much sparser region than they are (k-NN distance, local outlier factor).
  • Isolation: an outlier is easy to isolate. Cut the data with random lines; a lone point is separated after very few cuts, a point in a crowd needs many. Isolation forest averages the number of cuts (path length) over many random trees.
  • Boundary / reconstruction: learn what "normal" looks like (one-class SVM draws a boundary around the normal data; an autoencoder or PCA reconstructs normal data well but reconstructs anomalies badly → large reconstruction error).

Build it up: two hand computations

Eight sensor readings: 1, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 20.

Worked example

Statistical. Mean = 5.06, standard deviation (population) = 5.74. The z-score of 20 is (20 − 5.06)/5.74 = 2.60. Notice the outlier itself inflated σ and the mean: this is masking. Robust versions use the median and MAD.

Isolation path length. Pick a random cut between the smallest and largest value of the current group, keep the side that contains the point, and count cuts until it is alone. For the value 20 the range is 1–20; almost any cut separates it at once (a cut landing in 4.5–20, i.e. a 82% chance, isolates it in one cut). For the value 3 in the crowd, many cuts are needed. Averaging 20 000 random runs of this procedure (computed, not a hand estimate): value 20 → 1.20 cuts, value 3 → 4.74 cuts, value 1 → 2.91 cuts (it sits at the edge).

Score. Normalise by the average path length of an unsuccessful binary-tree search: c(n)=2H(n-1)-\tfrac{2(n-1)}n with H(m)\approx\ln m+0.5772 the harmonic number; exactly for n = 8: c(8)=2\cdot2.593-1.75=3.436. Then s=2^{-E[h]/c(n)}: for 20 → 2^(−1.20/3.436) = 0.785; for 3 → 2^(−4.74/3.436) = 0.384. Scores near 1 are anomalies; well below 0.5 are clearly normal.

Evaluating without labels. Hold out a small labelled audit set if you can and use precision at the top-N alerts, PR-AUC (not accuracy!), or the analyst's alert-review rate. Otherwise inject synthetic anomalies, check stability across random seeds, and monitor the alert rate over time. Choosing the threshold is a cost decision: alerts a human can review per day.

Common traps
  • Evaluating with accuracy: if 0.1% are anomalies, "never alert" scores 99.9%.
  • Training on data that already contains many anomalies, or assuming "normal" never drifts — retrain and monitor.
  • Treating every statistical outlier as an error: rare is not the same as wrong.
Where it shows up in AI
  • Fraud and intrusion detection, predictive maintenance on sensor streams, ML monitoring (data drift alarms, Module 9), manufacturing defect screening with autoencoders.
  • Isolation forest is a default first choice for tabular data; reconstruction error for images and sequences.
  • Prefer supervised classifiers when you have enough labelled anomalies; use anomaly detection when you do not.

Check yourself

1. Readings have mean 50 and σ = 5. What z-score does 68 have, and is it a 3-sigma outlier?
z = (68 − 50)/5 = 3.6 → yes, beyond 3σ.
2. Why does a lone outlier get a short isolation path?
A random cut in the data's range is likely to fall in the empty gap between the outlier and the crowd, separating it immediately; points in dense regions need many cuts to be separated from neighbours.
3. Why is PR-AUC better than accuracy for evaluating an anomaly detector?
With extreme imbalance accuracy rewards doing nothing. PR-AUC focuses on how many alerts are real (precision) and how many anomalies are caught (recall).

Takeaways

  • Anomalies are few and different: score them by distance, density, isolation or reconstruction error.
  • Isolation forest: short average path length → anomaly; s = 2^(−E[h]/c(n)).
  • Without labels, evaluate by alert budgets, audits and injected anomalies; monitor over time.
